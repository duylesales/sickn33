# LaunchStudio vs. Hiring Your First Full-Time Engineer: A 2027 Budget Decision
Every founder with early traction eventually hits the same line item on their 2027 budget spreadsheet: "hire first engineer." It feels like the obvious next step — the product is gaining users, the founder is stretched thin, and a full-time technical hire seems like the natural way to buy back time and add engineering capacity. What that budget line rarely accounts for is the actual, fully loaded cost of that hire, the months it takes before they're contributing at full speed, and what a founder is really buying versus what they actually need at this specific stage. This article runs the real numbers on hiring a first full-time engineer against a fixed-scope engagement with LaunchStudio, so the 2027 decision gets made with the full cost on the table, not just the salary line.

## What "Hire an Engineer" Actually Costs in 2027

A junior-to-mid-level full-time engineer in most European tech hubs commands a base salary in the range of €55,000-€85,000 annually, and that base is only the starting point of the real cost. Add employer social contributions and payroll taxes (commonly 20-30% on top of base salary in much of the EU), benefits, equipment, software licenses, and — for a founder offering equity to compete for talent without matching a larger company's salary — a meaningful slice of the cap table that gets permanently diluted regardless of how the hire works out. A realistic fully loaded cost for a single early engineering hire in 2027 lands somewhere between €70,000 and €115,000 for the first year alone, before accounting for the time a non-technical or overextended founder has to spend on recruiting, interviewing, and onboarding — time that isn't going into the product or customers during that entire search.

And that number assumes the hire goes well. Recruiting a good early engineer typically takes six to twelve weeks of active search even in a strong market, followed by a notice period at their current job, followed by a ramp-up period — typically one to three months — before they're genuinely productive on a codebase they didn't build. Realistically, a founder who starts a search in January isn't getting meaningful output from a new hire until April or May, a four-to-five month gap during which the original problem (not enough engineering capacity) is still unsolved, while the clock on that engineer's salary and equity is already running from day one.

## What a Full-Time Engineer Is Actually Good At — and What They're Not

None of this is an argument against hiring engineers — at a certain stage, a full-time technical hire is exactly the right move, and founders who wait too long to make that hire often pay for it in slower iteration and founder burnout. The question isn't whether to ever hire; it's whether hiring is the right tool for the specific problem in front of you right now.

A full-time engineer is well-suited to ongoing product development: shipping new features, iterating based on user feedback, owning the codebase's long-term direction, and being embedded in daily product decisions in a way no outside partner can replicate. What a first full-time hire is generally not well-suited to, at least not immediately, is a narrowly scoped, time-boxed hardening problem — auditing an AI-built prototype for security gaps, implementing proper payment infrastructure, and getting a product production-ready. That's not because a good engineer can't do that work; it's because a brand-new hire spends their first weeks and months learning the codebase and the product before they can even start on the actual hardening task, turning a problem that a specialized team could close in one to three weeks into a project that doesn't really begin until month two or three of employment.

For a founder whose actual, immediate problem is "my AI-built prototype isn't secure or production-ready yet," a fixed-scope hardening engagement solves that specific problem faster and at a fraction of the cost of a first hire, precisely because it's scoped narrowly to the problem rather than to an open-ended, ongoing role. LaunchStudio's packages range from €800 to €7,500 depending on scope — meaning even the largest, most comprehensive Enterprise Hardening engagement costs less than one-tenth of a single year's fully loaded cost of a first engineering hire, and completes in one to three weeks rather than the four-to-five months it typically takes a new hire to become genuinely productive.

This isn't a case for never hiring — it's a case for sequencing correctly. A founder who hardens their existing AI-built product first, with a fixed-scope engagement, arrives at their eventual first engineering hire with a codebase that's actually secure and stable to onboard onto, rather than asking a brand-new hire to inherit both the ongoing product work and a backlog of security debt at the same time. It also means the revenue and traction that justify hiring a full-time engineer in the first place have more runway to actually materialize, since the product reaches production-ready status in weeks instead of months.

## Running Both, in Sequence, on the Same Runway

The founders who navigate this best in 2027 tend to follow a specific sequence rather than treating it as an either-or decision. First, a fixed-scope hardening engagement closes the immediate security, payment, and infrastructure gaps in the existing AI-built product — fast, bounded in cost, and complete within weeks. Second, with a stable, production-ready product actually generating revenue or demonstrable traction, the founder makes a full-time hiring decision from a position of evidence rather than urgency, with real usage data to define what that first hire should actually focus on. Third, the new hire onboards onto a codebase that's already been professionally hardened, meaning their ramp-up time goes toward learning the product and shipping new features, not discovering and fixing infrastructure gaps nobody flagged for them.

Founders who reverse this order — hiring first, hoping the new engineer will "figure out" the security and infrastructure gaps as part of general onboarding — routinely find that a task which should have taken one to three weeks with a specialized team instead becomes a slow-burn project that competes with feature development for months, because a generalist new hire has neither the specific pattern-recognition for AI-builder-generated codebase issues nor the bandwidth to prioritize it over the feature requests actively coming from the founder.

## The Opportunity Cost of the Search Itself

There's a cost in this decision that never appears on a payroll spreadsheet: the founder's own time during the hiring search. Sourcing candidates, screening resumes, running technical interviews, and negotiating offers routinely consumes eight to fifteen hours a week for a founder who's also trying to run every other part of the business — sales, customer support, fundraising, product decisions. Over a six-to-twelve week search, that's somewhere between 50 and 180 hours of founder attention diverted away from the business, at exactly the stage when a founder's personal time is usually the single scarcest resource the company has. None of those hours show up in the €70,000-€115,000 figure, but they're a real cost, paid in the currency founders can least afford to spend: their own limited bandwidth during the most fragile stage of the company.

A fixed-scope engagement doesn't eliminate founder involvement entirely — there's still a kickoff conversation and a review of the finished work — but it replaces weeks of ongoing recruiting overhead with a defined, bounded commitment, freeing that founder bandwidth for the parts of the business only the founder can actually do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Isn't it cheaper long-term to just hire an engineer instead of paying for a separate hardening engagement?

Not for this specific problem. A first hire's fully loaded cost (€70,000-€115,000 in year one) and four-to-five month ramp-up dwarfs a one-time fixed-scope hardening engagement (€800-€7,500, completed in one to three weeks). The hire remains valuable for ongoing product work — it's just not the efficient tool for a narrowly scoped, time-boxed hardening problem.

### Won't a new engineering hire eventually fix the same security and infrastructure gaps anyway?

Possibly, but not efficiently. A new hire has to first learn the existing codebase, then discover the gaps (which requires the same pattern-recognition a specialized hardening team already has), then prioritize fixing them against incoming feature requests from the founder — a process that routinely takes months rather than the one to three weeks a specialized engagement requires.

### Does hardening the product first make the eventual hiring decision easier?

Yes, in two ways: it gives the founder real usage and revenue data to define what the first hire should actually focus on, and it means the new hire's ramp-up time goes toward learning the product rather than discovering and fixing infrastructure debt nobody warned them about.

### What if I already have a full-time engineer — does LaunchStudio still make sense?

Often, yes, for the same time-boxing reason: many founding engineers are strong at feature development but haven't specifically specialized in auditing AI-builder-generated codebases for security and payment-infrastructure gaps. A fixed-scope engagement can close that specific gap in parallel, without pulling the existing engineer off product work for weeks.

### How is LaunchStudio's pricing structured compared to a salary?

LaunchStudio's packages are one-time, fixed-price engagements (€800-€7,500 depending on scope) tied to a defined deliverable and timeline, not an ongoing salary, equity, or benefits commitment — making it a fundamentally different budget category than a hire, suited to solving a specific problem rather than filling an ongoing role.
