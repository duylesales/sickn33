# Refund Policies and Fixed Scope: How LaunchStudio Protects Founders From Overruns

Every founder who has ever paid a deposit to a development team and then watched the invoice grow past the original quote knows a specific kind of dread: the moment a vendor says "well, once we got into it, we found some additional work." That sentence, more than almost any other in software development, is the one that turns a bootstrapped founder's runway math from "manageable" into "uncertain." This article explains exactly how LaunchStudio's fixed-scope structure and refund terms are built to make that sentence impossible to say honestly about a LaunchStudio engagement — and what actually happens, contractually and financially, if a project doesn't go as planned.

## Why Budget Overruns Happen in the First Place

Cost overruns in software development are rarely the result of outright dishonesty. They're usually the predictable outcome of a specific structural choice made before the contract was even signed: pricing an engagement by the hour, with no cap, against a scope that was estimated rather than verified. When a vendor estimates "should take about three weeks" without first reviewing the actual codebase, that estimate is a guess dressed up as a number, and the gap between the guess and reality becomes the founder's problem, not the vendor's, because the vendor is still getting paid for every additional hour regardless of whether the original estimate was accurate.

Change-order clauses compound this. A quote that includes language like "any additional scope will be billed separately at [rate]," with no definition of what counts as "additional" and no cap on total spend, effectively hands the vendor a mechanism to convert nearly anything discovered mid-project into extra billing — without needing to prove the founder actually asked for something new.

## How LaunchStudio's Fixed-Scope Model Removes the Overrun Risk Structurally

LaunchStudio's pricing model is built to make overruns structurally difficult rather than simply promising they won't happen. Every engagement begins with a direct review of the founder's actual codebase — not an estimate based on a verbal description, but an engineer actually looking at the Supabase schema, the Stripe integration, the deployment configuration, and everything else in scope for a production-hardening pass. Only after that review does LaunchStudio issue a quote, and that quote is a single fixed number tied to a specific, itemized deliverables list: which RLS policies get implemented, which webhook gets built, which secrets get moved server-side, which monitoring gets configured.

Because the quote comes after the review rather than before it, the two biggest sources of overrun — a vendor underestimating complexity because they never actually looked at the code, and a vague scope document that leaves room for later reinterpretation — are both closed before the founder signs anything. If a founder's needs genuinely expand mid-project — a new feature request, a scope addition the founder decides they want — that's treated as exactly what it is: new, separately scoped and quoted work requiring the founder's explicit approval, never a silent addition to the original invoice.

## What the Guarantee Covers if Something Goes Wrong Anyway

Fixed scope handles the "will the price change" question. A separate guarantee structure handles the "what if the work itself has a defect" question. Every LaunchStudio package includes a defined post-launch guarantee window covering bugs traceable to the hardening work actually performed — RLS policy errors, Stripe webhook bugs, secret management issues, deployment misconfigurations, and monitoring gaps. If the root cause sits inside the layer LaunchStudio was hired to harden, the fix is included at no additional cost, with no new invoice and no negotiation about fault.

This matters for the overrun conversation specifically because it closes the other common way founders end up paying more than expected: a vendor who delivers something technically complete but subtly broken, then charges again to fix what should have worked the first time. LaunchStudio's guarantee removes that incentive structure entirely — the same team that built the hardening is the one responsible for making sure it holds up, at no extra charge, within the guarantee window.

## How Payment Milestones Are Structured to Protect Both Sides

LaunchStudio ties payment to defined, verifiable delivery points rather than requesting the bulk of a fee upfront with nothing to show for it. This isn't a formality — it's a structural incentive alignment. A vendor who's only paid in full on verified delivery has every reason to actually finish the work to the agreed specification; a founder who's only committed to a large payment at a milestone they can independently confirm has real recourse if the vendor stalls or under-delivers. Compare this to the common alternative — 50% or more requested upfront with no milestone structure — where a founder who's paid has effectively no leverage left if the engagement goes sideways.

## What Happens If a Project Genuinely Doesn't Work Out

No vendor can promise zero risk of any disagreement ever occurring, and a fixed-scope model is honest about that rather than pretending otherwise. What the fixed-scope and milestone structure together provide is a framework for resolving disagreement based on the original, specific, written scope document rather than a subjective argument about what was "implied." If a deliverable genuinely wasn't completed as specified in the agreed scope, that's a concrete, checkable fact — the founder can compare the deliverables list against the finished product line by line — rather than a matter of interpretation the founder has no leverage to contest. This is precisely why LaunchStudio's deliverables lists are written as itemized, checkable line items (specific RLS policies on specific tables, a specific webhook implementation) rather than narrative descriptions: a founder isn't asked to trust that the work was done, they're given a document they can verify it against.

## Why This Structure Is Different From What Most Founders Have Experienced Before

Founders who've previously worked with freelancers or traditional agencies have often experienced the opposite of this structure without necessarily being able to name why it felt risky: an hourly clock with no ceiling, a scope described in a paragraph rather than itemized, and a large upfront payment with no milestone tied to verifiable progress. LaunchStudio's structure — codebase review before quote, fixed total price, itemized deliverables, milestone-based payment, and a post-launch guarantee window — closes each of those specific gaps individually, rather than offering a general assurance of good faith. The point isn't that other vendors are acting in bad faith; it's that a founder shouldn't have to rely on good faith when a better-structured contract can remove the need for it entirely.

## Key Takeaways

- LaunchStudio issues a fixed price only after a direct codebase review, closing the two most common sources of overrun: guessed estimates and vague scope that leaves room for later "additional work" billing.
- New feature requests discovered mid-project are scoped and quoted separately with the founder's explicit approval — never silently folded into the original invoice.
- A post-launch guarantee window covers bugs traceable to the hardening work itself at no additional cost, removing the incentive to under-deliver and charge again for a proper fix.
- Payments are tied to verifiable delivery milestones rather than a large upfront deposit, giving both the founder and the engineering team real, aligned incentives to see the project through as scoped.
- Itemized, checkable deliverables lists mean disagreements about whether work was completed can be resolved against a specific written document, not a subjective argument about what was implied.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does LaunchStudio offer refunds if a project isn't delivered as scoped?

LaunchStudio's structure is built to make this scenario rare rather than something founders need to rely on a refund to resolve: milestone-based payments mean a founder isn't paying in full until each verifiable stage of the itemized deliverables list is actually confirmed, so there's no large sum exposed to a project that stalls or under-delivers.

### How does LaunchStudio avoid the "additional scope" billing trap common at other agencies?

By quoting only after a direct review of the actual codebase, LaunchStudio's original scope is based on what the code actually requires, not a guess made before anyone looked. If a founder's needs genuinely grow afterward, that's scoped and quoted as a clear, separate addition requiring explicit approval — never silently billed against the original fixed price.

### What if a bug shows up after launch that traces back to LaunchStudio's own work?

That's covered by the post-launch guarantee window included with every package, at no additional cost, provided the root cause sits inside the specific layer LaunchStudio was hired to harden — RLS, webhooks, secret management, deployment configuration, or monitoring.

### How are payment milestones typically structured?

Payments are tied to specific, verifiable progress points — commonly a portion at project kickoff, a portion at a mid-project checkpoint with a working demo, and a final portion on verified delivery and sign-off — rather than a single large payment upfront with no intermediate confirmation.

### What happens if I, as the founder, want to add scope partway through the project?

New scope discovered or requested mid-project is quoted separately and requires your explicit written approval before any additional work begins or additional cost is incurred — your original fixed price and itemized scope remain untouched unless you approve the addition.
