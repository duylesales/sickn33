# Choosing Between a Contract Renewal and Switching Dev Partners for Year Two
Somewhere between mid-November and the end of December, most founders who hired a development partner in year one face a decision they didn't budget mental energy for: the renewal email arrives, or the annual contract quietly auto-renews unless someone actively cancels it, and there's a narrow window to decide whether to sign again or start over with someone new. It's tempting to treat this as a formality — renewing feels like the path of least resistance, and switching feels like starting from zero. Neither assumption holds up under scrutiny. This article walks through the actual decision framework: what a renewal genuinely offers, what switching genuinely costs, and how to tell which situation you're actually in before the auto-renewal date makes the decision for you.

## Why Renewal Feels Like the Default — and Sometimes Is

There are real, defensible reasons to renew a contract with an existing dev partner. They already understand your codebase, your product decisions, and the reasoning behind choices that aren't documented anywhere except in the team's collective memory. Switching partners means paying, in some form, for a new team to rebuild that context — reading the code, asking the same "why did you do it this way" questions, and inevitably making a few decisions your original team would have avoided because they'd already learned the hard way. If your current partner has been responsive, delivered on scope and timeline, and your product's needs for year two look similar to year one, renewal is often the lower-friction, lower-risk choice — and there's nothing wrong with choosing lower friction when the underlying relationship is actually working.

The trap is treating "renewal is easier" as the same statement as "renewal is better." Those are different questions, and conflating them is how founders end up paying another year of a relationship that's quietly not serving them, simply because the alternative feels like more work to evaluate.

## The Signs Your Year One Partner Isn't the Right Year Two Partner

A handful of patterns are worth naming directly, because they're the ones founders most often rationalize away rather than act on. If your current partner bills hourly with no fixed scope, and you've noticed the invoice creeping upward each quarter without a corresponding increase in what's actually getting delivered, that's not a personality issue — it's a structural incentive problem: hourly billing pays the same whether a fix takes two hours or six. If your current partner is a generalist agency that handled your initial build but has no specific track record hardening AI-builder-generated codebases — the exact category of security, payment, and data-isolation issues that surface once real users arrive — year two may require expertise your year one partner never actually needed to have. And if your product's needs have genuinely shifted — from "build features" in year one to "pass an enterprise security review" or "scale past 10,000 users" in year two — that's a different skill set than the one you originally hired for, regardless of how well that original hire performed on the original job.

None of these signs mean the year one partner was a bad choice. It's entirely possible to have made the right decision for year one and still need a different decision for year two, because the problem you're solving has changed shape.

## What Switching Actually Costs — and What It Doesn't

The fear founders describe most often when considering a switch is "starting over" — as if a new partner has to relearn everything from a blank slate, the way a new hire would need months of onboarding. For a specialized hardening engagement like the ones LaunchStudio scopes, that fear is mostly unfounded, because the entire model is built around not touching what already works. A hardening partner reviewing an existing AI-built codebase for year two isn't rebuilding the frontend or relearning your product's UX decisions — they're auditing the backend, security posture, and infrastructure against a known checklist: is data properly isolated between customers, are payments confirmed server-side, are secrets out of client-side code, is there real monitoring. That review typically takes days, not months, and produces a concrete list of what actually needs fixing, versus what's already solid.

What switching genuinely does cost is a short ramp-up period — a few days for a new team to read the codebase and understand what's there — and the mild discomfort of ending a relationship, which is a real but usually short-lived cost compared to another year of a partnership that isn't meeting the product's current needs. It does not, in most cases, mean losing months of context or paying to rebuild anything that already works.

## A Framework for the Actual Decision

Rather than defaulting to whichever option feels less effortful in December, it's worth running through four concrete questions before the renewal date arrives. First: has the price-to-output ratio held steady, or has it crept in a direction that doesn't match what's actually being delivered? Second: does your year two roadmap require capabilities — security hardening, compliance readiness, scale engineering — that your year one partner has actually demonstrated, or only implicitly assumed? Third: when something has gone wrong in year one — a bug, a missed deadline, a scope disagreement — was the resolution fast and fair, or did it require repeated escalation? Fourth: if you got an independent, no-obligation review of your current codebase's actual production-readiness, would you be confident in what it found, or is there a nagging sense that some of what your year one partner shipped hasn't really been stress-tested?

A founder who answers all four questions favorably toward their existing partner has a genuinely strong case for renewal, and should renew with confidence rather than switching purely out of restlessness. A founder who hesitates on two or more of those questions is very likely paying the "easier" cost of renewal while quietly accumulating the harder cost of unaddressed gaps — the kind that tend to surface at the worst possible moment, like mid-scale-up or mid-procurement-review.

## Getting an Independent Read Before You Decide Either Way

The single highest-leverage move a founder can make before a renewal deadline is getting an independent codebase review — not from the partner asking to be renewed, but from a neutral third party whose only job is an honest assessment. This isn't an argument for switching by default; it's an argument for deciding with actual information rather than inertia. A short, fixed-scope review can confirm that your year one partner's work is genuinely solid, in which case renewal is the right call with real evidence behind it — or it can surface specific, fixable gaps that inform exactly what to ask for in a renewed contract, or what to look for in a new one.

## The Auto-Renewal Trap

One structural detail makes this decision harder than it should be: many dev partner contracts include an auto-renewal clause that fires unless the founder actively cancels within a defined notice window, often 30 or 60 days before the term ends. That clause exists for the vendor's benefit, not the founder's — it converts founder inertia into automatic revenue, and it means a founder who's quietly unhappy but hasn't gotten around to evaluating alternatives ends up locked into another twelve months by default, not by decision. The practical fix is simple but easy to forget under year-end workload: calendar the notice deadline the moment a contract is signed, not when the renewal email arrives, so the decision to renew or switch is always made deliberately, with enough runway to get an independent review first, rather than being made by the calendar itself.

## Key Takeaways

- Renewing a dev partner contract is a legitimate, often correct choice when the price-to-output ratio has held steady and your year two needs still match your year one partner's demonstrated strengths.
- Hourly billing with no fixed scope and a creeping invoice, a generalist agency without hardening-specific experience, and a shift in your product's needs (from feature-building to security or scale readiness) are the clearest signs it's worth evaluating a switch.
- Switching to a specialized partner for a hardening engagement is typically a days-long codebase review, not a months-long re-onboarding — the fear of "starting over" is usually overstated for this category of work.
- A structured four-question framework — pricing trend, capability match, dispute resolution history, and confidence in an independent review — replaces gut-feel renewal decisions with an actual evaluation.
- Getting an independent, no-obligation codebase review before a renewal deadline is the highest-leverage move available; it either confirms your current partner deserves renewal or surfaces gaps worth acting on either way.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my dev partner's rising invoice is a red flag or just normal scope growth?

Compare what's actually shipping against what's being billed over a few consecutive invoices. If the hourly total is climbing but the delivered features or fixes aren't growing proportionally, that's a structural incentive issue with hourly billing, not a one-off. A fixed-scope partner removes that ambiguity entirely by pricing the outcome, not the hours.

### Does switching dev partners really only take a few days to ramp up?

For a specialized hardening engagement, yes, in most cases. The work is a backend and security audit against an existing frontend that isn't being touched, which is a much narrower scope than a full re-onboarding. A codebase review typically takes days, producing a concrete list of what needs fixing before any work begins.

### What if my year one partner did good work but my needs have just changed?

That's one of the most common and least dramatic reasons to switch — no partner needs to have failed for their scope to no longer match your stage. A partner suited to early feature-building isn't automatically suited to security hardening or enterprise-readiness work, and recognizing that distinction isn't a criticism of year one.

### Is it worth getting an independent review even if I'm planning to renew?

Yes — an independent review either confirms your current partner's work is solid, which makes renewal a confident decision rather than a default one, or it surfaces specific gaps you can raise directly in renewal negotiations, giving you leverage either way.

### What does LaunchStudio actually check in an independent codebase review?

The review covers the categories that most commonly go unaddressed in AI-builder-generated codebases: Row Level Security enforcement and data isolation between customers, whether payments are confirmed server-side via signed webhooks, whether API keys and secrets are exposed in client-side code, and whether real error monitoring and backups exist.
