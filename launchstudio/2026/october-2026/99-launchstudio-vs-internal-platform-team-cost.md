# LaunchStudio vs. Building an Internal Platform Team: A Founder's Cost Comparison

At some point after your AI-generated prototype starts gaining real users, a specific thought crosses every founder's mind: "Maybe I should just hire someone full-time for this." Infrastructure keeps needing attention, security reviews keep coming up, database performance keeps mattering more as you grow, and outsourcing each fix piecemeal starts to feel inefficient compared to having a dedicated person who just knows your system. This is a legitimate instinct, and at a certain scale, building an internal platform team is absolutely the right call. But for most AI SaaS founders weighing this decision in their first one to two years, the actual cost comparison is far less favorable to hiring than it first appears. This article walks through the real, fully-loaded cost of both paths, so you can make this call with numbers instead of instinct.

## What "Hiring a Platform Engineer" Actually Costs

Founders comparing options tend to compare LaunchStudio's package pricing against a platform engineer's advertised salary, and that comparison is misleading because salary is only one part of the real cost. A senior platform or DevOps engineer capable of independently handling security hardening, database scaling, infrastructure cost optimization, and compliance work — the full scope most AI-builder founders actually need — commands a meaningfully higher salary in most European tech markets than a mid-level generalist developer, precisely because this skill set is specialized and in high demand. On top of base salary, the fully-loaded cost includes employer payroll taxes and social contributions (typically adding 20-30% on top of gross salary in most European jurisdictions), benefits, equipment, software licenses and tooling, and recruiting cost — which for a specialized senior hire can itself run into the thousands of euros in agency fees or the equivalent in founder time spent sourcing and interviewing candidates. None of this shows up in a simple salary comparison, but all of it hits your runway the same way payroll does.

## The Ramp-Up Cost Nobody Budgets For

Even after a platform engineer is hired, there is a real cost before they become fully productive: the time spent learning your specific AI-generated codebase, your particular Supabase or Postgres configuration, the quirks of whichever AI builder (Lovable, Bolt, Cursor) produced your original architecture, and the business context behind decisions that were made under launch pressure and never documented. This ramp-up period, typically four to eight weeks for a senior hire joining a small team with limited onboarding infrastructure, is time you're paying full salary for work that isn't yet at full output — a cost that's invisible on a hiring spreadsheet but very real on your bank statement. A specialized delivery partner doesn't carry this same ramp-up cost in the same way, because the team has already hardened dozens of AI-builder outputs and recognizes the common patterns immediately, rather than discovering your specific codebase's quirks from a standing start.

## The Utilization Problem: Paying Full-Time for Part-Time Work

This is the crux of why hiring often doesn't make sense for most AI SaaS founders in their first one to two years: platform and infrastructure work is genuinely not a full-time job at early-to-mid stage usage levels. A security audit, a database scaling pass, a compliance hardening sprint, a cost optimization review — these are real, valuable, periodic pieces of work, but they don't add up to 40 hours a week of sustained demand until a product has meaningfully scaled its user base, its data volume, and its infrastructure complexity. A founder who hires a full-time platform engineer at this stage is often paying a full salary for a role that's genuinely busy perhaps 30-40% of the time, with the engineer either underutilized or drifting into general feature development work that isn't actually their specialization — work a generalist developer could have done for less. A package-based or project-based engagement, by contrast, is priced against the actual scope of work needed, not against a fixed 40-hour weekly commitment you may not have enough specialized work to fill.

## When Hiring Actually Becomes the Right Call

To be fair to the hiring path, there is a real scale point where an internal platform team becomes the economically correct choice, and founders should know what that inflection point looks like rather than defaulting to either extreme out of habit. Once infrastructure and platform work is genuinely constant — multiple concurrent projects, a growing engineering team that needs platform support daily, a data volume and traffic pattern requiring ongoing tuning rather than periodic review — the utilization problem disappears, and a full-time hire's fully-loaded cost starts to compare favorably against an ongoing series of external engagements. This inflection point typically arrives well after a founder's first year of real revenue, often once a company has its own multi-person engineering team that a platform specialist needs to support directly, rather than a single founder or a two-to-three-person team still doing much of the core product work themselves.

## The Hybrid Model Most Growing AI SaaS Companies Actually Use

The realistic picture for most founders isn't "hire" versus "outsource forever" — it's a sequence. Early on, when infrastructure work is periodic rather than constant, a specialized partner delivers the needed expertise without the fixed cost and utilization risk of a full-time hire. As the company scales and platform work becomes genuinely continuous, bringing that function in-house starts to make sense, often with the external partner staying involved for specialized, occasional work (a major compliance review, a significant scaling event, an infrastructure migration) that doesn't justify keeping every possible specialization on staff even at a larger scale. Founders who try to jump straight to a full internal team before infrastructure work is genuinely constant tend to either overpay for underutilized specialized talent or end up with a generalist hire who lacks the depth for the specific, high-stakes problems — security, compliance, scaling — that a specialist would catch.

## The Key-Person Risk of a Single Internal Hire

There is a risk to the hiring path that rarely shows up in a cost spreadsheet but matters enormously for a small company: concentrating all your infrastructure and security knowledge in one person. If your one platform engineer takes a two-week vacation during a security incident, leaves for a better offer six months in, or is simply out sick during a critical scaling event, there is no backup — the knowledge of your specific architecture, the decisions behind it, and the context needed to fix problems quickly lives in a single person's head, often undocumented, because a single generalist hire under normal workload pressure rarely has the bandwidth to also maintain thorough documentation for a hypothetical successor. A specialized delivery partner does not carry this same single point of failure risk, because the work sits with a team, not an individual, and the team's collective experience across many client engagements means institutional knowledge doesn't walk out the door with one person's resignation. This is a genuinely underappreciated factor in the hire-versus-outsource decision, and it becomes more consequential, not less, the smaller and earlier-stage your company is, because a small team has the least slack to absorb the sudden loss of its only specialist.

## The Real Numbers: A Practical Comparison

Consider a founder needing a security audit, database hardening, and cost optimization work over a six-month period — a realistic scope for a growing AI SaaS product in its first year of real traction. A LaunchStudio engagement covering this scope, likely combining a Launch Ready audit with a Relaunch & Scale hardening pass, typically runs in the €3,000-6,000 range total, delivered by a team that has already solved these exact problems dozens of times, with no ramp-up cost and no ongoing salary commitment once the work is done. The fully-loaded cost of a senior platform engineer over that same six months, factoring salary, employer costs, benefits, tooling, and ramp-up time, typically runs several times higher, for a role that is meaningfully underutilized at this stage of most companies' growth. That gap doesn't mean hiring is wrong — it means the gap needs to be understood and the timing needs to be deliberate, not assumed.

## Key Takeaways

- The real cost of hiring a platform engineer includes payroll taxes, benefits, tooling, recruiting, and a four-to-eight-week ramp-up period, not just the advertised salary — all of it hits your runway.

- Platform and infrastructure work is genuinely not a full-time job at early-to-mid stage usage levels, meaning a full-time hire is often paying for 40 hours of capacity to fill 15-20 hours of real specialized demand.

- Hiring an internal platform team becomes the right economic call once infrastructure work is genuinely constant — typically after a founder's first year of real revenue, once a multi-person engineering team needs daily platform support.

- The realistic path for most growing AI SaaS companies is sequential: a specialized external partner early, transitioning to an internal team as platform work becomes continuous, often with the partner staying involved for occasional specialized needs even after that transition.

- A scoped, project-based engagement is priced against actual work needed, not a fixed weekly commitment — for periodic needs like audits, scaling passes, and compliance hardening, this typically costs a fraction of a fully-loaded senior hire over the same period.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it cheaper to hire a platform engineer or use a service like LaunchStudio?

For most AI SaaS founders in their first one to two years, a project-based engagement is significantly cheaper than a full-time hire, once payroll taxes, benefits, tooling, recruiting costs, and ramp-up time are factored into the hire's real cost. Hiring becomes more cost-competitive only once infrastructure work is genuinely constant, which usually happens later in a company's growth.

### How do I know if I need a full-time platform engineer yet?

The key signal is utilization: if your infrastructure, security, and scaling work adds up to periodic projects rather than a sustained 40-hour-a-week demand, a full-time hire will likely be underutilized. This is typically the case until a company has its own multi-person engineering team needing daily platform support.

### What does the ramp-up cost of a new hire actually include?

It's the four-to-eight-week period, typical for a senior hire joining a small team, where you're paying full salary while the engineer learns your specific AI-generated codebase, database configuration, and the undocumented context behind decisions made under launch pressure — time that doesn't show up on a hiring spreadsheet but does hit your runway.

### Can I use an external partner and later build an internal team?

Yes, and this is the realistic path for most growing AI SaaS companies — a specialized partner handles infrastructure work early when it's periodic, and the function moves in-house once platform work becomes genuinely continuous, often with the partner staying involved for occasional specialized needs even afterward.

### Does LaunchStudio replace the need for an internal engineering team eventually?

No, and it isn't meant to for a company that scales significantly. LaunchStudio is built to deliver specialized infrastructure, security, and scaling expertise on a project basis, which fits most founders' actual workload in their early stages; a larger company with continuous platform needs will eventually benefit from an internal team, potentially alongside continued specialized engagements.
