"The challenge is no longer turning ideas into software. It's the architecture and security needed to bring that software to maturity." Herre Roelevink, founder of Manifera and CEO of LaunchStudio, says this to describe exactly the position most AI-native founders find themselves in today — and it's a useful way to reframe what AI software development actually costs you, because the cost isn't in the part you already did. If you built your prototype with Lovable or Bolt and can't read a line of the code it wrote, that's fine — you were never supposed to need to. What you do need is a realistic number for what comes next, because "I'll figure out pricing later" is how founders end up quoted €18,000 by a freelancer three weeks before they wanted to launch.

This is a straightforward cost breakdown, written for someone who can't audit code themselves and needs a number they can actually plan around.

## What You've Already Paid For (And What It Doesn't Include)

Most AI coding tools run somewhere between free and a few hundred euros a month in subscription cost, which buys you the frontend, basic app logic, and a working demo. That's real value for very little money — a decade ago the equivalent would have cost tens of thousands in a design and dev team's time. What that subscription doesn't include, because it was never designed to, is a production-grade database, tested authorization between users, working payment processing, monitored hosting, or a security review. Those are separate categories of work, done by people, not something a monthly SaaS fee for an AI tool was ever going to bundle in.

## Option 1: Do Nothing and Launch As-Is

This costs €0 upfront and is, functionally, a bet. Given that 80% of AI-built projects never reach production and 45% of AI-generated code carries a security vulnerability serious enough to matter, launching without addressing either number is a real risk, not a hypothetical one. The "cost" here isn't a line item — it's the expected value of a data breach, a payment failure, or simply an app that can't handle real traffic once your first batch of customers actually shows up.

It's worth being honest about why founders choose this option anyway: it's not usually recklessness, it's that the risk feels abstract and the cost of fixing it feels concrete and possibly unnecessary. That calculation flips the moment something actually goes wrong, at which point the founder is paying for the fix anyway, plus whatever reputational cost comes from a customer discovering the issue first.

## Option 2: Hire a Freelancer

Freelance rates for this kind of production-hardening work typically land somewhere between €5,000 and €20,000, and the range is wide because outcomes are inconsistent — a freelancer might genuinely fix your gaps in three weeks, or they might spend three months trying to understand code an AI tool generated without documentation, running up hours while you wait. This is the most common "hidden cost" trap for non-technical founders: a rate that looks reasonable per hour turns into an unpredictable total because nobody scoped the actual gap upfront.

The specific risk with freelancers isn't skill — plenty are genuinely capable — it's unfamiliarity with the particular patterns AI tools tend to produce. A freelancer who's spent years hand-writing every line of code for clients may reasonably default to treating an AI-generated codebase with more caution than it needs, padding estimates to cover their own uncertainty about architecture they didn't build and haven't seen the shape of before.

## Option 3: Hire a Traditional Agency

A traditional software agency will typically want to rebuild significant portions of your product from scratch, using their own stack and processes, at a cost ranging from €20,000 to well over €500,000, over three to twelve months. For a founder who already has a working, AI-built frontend they like, this is usually the wrong tool for the job — you're paying full rebuild pricing to redo work that's already been done correctly. It's not that agencies do bad work; it's that their process typically wasn't designed around the specific scenario of a founder who already has a functioning product and just needs the last stretch finished. You end up paying for months of discovery, design, and rebuild on a product where most of that work is already sitting in front of you.

## What Actually Drives Your Specific Number

Within the €800–€7,500 range, your specific number depends on a few concrete factors, and knowing them helps you sanity-check any quote you receive. What you're building matters most — a simple website-style tool costs less to harden than a full SaaS platform with multiple user roles and a database of real customer data. What you already have matters too: a working prototype costs less to finish than a design-only mockup or an idea with nothing built yet, since more of the foundation is already in place. And what you specifically need drives the rest — adding payment processing, user accounts, security hardening, and hosting each adds a defined amount to the total, rather than being folded into a vague "it depends" answer.

This is worth knowing specifically because it means a legitimate quote should be able to explain itself in these terms. If a quote can't tell you which of these factors is driving the number — what you're building, what you already have, and what you specifically need added — that's a sign the scoping wasn't done carefully, regardless of whether the number itself sounds reasonable.

## The Real Comparison Isn't Price — It's Price Per Certainty

The cheapest option on paper (do nothing) carries the highest hidden risk. The most expensive option (a full agency rebuild) solves a problem you may not actually have, since your frontend already works. The middle options — freelancer or fixed-scope specialist — differ mainly in how predictable the outcome is, and predictability is worth paying for when the alternative is discovering a security gap after launch instead of before it.

For a founder who can't personally verify code quality, predictability isn't a nice-to-have, it's the entire basis on which you can make a rational decision at all. You're not choosing based on your own read of the technical work — you're choosing based on how confident you can be in the number and the process before you commit to either, and that confidence is exactly what a fixed, scoped quote after a short review is designed to provide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do freelancer quotes for AI-built apps vary so much?

Freelancers unfamiliar with AI-generated code often assume more needs to be rebuilt than actually does, since they lack the pattern recognition of reviewing that kind of codebase regularly, which inflates both price and timeline.

### Is it cheaper to rebuild my app with a traditional agency instead of fixing it?

Almost never, if your frontend already works. Agencies typically price around a full rebuild, which is unnecessary if the underlying issue is a missing production layer rather than a flawed product concept.

### What does a fixed-scope quote actually include?

Typically the specific gaps found in a technical review — database security, authorization, payments, and hosting — priced as one number after that review, rather than an open-ended hourly rate.

### How accurate is a price calculator compared to a real quote?

A calculator gives a solid estimate range based on what you're building and what you need, while a final fixed quote comes after a short call confirms the specifics of your actual prototype.

### Do I risk being overcharged if I don't understand the code myself?

That risk drops significantly when the reviewing team specializes in AI-generated code specifically, since they can quickly separate genuine gaps from unfamiliar-but-fine architecture, unlike a generalist freelancer guessing at scope.
