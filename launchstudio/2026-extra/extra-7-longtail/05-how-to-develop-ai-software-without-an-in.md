A client walks into your agency with a working prototype they built themselves using Lovable, a logo, a pitch deck, and a launch date they've already told their investors about. They want you to "just make it real." Your team is three people deep — a designer, a project manager, and you — and none of you have shipped a production backend in the last two years. This is not a hypothetical. It's the exact position a growing number of small agencies and freelancers find themselves in right now, as AI tools put working prototypes in the hands of clients who never used to show up with anything more than a Figma file. Here's how to actually develop AI software for that client without hiring, without a six-month ramp-up, and without losing the account.

## Step 1: Separate What the Client Actually Built From What Still Needs Building

Before you commit to anything, get access to the client's prototype and do a plain-language inventory: what works, what's clickable-but-fake, and what's entirely missing. Most AI-built prototypes have a real, working frontend and partial backend logic, but gaps in the areas that don't show up in a demo — persistent data storage, authorization between user accounts, payment processing, and production hosting. Naming this gap clearly, in terms the client can understand, is what separates a controlled scope from an open-ended mess later.

This inventory doesn't need to be technical in language, even if the client is more sophisticated than most. A simple framing works well: "here's what's genuinely built and working, here's what looks built but isn't wired up yet, and here's what hasn't been started." Clients who arrive with an AI-built prototype are often surprised to learn how much of it is frontend polish versus functioning infrastructure — many assume that because it looks finished, it mostly is, and correcting that assumption early, gently, protects the relationship far more than discovering it together after a missed deadline.

## Step 2: Decide What You Build In-House and What You Don't

Some agencies try to stretch their existing team across unfamiliar backend and security work because saying no to the scope feels like saying no to the client. This usually backfires — either the timeline slips past what was promised, or the work gets done but not well, and the gap surfaces after launch instead of before it, which is a much worse place for it to show up. The more sustainable approach: keep design, project management, and client relationship work in-house, and route the backend, security, and production engineering to a partner who does that specific work daily.

There's a version of this decision that feels like admitting weakness, and it isn't. A design agency saying "we don't do backend security audits in-house, so we partner with specialists for that" is no different from a marketing agency partnering with a video production house for a campaign that needs it — it's specialization, not a gap in competence. Clients generally respond well to a clear, confident explanation of how the work gets delivered, and poorly to discovering after the fact that a promised capability didn't actually exist internally.

One of the reasons agencies avoid subcontracting backend work is bad past experience with open-ended freelance engagements that balloon in time and cost. A structured partner quotes fixed scope after a short technical review — for a project like a client's SaaS prototype, that typically falls into the [Launch & Grow package](https://launchstudio.eu/en/#packages) range, covering payments, hosting, and ongoing production support at a fixed number you can mark up transparently and present to your client as your own delivery estimate.

## Step 5: Keep the Client Relationship, Hand Off the Production Risk

The single biggest advantage of this approach for an agency isn't cost — it's risk transfer. If a security gap or a broken payment flow surfaces after launch, that liability sits with engineers who specialize in exactly this category of problem and who documented what they built, rather than with a generalist team debugging unfamiliar territory under client pressure. You keep the account, the relationship, and the margin. You hand off the part of the work that was never your core competency to begin with.

This matters more than it might initially seem, because production issues in someone else's client project are a uniquely bad place to be caught unprepared — the client's trust, your agency's reputation, and a live product are all on the line simultaneously, and "we're still figuring out what went wrong" is not a sentence any agency wants to say twice. ## Step 6: Deliver, Document, and Repeat

Once the project ships, make sure you receive full documentation and clean, client-owned code — this is non-negotiable for a white-label arrangement, since it protects both your credibility with the client and your ability to bring the same partner back for the next one. A good production partner treats this as standard practice, not a special request, because the code needs to be maintainable by whoever touches it next, agency or otherwise.

## Step 7: Build the Relationship Before You Need It Urgently

The scenario that opened this article — a client showing up with a working prototype and a launch date already promised to investors — is exactly the moment you don't want to be evaluating engineering partners for the first time. The agencies that handle this well have usually already had at least one conversation with a production partner before the pressure hit, so when a client does show up with an urgent AI-built prototype, the response is "we know exactly who handles this," not a scramble to find someone credible on short notice. That single difference tends to separate agencies that keep the account under pressure from ones that lose it to a competitor who looked more prepared.

This is worth treating as ongoing infrastructure for your agency, not a one-time fix for a single client emergency. AI-native clients showing up with working prototypes instead of blank-slate briefs is becoming the default, not the exception, and having a reliable answer for "who builds the production layer" is quickly becoming as core to running a small agency as having a reliable designer or project manager already is.
