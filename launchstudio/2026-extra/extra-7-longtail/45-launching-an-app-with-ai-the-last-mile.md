A client just handed you a Lovable link and said "it's basically done, we just need to launch it." How do you actually verify that in the next hour, without quoting a full rebuild you know they can't afford and don't need? This is the exact position a growing share of small agencies and freelancers find themselves in: clients arriving having already built an app with AI, expecting the last stretch to production to be quick, cheap, and low-drama. It can be — if you know what to check.

This guide walks through that last mile step by step, written specifically for the agency or freelancer sitting between an AI-native client and a launch deadline, not for the founder building solo.

## Step 1: Separate "It Works" From "It's Production-Ready" for Any App With AI

Before quoting anything, walk the client through the difference out loud. An app with AI built into it can look completely finished — polished UI, working demo flows — while still missing server-side authorization, real payment handling, and production hosting. Setting this expectation early prevents the awkward conversation later where the client assumes "launch" means flipping a switch rather than doing real, billable work.

## Step 2: Audit the Codebase Against a Fixed List, Not a Vibe

Rather than reading through the entire codebase impressionistically, check five specific things: whether authorization is enforced server-side on every data endpoint, whether payment webhooks handle failures and not just successes, whether the database has backups and access controls, whether the app is deployed to a real, monitored production environment, and whether basic error logging exists. This turns a vague "let me look into it" into a concrete scope you can quote confidently within a day.

## Step 3: Decide What You Can Fix In-House and What You Should White-Label

Most small agencies and solo freelancers are strong on frontend polish, client communication, and project management, but thinner on the specific backend security and infrastructure work an app with AI often needs. Rather than turning down the last-mile work or quoting something you're not confident delivering well, this is exactly the gap a white-label partner closes — your branding and client relationship stay intact, while the actual hardening work is delivered by a specialized team behind the scenes.

## Step 4: Quote the Client a Fixed Scope, Not an Open-Ended Estimate

Clients who've already built with AI tend to expect fixed-price, fast turnaround — it's the norm the AI tools themselves set. An open-ended hourly estimate for "cleanup work" tends to erode trust fast, especially with a client who just paid nothing to a tool for the first 60% of their build. Scope the last mile as its own fixed-price line item, ideally with a delivery window measured in days or a couple of weeks, not months.

## Step 6: Deliver the Launch and Keep the Relationship

Once the last-mile work is done, the client sees a launched, secure app under your agency's name and delivery, while the specialized hardening work happened quietly behind the scenes. This is the model worth building into your standard offering rather than treating as a one-off: clients will keep arriving with apps built with AI, and having a reliable, fast, white-label partner for the last mile turns what used to be a scope problem into a repeatable, profitable service line. ## Step 7: Put an NDA and Clear Handoff Terms in Writing Before You Start

Before bringing in any production partner, white-label or otherwise, get an NDA in place and agree in writing on exactly what "done" looks like for this specific project. This protects both your client relationship and your own agency's reputation, and it prevents the scope-creep conversations that otherwise happen mid-project when expectations were only ever discussed verbally. A production partner used to working with agencies should have standard NDA paperwork ready to sign quickly, without turning it into its own negotiation.

## Step 8: Set the Right Expectation on Ongoing Maintenance With Your Client

Many clients assume that once their app with AI is launched, it's simply done — no further cost, no further attention needed. Set that expectation correctly upfront: production apps need monitoring, security updates, and occasional fixes as dependencies change, the same as any software product. Whether you handle that ongoing relationship yourself with a support retainer, or pass it through to your white-label partner's managed hosting plan, deciding this before launch prevents an awkward conversation three months later when something needs attention and nobody agreed who's responsible for it.

## Step 9: Build a Repeatable Intake Process for the Next Client

The first time you run through this last-mile process, it'll feel like a one-off project. The real value shows up once you formalize it into a repeatable intake step for every new client who arrives with an app with AI already built: a standard first-meeting script covering the "it works versus it's ready" distinction, a fixed checklist you run their codebase through, and a pre-negotiated relationship with your production partner so scoping a new project takes hours, not days. Agencies that build this into their standard onboarding tend to close last-mile engagements faster and with less client friction than agencies treating each one as a fresh, from-scratch negotiation.

## Step 10: Know When to Say the Rebuild Really Is Necessary

Not every app with AI is a good candidate for a last-mile fix, and it's worth being honest with clients about that too. If a client's core requirements have genuinely outgrown what their chosen AI tool's architecture can reasonably support — extremely high transaction volume, complex multi-tenant permission structures far beyond what a quick fix addresses — a partial rebuild or a different technical approach may actually be the right call. The skill this guide is really building isn't "always avoid a rebuild." It's knowing, specifically and confidently, when a rebuild is truly warranted versus when it's the easier answer for whoever's giving the quote.

Getting comfortable making that call — fix versus rebuild, in-house versus white-label, fixed scope versus open-ended — is genuinely what separates agencies that profitably absorb the growing wave of AI-native clients from agencies that either turn the work away or lose money guessing at scope after the fact. It's a learnable judgment, not an instinct you either have or don't, and it gets sharper with every client project you run through the same disciplined process described above.

Treat the first few clients who arrive with an app with AI as a chance to build this muscle deliberately, documenting what you found, what you quoted, and how the fix actually went, rather than letting the knowledge stay informal in one person's head. That documentation becomes the foundation for training any junior staff you bring on later, and it's what turns "the agency happens to be good at this" into "the agency has a repeatable process for this" — a distinction clients increasingly notice and value as more of them arrive holding an AI-built prototype instead of a blank brief.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can my agency offer AI app launch services without hiring backend engineers?

Yes. A white-label production partner handles the backend, security, and infrastructure work under your branding, so you can offer full last-mile launch services without building that specialized capacity in-house.

### How do I quote last-mile work to a client who already paid an AI tool almost nothing?

Frame it as a distinct, fixed-price service — production-hardening, not "finishing the app" — since clients generally accept that security, payments, and hosting are a separate, specialized scope from prototype-building.

### What's the biggest risk of taking on an app with AI without checking it first?

Quoting a launch date before confirming what's actually missing. Authorization gaps, incomplete payment handling, and unmonitored hosting are common enough that skipping the audit step risks a launch that breaks in front of the client's own customers.

### Does white-labeling the production work mean losing client trust or control?

No, when structured properly the client relationship, branding, and delivery timeline stay with the agency, while the specialized hardening happens behind the scenes exactly as if it were an internal team.

### How fast can a white-label last-mile fix typically be delivered?

Most single-app engagements complete within one to two weeks at a fixed price, which fits comfortably inside a typical agency project timeline without disrupting client-facing milestones.
