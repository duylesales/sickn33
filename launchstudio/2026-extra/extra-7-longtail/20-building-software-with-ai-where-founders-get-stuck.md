80% of AI-built projects never reach production. Sit with that number for a second, because it's not describing bad ideas, weak execution, or lazy founders — it's describing a specific, structural stall point that happens after building software with AI works exactly as intended. The prototype exists. It's genuinely good, sometimes good enough to raise a friends-and-family round on the strength of the demo alone. And then, for four out of five founders, it just... stops there. Understanding exactly where that stall happens, and comparing the paths out of it, is more useful than another motivational post about "shipping fast."

## Where the Stall Actually Happens

It's not at the idea stage, and it's rarely at the "does the AI tool work" stage — Bolt, Cursor, Lovable, and v0 all reliably produce working, demoable prototypes now, faster than almost anyone predicted a few years ago. The stall consistently happens at the same three junctions, regardless of the founder's background.

**Junction one: realizing the prototype and the product are different things.** This is where founders first discover that "it works when I click through it" doesn't mean "it's ready for a stranger with a credit card." Some founders catch this quickly. Many don't catch it until a beta user finds a bug that should have been obvious, or until they try to actually process a real payment and it doesn't work.

**Junction two: pricing out what closing the gap costs.** This is where a lot of founders freeze. A traditional agency quote for a full rebuild — often €20,000 or more — looks like it validates every fear about software being expensive, and plenty of founders shelve a genuinely good product at exactly this point because they compared their actual, narrow need against the price of a service built for a completely different scope.

**Junction three: finding someone who understands AI-generated code specifically.** Even founders who get past junction two often stall here, because a general freelancer or dev shop has to spend real time understanding code generated by a tool they may not have worked with before they can safely touch it — and that unfamiliarity shows up as delay, inflated pricing, or both.

## Why the Same Three Junctions Show Up Regardless of Which AI Tool You Used

It's tempting to assume the stall point depends heavily on which tool built your prototype — that Bolt projects fail differently than Cursor projects, or that v0's output needs different handling than Lovable's. In practice, the three junctions above show up almost identically across all four tools, because none of them are really about the tool's code quality. They're about what a prompt-driven build process structurally can't include: a founder's honest assessment of what's still missing, an accurate price for closing that gap, and someone who can move quickly on code they didn't originally write. Those three things are independent of which AI generated the underlying implementation. A polished, well-structured Bolt prototype and a slightly rougher Cursor one can both stall at exactly the same three junctions, for exactly the same reasons, because the gap was never really about code quality in the first place.

This matters practically because it means founders comparing AI tools based on "which one produces more production-ready code" are optimizing for something that barely moves the actual outcome. All four tools produce prototypes that need the same category of finishing work. The tool choice affects how fast and pleasant the building process feels — a real and legitimate consideration — but it doesn't meaningfully change how far you'll get on your own before hitting one of these three junctions.

## Comparing the Paths Through Each Junction

**At junction one**, the comparison is really "wait until a user finds the gap" versus "run a structured pre-launch checklist yourself." The second path is strictly better and costs nothing but a few focused hours — checking authentication versus authorization, testing payment edge cases, confirming your database is durable. Most founders who stall here simply didn't know this checklist existed, not that they lacked the discipline to run it.

**At junction two**, the comparison that actually matters is agency pricing versus scoped last-mile pricing, and it's not close. A traditional agency prices a rebuild because rebuilding is what agencies are set up to sell. A scoped engagement — reviewing what already exists and fixing specifically what's missing — costs a fraction of that because it's a smaller job, not because the same work is being done cheaper. LaunchStudio's pricing, at roughly 20% of a traditional agency's cost for comparable scope, exists specifically because the scope itself is different, not discounted.

**At junction three**, the comparison is generalist versus specialist, and it maps directly to speed. A generalist freelancer figuring out Bolt's or Cursor's output for the first time bills you for that learning curve. A team that reviews AI-generated codebases as a matter of routine skips it entirely, which is the practical reason last-mile engagements complete in one to three weeks rather than months.

Put side by side, the difference across all three junctions is less about any single price or timeline and more about predictability. A founder muscling through alone faces an open-ended timeline with no clear finish line. A founder chasing agency quotes faces sticker shock that stalls the decision entirely. A founder working with a specialist last-mile team gets a fixed number and a fixed date before committing to anything — which, for a founder who's already spent real time and money getting this far, is often worth more than the number itself.

## A Fourth Junction Founders Don't See Coming

There's a less obvious stall point worth naming, because it catches founders who've successfully navigated the first three: what happens after the fix is delivered. A founder who's spent weeks stuck at junctions one through three sometimes treats getting unstuck as the finish line itself, without a plan for what comes after launch — who monitors the app, who gets paged if something breaks, who keeps dependencies updated. This isn't really a fourth stall so much as a delayed version of junction one, and it's worth deciding upfront whether you want a one-time fix you'll maintain yourself going forward, or an ongoing arrangement that keeps someone else responsible for exactly that.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do 80% of AI-built projects never reach production?

Most stall at one of three predictable points: not realizing the prototype needs additional production work, freezing at an inflated rebuild quote, or failing to find someone who can work efficiently with AI-generated code specifically.

### Is a full rebuild ever actually necessary for software built with AI?

Rarely, if the core functionality already works. Most gaps are narrow and specific — security, payments, hosting — rather than requiring the entire product to be rebuilt from scratch.

### How do I know if my project is stuck at the pricing junction unnecessarily?

If a quote you've received assumes rebuilding your product rather than reviewing and fixing what already exists, it's likely priced for the wrong scope. A scoped review usually reveals a much narrower, cheaper fix.

### Does working with a specialist team actually save meaningful time over a generalist freelancer?

Yes, primarily because a specialist skips the time a generalist needs to first understand AI-generated code before safely changing it, which directly shortens delivery timelines.

### What's a realistic budget range for getting an AI-built SaaS prototype production-ready?

Most projects fall between €800 and €7,500 depending on scope, with straightforward fixes at the lower end and full payment, hosting, and security work at the higher end.
