## Myth: The Best AI Tool List Is the Only Research You Need

The assumption baked into most "best websites for AI" content is that your biggest risk is picking the wrong tool. Pick Cursor over Bolt, or v0 over Lovable, and supposedly your project is on the right track. In practice, the tool choice barely matters compared to what you do after the prototype works. Indie hackers who've shipped multiple projects know this instinctively — the tool gets you 60% of the way in a fraction of the time it used to take, but that remaining 40% (security, payments, deployment, monitoring) doesn't shrink just because the first 60% got faster.

## Myth: If the Demo Works, the Architecture Is Fine

A working demo and a sound architecture are not the same claim. AI coding tools are optimized to satisfy the prompt in front of them — get the login screen working, get the dashboard rendering, get the API call returning data. They are not optimized to ask whether your database schema will hold up under concurrent writes, whether your authentication flow leaks session tokens in the wrong place, or whether your error handling silently swallows failed transactions. Indie hackers with some coding background often catch some of this instinctively, but even experienced solo founders miss the parts of security and infrastructure that only show up under real production load — not in a local dev environment with one test account.

## Myth: More AI Tools Compound Into Better Outcomes

There's a temptation to stack tools — prototype in v0, refine in Cursor, deploy with whatever the latest AI-powered hosting assistant promises. Each tool individually is competent. Stacked together without someone reviewing the seams, they tend to compound small inconsistencies: one tool's assumptions about your data model don't match another's, and nobody catches the mismatch until a user hits it in production. More tools isn't automatically more coverage. It's often just more surface area for something to fall through unnoticed.

## Myth: Reading Enough Documentation Substitutes for a Real Review

Some technical solo founders assume that reading the AI tool's own documentation thoroughly enough will surface every gap themselves, without needing an outside review. Documentation, even good documentation, describes what a tool is capable of doing when configured correctly — it rarely audits what your specific implementation actually did. Two founders can read the exact same Bolt or Cursor documentation and end up with meaningfully different levels of security in their finished app, because the gap usually lives in the specific choices made during their particular build, not in a general capability the documentation failed to mention. This is why a codebase-specific review catches things that even careful documentation reading won't.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I actually look for in a "best websites for AI" resource?

Look for resources that go beyond comparing tool features and pricing, and that address what happens after your prototype works — security review, deployment, and cost of production-hardening. Most lists stop at the tool comparison and skip the harder, more expensive part.

### Is one AI coding tool objectively better than the others for indie hackers?

Not universally. Lovable, Bolt, Cursor, and v0 each suit different workflows — Cursor fits founders comfortable editing code directly, while Lovable and Bolt suit faster, more prompt-driven building. The bigger factor in outcomes is what happens after the prototype, not which tool you picked.

### Why does a working demo sometimes hide real architecture problems?

Demos are usually tested with one user account and light traffic, which hides issues like missing rate-limiting, weak authorization, or database schemas that don't hold up under concurrent access. Those only surface once real usage patterns hit the app.
