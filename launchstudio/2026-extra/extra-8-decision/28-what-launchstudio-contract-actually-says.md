Most founders sign a technical services contract the way most people sign a phone contract — skimming for the price and the start date, then trusting the rest is boilerplate — and the gap that creates rarely matters until a dispute or a change of plans makes a specific clause suddenly, urgently relevant. A contract for hardening an AI-built prototype covers a handful of provisions that actually matter to a founder's outcome far more than the length of the document suggests, and understanding them in plain language before signing anything is a better use of twenty minutes than most founders realize until they've been on the wrong end of a contract they didn't fully read.

## Scope of Work: The Section That Prevents the Most Common Dispute

The scope-of-work section defines, in specific terms, exactly what the engagement covers — which risk categories, which parts of the codebase, what "done" looks like for each item. This is the section responsible for preventing the single most common dispute in any technical engagement: disagreement over whether something was actually included. A vague scope section that says something like "general improvements to the backend" invites exactly this disagreement later, because "improvement" has no defined endpoint either party can point to. A well-written scope section, the kind that should appear in any engagement a founder signs, itemizes the specific findings from the scoping call and ties the fixed price directly to closing each one — which is also why the scoping process described elsewhere matters so much: a rushed scoping call produces a vague scope section, and a vague scope section is where later disagreements live.

## IP Ownership: Who Actually Owns the Code After Delivery

Intellectual property ownership is the clause founders most frequently assume rather than read, and the assumption is usually correct but shouldn't be taken on faith: a properly written engagement contract should explicitly state that the founder owns all code and work product delivered under the engagement, with no ongoing licensing fee, no retained rights by the engineering partner, and no dependency on continuing the relationship to keep using what was built. This matters enormously in practice — a founder who later switches engineering partners, brings work in-house, or simply wants to modify the code independently needs unambiguous ownership, not an assumption that turns out to be wrong the one time it's tested. A contract that's vague or silent on this point, rather than explicit, is a legitimate reason to ask for clarification before signing, regardless of which company issued it.

It's worth distinguishing this from a separate, narrower question that sometimes gets conflated with it: ownership of any general-purpose tools, internal libraries, or reusable methodology the engineering partner brings to every engagement, which typically remain the partner's own property even as the specific code written for a founder's product transfers fully. A well-drafted contract separates these two categories clearly, so a founder isn't left wondering whether a tool used during the engagement is something they now own or something the partner simply used to do the work.

## Confidentiality: Protecting What the Engineering Team Sees

A hardening engagement necessarily involves an outside team looking closely at a founder's codebase, business logic, and often sensitive operational details — which means confidentiality provisions aren't a formality, they're the mechanism protecting genuinely sensitive information from a party that, by the nature of the work, has to see quite a lot of it. A proper confidentiality clause covers both directions: what the engineering partner learns about the founder's business stays protected, and typically also covers what the founder learns about the partner's own internal methods or tooling. Founders evaluating any technical partner should expect a clear, mutual confidentiality provision as standard, not as a special request that has to be negotiated in.

## Liability and Warranty: What Happens If Something Still Goes Wrong

No engagement can honestly promise a perfect, guaranteed-forever result — software is complex, and new categories of risk emerge over time regardless of how thorough an initial hardening pass was. What a contract can and should specify is what happens if something covered by the engagement's specific scope turns out to be incomplete or incorrect shortly after delivery: is there a remediation period, a defined process for reporting and fixing issues traced back to the delivered work, and clear boundaries around what falls inside versus outside that coverage. This section is where a founder can distinguish a partner confident enough in their own work to stand behind it from one whose contract is silent specifically because they'd rather not commit to anything past the invoice being paid.

## Payment Terms and What Triggers Them

Payment terms specify not just the total price but the structure — whether payment is due upfront, split across milestones, or due on delivery — and what specifically constitutes each milestone being reached. This section should be legible enough that a founder can, at any point during the engagement, independently verify whether a payment trigger has actually occurred, rather than relying entirely on the engineering partner's own assertion that a milestone is complete. Clear, objective milestone definitions tied to the scope-of-work section protect both parties: the founder isn't asked to pay for something ambiguous, and the partner has a clear, defensible basis for each invoice.

Termination terms are the quieter cousin of payment terms, and worth reading alongside them: what happens if either party needs to end the engagement early, what's owed for work already completed, and whether the founder retains rights to any partial work product delivered up to that point. A contract silent on early termination leaves both sides guessing at exactly the moment guessing is least useful — mid-dispute, with unfinished work and an unclear financial position on both sides.

## Reading the Whole Document Once, Properly, Is Worth the Twenty Minutes

The practical takeaway isn't that founders need to become contract lawyers before hiring an engineering partner — it's that these five sections specifically are worth reading closely, once, before signing, because they're the sections that actually govern what happens when something doesn't go exactly as planned, which is precisely the situation a skimmed contract leaves a founder least prepared for. A partner willing to walk through these sections in plain language before a founder signs, rather than treating the contract as a formality to get past quickly, is generally signaling something useful about how the rest of the engagement will go.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does LaunchStudio's contract retain any rights to the code after delivery?

No — the contract explicitly transfers full ownership of all delivered code and work product to the founder, with no ongoing licensing fee or retained rights, as Sem's case confirms after his own prior experience with a vaguer contract.

### What happens if an issue traced back to LaunchStudio's work appears after delivery?

The contract specifies a defined remediation window for issues traced back to the engagement's specific scope, with a clear process for reporting and resolving them, rather than leaving that question unaddressed.

### Can I see a sample contract before committing to a scoping call?

Yes — founders can request to review contract terms in advance, and walking through sections like IP ownership, liability, and payment milestones in plain language before signing is standard practice, not a special accommodation.

### How are payment milestones defined, and can I verify them independently?

Payment milestones are tied directly to the specific findings and scope items identified during the scoping call, defined clearly enough that a founder can independently verify whether a milestone has actually been reached rather than relying solely on the partner's own assertion.

### Is confidentiality covered even if my codebase includes sensitive business logic?

Yes — a mutual confidentiality provision covering what the engineering team learns about the founder's business is standard in every engagement, not something that needs to be separately negotiated.
