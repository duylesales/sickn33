Someone at a demo night or a founder meetup offers to be your part-time CTO for five or ten percent equity, and the math looks generous — no invoice, no monthly retainer, just a slice of a company that doesn't exist yet in exchange for solving a problem you can't solve yourself. It's an appealing offer precisely because it feels free in the moment a non-technical founder is most anxious about cash. It rarely stays free, and understanding exactly what a part-time CTO paid in equity actually costs, compared with a fixed-price engineering engagement built for the same gap, is worth doing before signing away a piece of the company you haven't built yet.

## The Pitch That's Hard to Say No To

The part-time CTO offer solves a real and urgent problem: a non-technical founder with a vibe-coded prototype genuinely needs someone who understands infrastructure, security, and production risk, and doesn't have the cash to hire that expertise outright. Against that backdrop, "no cash required, just equity" sounds less like a trade and more like a rescue. What the pitch obscures is that equity isn't free money sitting unused in the founder's pocket — it's a permanent, compounding claim on every future round, every acquisition, every dollar of value the company ever creates, granted in exchange for what is very often a few hours a week of attention rather than the full-time technical leadership the word "CTO" implies.

## What Equity Actually Costs, Measured Honestly

Five percent sounds small at the seed stage, when the company is worth little on paper. It stops sounding small the first time a founder models it against a realistic exit or a later funding round, where that same five percent, diluted less than a founder expects because equity grants like this are rarely revisited downward, can represent a genuinely material sum — often far more than the same engineering work would have cost paid in cash, sometimes by an order of magnitude. The comparison that actually matters isn't "equity versus a big cash number I don't have." It's "equity versus a fixed, bounded cash number I can plan around," because a scoped engineering engagement has a ceiling. Equity, by construction, does not — it grows with the company for as long as the company exists, regardless of how much or how little ongoing work the equity holder actually does after the first few months.

## The Availability Problem No One Mentions in the Handshake

The other cost that rarely gets discussed at the demo night handshake is availability. A part-time CTO, almost by definition, has other commitments — another job, other advisory relationships, sometimes another startup of their own — and the hours they can actually give a founder's product are a small, often unpredictable fraction of what a founder pictures when they hear the title "CTO." Production issues don't wait for a convenient evening; a payment webhook failing at 2pm on a Tuesday needs attention at 2pm on a Tuesday, not whenever the part-time CTO's other obligations free up. Founders who go this route frequently describe a familiar pattern: fast, energetic attention in the first few weeks while the relationship is new, followed by slower response times as the equity holder's other priorities reassert themselves — with the equity grant, unlike the attention, never shrinking back down to match.

## What a Fractional CTO Is Actually Good At — and Where the Model Breaks

None of this means a part-time technical advisor is worthless. For strategic decisions — architecture direction, evaluating a full-time engineering hire, translating business goals into a technical roadmap — a few hours a month from an experienced person can be genuinely valuable, and equity is a reasonable way to compensate strategic input that doesn't require sustained hands-on availability. The model breaks specifically when a founder expects that same relationship to also cover hands-on production hardening: implementing row-level security, fixing authentication, setting up monitoring, responding to incidents. That's not strategic advisory work measured in hours per month; it's focused execution work measured in engineer-days, and pricing execution work in permanent equity is a mismatch that tends to become obvious to the founder only once the bill — paid in dilution rather than dollars — comes due.

## The Alternative: Buying the Outcome, Not the Title

A fixed-price engagement built specifically to hardening an existing AI-generated prototype solves the execution half of this problem directly, without touching company ownership at all. The founder isn't buying an ongoing relationship, a title, or a permanent stake in the business — they're buying a defined outcome: security, payments, and infrastructure brought to production standard within a scoped timeline, at a price agreed before work begins. When that outcome is delivered, the engagement is complete; there's no lingering equity claim on the next funding round, no ambiguity about how many hours per week the relationship is actually worth. For the specific, bounded problem most vibe-coding founders actually have — closing the gap between a working demo and a production-ready product — that's a closer match to the job than a permanent ownership stake exchanged for part-time, unpredictable attention.

## When a Part-Time CTO Still Makes Sense

None of this argues that equity-for-advice is always the wrong call. A founder who genuinely needs an ongoing strategic technical voice — someone helping decide when to hire a full-time engineer, how to structure a growing team, which architecture bets to make two years out — may find that a modest, carefully negotiated equity grant for real strategic involvement is worth it precisely because that kind of judgment compounds in value the way equity itself does. The mistake isn't offering equity for technical help in general; it's offering equity, sized for years of ongoing partnership, in exchange for what is actually a few weeks of focused, bounded hardening work that a fixed-price engagement was always the better-priced tool for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Isn't a part-time CTO for equity better than nothing if I have zero cash budget?

It depends what you actually need. For strategic advisory input, it can work well; for hands-on production hardening — the work most vibe-coding founders actually need first — a fixed, bounded price is often cheaper than equity once you model what that equity could realistically be worth later, as Noor's comparison shows.

### How much is startup equity actually worth if I give some away early?

It's impossible to know precisely, which is exactly the risk — equity has no ceiling and dilutes less predictably than founders expect, whereas a fixed-price engagement has a defined cost you can compare directly against a realistic valuation scenario before deciding.

### What should I ask a part-time CTO candidate before agreeing to equity?

Ask for a written scope of exactly what work the equity covers, how many hours per week they can realistically commit, and what happens if either side wants to end the arrangement — vague answers to any of these are worth treating as a signal.

### Does LaunchStudio ever work in exchange for equity instead of a fixed price?

No. LaunchStudio's engagements are fixed-price specifically so founders retain full ownership and know their total cost before committing, rather than trading an unbounded stake in the company for bounded, scoped engineering work.

### Can a fixed-price engagement and a part-time technical advisor coexist?

Yes, and this is common — many founders use a fixed-price engagement to close the immediate production gap, then bring in a part-time technical advisor separately for ongoing strategic guidance once the urgent hardening work is already done.
