"How can you quote me a fixed price before you've even opened my codebase?" is a fair question, and one nearly every founder asks at some point during their first scoping call with LaunchStudio, usually with a note of skepticism earned from past experience with hourly-billed engagements that quietly grew far beyond their original estimate. The honest answer is that the fixed price isn't a guess made in the dark — it's the output of a structured scoping process specifically designed to surface the categories of risk that matter before any commitment is made, and understanding how that process works explains both why the number is reliable and why the alternative, open-ended hourly billing, tends to reward exactly the wrong behavior in a technical engagement.

## What Actually Happens During Scoping, Before Any Number Is Given

The fixed price a founder receives isn't produced by a generic template applied to every project, nor is it a number pulled from a founder's stated budget — it follows a structured review of the specific codebase against a known, bounded set of risk categories: secrets and credential management, authentication and authorization enforcement, payment webhook handling, data isolation between users, error handling for external dependencies, and basic observability. Because AI coding tools like Lovable, Bolt, Cursor, and v0 tend to produce a consistent, recognizable pattern of gaps across these same categories, an engineer reviewing a new codebase against this checklist can identify with real precision which specific gaps exist and how much work closing each one actually requires, well before any code changes begin. This is fundamentally different from quoting an unfamiliar codebase with no structure to check it against — it's closer to a mechanic running a known diagnostic checklist against an engine than guessing at what might be wrong.

## Why Hourly Billing Quietly Rewards the Wrong Thing

Hourly billing isn't inherently dishonest, but it creates an incentive structure worth naming plainly: the person doing the work is paid more the longer the work takes, regardless of whether that additional time reflects genuine complexity or simply less efficient problem-solving. A founder on the other end of that arrangement has no reliable way to distinguish "this took longer because it was genuinely harder than expected" from "this took longer than it needed to," because they're not the one doing the work and usually can't independently verify the difference. This isn't necessarily bad faith on the part of any individual freelancer or agency — but the incentive exists whether or not anyone acts on it, and a founder bearing that risk with no visibility into which explanation is true is bearing a cost that a properly scoped fixed price removes entirely.

There's also a psychological cost to hourly billing that founders rarely name directly: it turns every status update into an anxious accounting exercise, where the founder is quietly tallying hours against expectations instead of evaluating whether the work itself is actually good. A fixed price removes that background calculation entirely, letting a founder evaluate the engagement on what's actually being delivered rather than running a parallel mental invoice throughout.

## What the Fixed Price Actually Transfers, and to Whom

A fixed price quoted after proper scoping shifts the risk of underestimation from the founder to LaunchStudio. If the scoping process missed something and the actual work takes longer than estimated, that's a cost absorbed by LaunchStudio, not passed back to the founder mid-engagement as a surprise invoice — which is precisely why the scoping process has to be thorough enough to make that a rare occurrence rather than an operating assumption. This is also why LaunchStudio's scoping call sometimes takes longer than a founder initially expects for what feels like it should be a quick conversation: a rushed scoping call produces an unreliable number, and an unreliable number is worse for both parties than an honest "we need another day to look closer before quoting this" — which does occasionally happen on genuinely unusual codebases, and is treated as a normal part of getting the number right rather than a failure of the process. A founder should generally be more reassured by a partner willing to say "we need to look closer" than one who quotes instantly on every codebase regardless of how unusual it turns out to be, since speed at the expense of accuracy simply relocates the risk from the quote back onto the founder later.

## Why the Price Scales With What's Actually Found, Not a Flat Rate

The fixed price isn't the same number for every project, and founders sometimes find that surprising given how confidently it's quoted after a single scoping call — but a flat, one-size-fits-all price would be a different kind of dishonesty, charging a founder with three straightforward gaps the same as a founder with a genuinely tangled authorization scheme across a dozen tables. The scoping process exists precisely to make the price reflect actual scope: LaunchStudio's package tiers, from Launch Ready through Enterprise Hardening, map to the real range of what different codebases need, and a founder is quoted into the tier that matches what the scoping call actually found, not steered toward whichever package is easiest to sell.

## What This Means for a Founder Deciding Whether to Commit

The practical upshot for a founder evaluating a fixed-price quote is that the number itself is diagnostic information, not just a bill — it reflects a specific, itemized assessment of what's actually wrong with a specific codebase, which a founder can and should ask to see broken down before committing to anything. A quote a founder can't get explained in plain language, mapped to specific findings, is a warning sign regardless of which company issued it; a quote that comes with a clear list of what was found and why each item costs what it costs is what a properly scoped fixed price is supposed to look like, and it's reasonable for a founder to expect exactly that level of transparency before signing anything.

It's also reasonable to ask what happens if the founder wants to add scope mid-engagement — a new requirement surfaces, or a stakeholder asks for something beyond the original findings. A properly structured fixed-price engagement handles this cleanly: additional scope gets its own itemized addition to the price, agreed before the work starts, rather than either being absorbed silently or triggering an ambiguous renegotiation of the original number. That clarity, upfront, about how changes get handled is itself part of what a founder should expect from a properly scoped quote.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can a fixed price be accurate without first doing the full engineering work?

The price follows a structured scoping review against a known, bounded set of risk categories that AI-generated codebases consistently share, letting an engineer identify specific gaps and their scope with real precision before any code changes begin, as Naomi's itemized quote illustrates.

### What happens if the actual work ends up being harder than the scoping call estimated?

That risk is absorbed by LaunchStudio, not passed back to the founder as a surprise invoice mid-engagement — which is part of why the scoping process is thorough enough to make underestimation a rare exception rather than a routine occurrence.

### Why does the price differ between projects if the process is the same for everyone?

The scoping process is consistent, but what it finds isn't — a codebase with three contained gaps costs less to fix than one with a dozen interconnected issues, and the fixed price reflects that specific scope rather than a flat, one-size-fits-all rate.

### Can I see exactly what the quote is based on before agreeing to anything?

Yes — a properly scoped fixed-price quote should come with a specific, itemized list of findings, as it did for Naomi, and a founder should reasonably expect that level of transparency from any vendor issuing a fixed-price number.

### How long does the scoping call itself typically take?

Most scoping calls take under an hour, though LaunchStudio will occasionally ask for a bit more time on an unusually complex or unfamiliar codebase before finalizing a number, rather than rush to a figure that turns out to be unreliable.
