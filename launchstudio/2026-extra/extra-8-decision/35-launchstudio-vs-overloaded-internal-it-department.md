"Just hand it to IT" sounds like the responsible move when a business unit inside a growing company has quietly built a working prototype with an AI coding tool and now needs it made production-safe. In practice, at most scale-up companies, that sentence sends the project into a queue behind an ERP migration, three security patches, and whatever the CEO's latest priority happens to be — and it can sit there for months, not because anyone doubts the project matters, but because "matters" and "matters more than everything already in the queue" are different bars, and internal IT teams are almost always juggling the second one, not the first. Recognizing that distinction early is what separates a business unit that stays stuck for a quarter from one that finds a workable path within weeks, without ever having to escalate the disagreement above the IT director's head.

## The Queue Problem, Not the Competence Problem

Internal IT and platform teams at growing companies are rarely under-skilled — they're under-capacity, which is a completely different problem with a completely different fix. A team already responsible for keeping existing systems running, managing security patches across the company's actual production infrastructure, and supporting every other department's tooling requests doesn't have idle bandwidth waiting for a new prototype to show up. When one does, it gets triaged against everything else already on the board, and a prototype from one business unit — however promising — rarely outranks an incident affecting the whole company or a compliance deadline with a hard date attached. This isn't a failure of the IT team's judgment. It's the correct application of scarce capacity against competing demands, and it produces exactly the outcome founders inside these companies keep running into: a reasonable request that nonetheless waits.

## What Gets Lost While the Prototype Waits

Every week a hardening request sits in an internal queue is a week the business unit that built the prototype can't move forward on whatever it was for — a client pilot that needed a safe demo environment, an internal tool meant to replace a manual process bleeding hours weekly, a new revenue line waiting on a working product. Momentum inside a company decays in a way that's structurally similar to momentum with an external customer: stakeholders who were excited about a working prototype three months ago start asking why it still isn't live, budget approved for a specific initiative starts looking questionable if nothing visible has shipped, and the team that built the original prototype quietly reallocates their own attention elsewhere because waiting on IT isn't a task, it's limbo. None of this shows up as a formal cost anywhere, which is exactly why it's so easy for an organization to underweight it against the very real, very visible cost of pulling internal engineers off other priorities. A useful comparison: nobody schedules a meeting to formally weigh "three more months of a manual process costing the operations team six hours a week" against "the two-day cost of pulling one engineer from the ERP migration," because the second cost shows up on a resourcing spreadsheet and the first one doesn't show up anywhere at all, even though it's frequently larger by the time it's actually totaled up.

## Why a Fixed-Scope Outside Engagement Skips the Queue Entirely

An external, fixed-scope hardening engagement doesn't compete for the same capacity internal IT is rationing, because it isn't drawn from the same pool of hours — it's a separate, bounded project with its own start date, defined by exactly what it will touch and what it won't, running in parallel to whatever the internal team is already doing rather than waiting for a slot to open up in their existing workload. This is the structural reason an outside engagement can move in one to three weeks when an internal request might sit for a quarter: it was never actually competing on the same list. The prototype gets hardened, the internal team's existing priorities stay exactly as prioritized as they already were, and nobody has to make the case internally for why this particular project deserves to jump the queue ahead of a security patch or a platform migration.

## This Isn't a Bypass of Internal IT — It's a Complement to It

A well-scoped outside engagement doesn't sideline the internal team or create the kind of shadow IT sprawl that makes CTOs nervous — the opposite, done properly. A clear handoff document, a defined scope agreed with internal stakeholders before work starts, and a final state that internal IT can inherit and monitor going forward means the business unit gets unblocked without internal engineering losing visibility into what now exists in their environment. The concern IT departments raise about outside help isn't usually "we don't want help" — it's "we don't want an unmonitored, undocumented system appearing in our stack that we're accountable for but don't understand." That concern is legitimate, and it's addressed directly by scoping the engagement transparently and handing off cleanly, rather than by avoiding outside help altogether and letting the prototype sit in a queue instead.

## Recognizing When You're in This Situation

The signal that a business unit is stuck in exactly this pattern is usually a specific kind of vague answer from IT: not "no," and not a date, but "it's on the list," repeated across several check-ins with no movement in between. If a working prototype has been sitting in that state for more than a few weeks, the honest read is rarely that the request lacks merit — it's that it's genuinely, correctly, lower priority than everything already committed, and no amount of internal follow-up is likely to change that ranking on its own. That's the moment a parallel, fixed-scope path becomes worth raising, not as a criticism of the internal team, but as a practical way to stop waiting on a queue that was never going to move faster on its own. Raising it framed that way — as an addition to the internal team's capacity rather than a bypass of their judgment — tends to land far better in practice than founders expect, because most IT leads are relieved to have a business unit's need addressed without it costing them any of the hours they don't have to spare.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Won't bringing in outside help make my internal IT team feel bypassed?

Not when the engagement is scoped transparently with IT informed in advance, as in Reinier's case — the concern IT teams actually raise is usually about unmonitored systems appearing in their environment, which a clear scope and clean handoff addresses directly rather than avoids.

### How do I know if my prototype is genuinely stuck versus just early in a reasonable queue?

A specific pattern is the signal: repeated check-ins that return "it's on the list" with no date and no movement over several weeks. A few weeks of normal queueing is different from months of the same vague answer with the queue never actually moving.

### Does this create shadow IT that my company will regret later?

Properly done, no — a documented, monitored handoff that internal IT reviews and inherits is the opposite of shadow IT, which by definition lacks that visibility and documentation in the first place.

### Will my internal IT team need to approve the engagement before it starts?

That's the recommended approach, and it's what protected Reinier's rollout — defining scope with IT informed upfront means the final system is something they can confidently take ownership of, not a surprise they discover later.

### How much internal engineering time does a parallel engagement actually require?

Very little on an ongoing basis — the engagement runs independently, with internal involvement mainly at the scoping and handoff stages, which is precisely what allows it to move without pulling hours from whatever the internal team is already prioritizing.
