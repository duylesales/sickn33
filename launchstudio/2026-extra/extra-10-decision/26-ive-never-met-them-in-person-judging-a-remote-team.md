It's 11 PM, three days before you're supposed to sign an engagement letter, and you're re-reading the same email thread for the fourth time. The team seems competent. The proposal is specific. The price is fair. And you still haven't shaken the feeling that you're about to hand your product — and access to your customers' data — to people you've only ever seen in a video call window, based in a city you've never visited, whose office you will in all likelihood never walk into.

That instinct isn't paranoia, and it shouldn't be talked out of you with reassurance alone. It should be answered with verification. The honest position isn't "trust remote teams because plenty of good work happens remotely" — true, but useless on its own. It's "here is exactly what you can check, today, before you send anything, that tells you more than a video call ever will."

## Why "Never Met in Person" Is the Wrong Thing to Be Worried About

Start by separating the actual risk from the feeling. The feeling is triggered by physical absence — no office to visit, no handshake, no local reputation you can ask around about at a networking event. But physical presence has never reliably predicted competence or honesty; it's simply the verification method humans defaulted to before better ones existed. A local freelancer with a nice office and a firm handshake can still ghost you at week six. A team on another continent with a documented process, verifiable references, and a public track record can be considerably more reliable than either.

The actual risks worth worrying about are specific and checkable: does this team have a real, ongoing legal existence, not just a website; can their claimed track record be independently verified rather than taken on their word; do they have a documented process for exactly the kind of work you need; and is there a contract that survives the relationship going wrong. None of these require meeting anyone in person. All of them require more than reading testimonials on the team's own site.

## Verification Step One: The Legal and Corporate Trail

Ask for the legal entity name behind the team you're talking to, not just the brand name on the website — and check it. A registered company with a Chamber of Commerce number (KvK number in the Netherlands, or the equivalent business register in whatever jurisdiction they claim), a real registered address, and a history that predates your conversation by years is a fundamentally different proposition than a brand that appeared six months ago with no corporate footprint behind it.

This check takes under ten minutes and tells you something a video call cannot: whether there's an actual legal entity that can be held to a contract, sued if necessary, and that has skin in the game beyond this one project. A team that hesitates to give you this information, or gives you a name that doesn't match anything findable, has told you something important before you've spent a euro.

Go one layer deeper if the engagement is significant: check how long that entity has existed, not just whether it exists. A company registered eighteen months ago with a slick website can still be a perfectly good team — plenty of legitimate businesses are young — but it changes what "track record" can honestly mean. A company with a registration history stretching back a decade, verifiable through the same public register, has weathered enough project cycles, staff changes, and difficult clients to have a track record in the fuller sense of the word, not just a portfolio page. Neither age alone proves competence, but a young entity claiming eleven years of unspecified "team experience" is worth a direct follow-up question about which entity actually did that work, and whether it's the one you'd be contracting with.

## Verification Step Two: References You Actually Call

Testimonials on a website are marketing copy, written to be read, not to be checked. A reference is different: a real person, contactable independently, who will tell you things that don't appear on the website — including the awkward parts. Ask specifically for one or two references from projects similar in size and shape to yours, not their single flagship enterprise case study, and then actually call them rather than exchanging emails, because tone and hesitation carry information that text doesn't.

Ask the reference concrete questions: did the delivered work match what was originally scoped, or did the scope quietly shrink along the way? Was communication during the project responsive, or did it lag when something went wrong? If something did go wrong, how was it handled — fixed without argument, argued over, or ignored? Would they hire this team again for a similarly sized project, and why or why not? A reference who answers fluently and specifically is worth far more than five polished testimonials you can't verify.

## Verification Step Three: Test the Process Before You Commit to It

Before signing anything substantial, most legitimate development partners will walk you through their actual process on a call — how a project gets scoped, what the contract looks like, how access to your code and infrastructure is requested and structured, what a typical week of communication looks like. Pay attention not just to what they say, but to whether it's specific. "We use an agile process and communicate regularly" is filler. "You'll get a written scope document before we start, a staging environment you can check at any point, and a status update every Monday and Thursday" is a process you can actually verify against later, because it makes concrete promises you can hold them to.

If it's feasible for your situation, a small, low-stakes paid pilot — a single scoped task, priced and time-boxed — is a better verification tool than any amount of conversation. It tests communication quality, delivery speed, and whether the invoice matches what was agreed, all against real stakes but a contained downside. Not every engagement size makes a pilot practical, but where it does, it answers "can I trust this team" with evidence instead of impression.

Pay attention, too, to how a team responds to your verification questions themselves — this is a live test running in parallel with everything else. A team that answers a direct question about their legal entity, their process, or their contract terms promptly and specifically is demonstrating the same communication behavior you'd be relying on mid-project when something needs a quick, clear answer. A team that becomes vague, slow, or mildly defensive when asked to substantiate a claim is showing you, for free and before you've committed anything, exactly how they'll behave the first time a real problem needs the same kind of direct answer.

## Verification Step Four: Read the Contract for What It Doesn't Say

A contract's absences matter as much as its contents when you're assessing a team you can't casually drop in on. Look specifically for what happens if the relationship ends early — does the contract clearly state that you retain full access to your code and infrastructure regardless of why the engagement ends, or is that left ambiguous? Look for who owns the intellectual property produced during the work — it should say, explicitly and unambiguously, that you do, from the moment it's created, not upon final payment or some other condition. Look for a data processing agreement or equivalent if the team will have access to customer data at any point, particularly relevant under GDPR if you or your users are in the EU.

A contract that's vague on these points isn't necessarily malicious, but vagueness is exactly the terrain where "never met them in person" turns from an uneasy feeling into an actual problem, because there's no informal local relationship to fall back on if something goes wrong. The contract is the relationship, in a remote engagement — treat its specificity as the primary signal, not a formality to skim past to get to the price.

## What Geography Can Still Tell You (and What It Can't)

To be fair to the instinct, geography isn't entirely irrelevant — it's just not predictive the way people assume. Time zone overlap affects communication speed in a real, practical way: a team eight hours ahead of you will naturally have less real-time overlap than one two hours ahead, and that's worth factoring into your expectations for response time, not as a red flag but as an operational fact to plan around. Jurisdiction matters for contract enforceability — a contract governed by a legal system you understand, or at least one your own lawyer can meaningfully review, is worth asking for specifically.

What geography doesn't reliably tell you is quality or trustworthiness. Manifera's own delivery model — European client relationships out of an Amsterdam office, with development centers in Ho Chi Minh City and a hub in Singapore — is itself a working example of a genuinely distributed, cross-border team producing work for enterprise clients including Vodafone and TNO, verified through exactly the mechanisms above: registered entities, checkable track record, and contracts that hold up regardless of which office is doing the work on a given day. Distance is a logistics variable to plan around, not a trust variable to resolve by proximity.

## The Actual Checklist, in Order

Compressed into something you can run through in under an hour before a call: verify the legal entity exists and matches what's claimed. Request two references sized similarly to your project and call them, not just email them. Ask for their process in specific, checkable terms, not general reassurance. Read the contract specifically for code ownership, data handling, and exit terms. If the size of the engagement allows it, propose a small paid pilot before the full commitment. None of these require being in the same room, the same country, or even the same time zone as the people doing the work — and all of them tell you more than a handshake would.

## Real example

### A Founder Who Verified Before She Trusted

Marit Andersen had a working nutrition-coaching app built in Bolt and three competing quotes from teams she'd found through different channels — one local, two remote. The local option felt safest by instinct alone, despite being the most expensive and the least specific about process. Before deciding, she ran the checklist above against all three.

The local team's legal registration checked out, but neither reference she called had worked with them on anything resembling her project's scope, and the contract was silent on data ownership after the engagement ended. One remote team's corporate registration didn't match the name on their invoice template, which ended that conversation in under a day. The second remote team's references were specific, checkable, and enthusiastic about exactly the kind of access-control and payment work Marit needed, and their contract explicitly stated she retained all code and infrastructure access regardless of how the engagement concluded.

**Result:** Marit chose the verified remote team over the locally reassuring but less-substantiated option, closed the engagement in nine business days, and specifically credits the reference calls — not the video call impressions — with the decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the single fastest check if I only have time for one?
Call a reference from a similarly sized project and ask what went wrong and how it was handled. A team with nothing to hide will have an answer; a team that can't produce a checkable reference at all has told you something important on its own.

### Is it reasonable to ask for a video call with the actual engineers, not just a sales contact?
Yes, and a legitimate team should accommodate it without resistance. Talking to the person who will actually touch your code, even briefly, surfaces technical specificity that a sales-oriented conversation often doesn't.

### How do I check a company's legal registration if I don't know the local system?
Most EU countries have a public business register searchable online — the Dutch KvK register, for example, is free and searchable in English. Ask the team directly for their registration number; a legitimate one will provide it without hesitation.

### Should time zone difference alone be a dealbreaker?
No, but it should shape your expectations. A larger time zone gap means less real-time overlap and more asynchronous communication, which works fine for many engagements if both sides plan for it explicitly rather than assuming instant responses.

### What if a remote team refuses a small paid pilot before a bigger engagement?
It's not automatically disqualifying — some engagements are too small or too tightly scoped to split sensibly — but ask why, specifically, and weigh the answer alongside the other checks. A reasonable explanation plus strong references and a solid contract can still add up to a trustworthy picture.
