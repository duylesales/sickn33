Which is worth more to you right now: someone who can make your Lovable app do three new things this week, or someone who can make it survive its first hundred real users?

Those are two different people. The industry has started calling the first a Lovable expert or a vibe coding developer, and the second is what most of us used to just call a developer. Both are legitimate. Hiring the wrong one for your moment is how founders end up spending a budget twice, and the distinction is not about talent or tooling — it is about what each of them optimises for.

## What Each One Is Actually Good At

**A tool specialist optimises for velocity inside the builder.** They know how Lovable structures a project, how to prompt it into producing something close to what you described, where it tends to make a mess, and how to unpick that mess without breaking the app. Give them a Friday and a feature idea and you will have the feature by Monday.

**A general production developer optimises for behaviour under stress.** They think about what happens when two people submit at the same time, when someone edits a URL, when the payment provider times out mid-transaction, when a table has a hundred thousand rows instead of forty. They will be slower at adding your feature and vastly better at making sure it does not lose data.

Neither of them automatically has the other's skills, and the market currently prices them similarly, which is why founders assume they are interchangeable.

## What a Lovable Expert Genuinely Knows That Others Do Not

It would be unfair to treat tool expertise as trivial. There is real, non-obvious knowledge here.

How a project is structured and where generated code hides assumptions. Which prompts produce maintainable output and which produce something that works once. How to make changes without the tool undoing them on the next generation. Where the platform's own limits sit, and which problems are the tool's rather than yours. How to keep a codebase in a state where the builder can still edit it after a human has been in there — which is the thing most traditional developers destroy without realising it matters.

That last point is worth paying for on its own if you intend to keep building the product yourself.

## What a General Developer Brings That the Specialist May Not

Everything that only becomes visible with real users: access control that holds when someone probes it, transactions that do not half-complete, indexes so a page still loads at scale, monitoring, backups that have been restored once, and the instinct to ask what happens when this fails rather than assuming it will not.

There is also the reading skill. A production developer's core competence is inheriting a system they did not design and forming an accurate model of it. That is precisely what your AI-built app requires, and it is the opposite of what a rebuild-minded specialist offers.

## Four Scenarios, Four Clear Answers

**You have a demo, no users, and three features missing before anyone will pay.** Hire the tool specialist. Your risk right now is not scaling; it is never reaching a product anyone wants. Speed inside the builder is exactly the right investment.

**You have a working product and your first paying customers are two weeks away.** Hire the production developer. Features can wait; the access rules, the payment reconciliation and the hosting cannot. This is the moment founders most often get wrong, because adding features feels like progress and hardening feels like delay.

**You are already live and something is quietly wrong** — emails not arriving, performance degrading, a customer seeing another customer's data. Production developer, immediately, and preferably one who will diagnose before proposing.

**You are an agency with a client who arrived holding a prototype.** You need production capacity you can put your own name on, and probably not a new permanent hire for one engagement.

## The Rebuild Conversation, Which Is Really a Budget Conversation

The most expensive sentence in this market is "it would be quicker to start again".

Sometimes it is true. A prototype built around a fundamentally wrong data model, or one where the same logic is copied into fourteen places, can genuinely be harder to repair than to redo. That is a minority of cases.

More often, a rebuild is proposed because reading someone else's code is unpleasant and writing your own is not, and because a rebuild converts an ambiguous repair job into a familiar greenfield project. The cost to you is severe: you pay again for what you already have, you wait months instead of weeks, you lose the ability to edit your own app in the tool you know, and you carry the risk that the new version reintroduces problems the old one had already solved.

Before agreeing, ask for the specific list of what cannot be fixed in place. If the list is short, you are being sold a preference.

## The Continuity Question Nobody Asks Until Afterwards

Ask any candidate, specialist or generalist: after you finish, can I still edit this in Lovable?

The answer shapes your next twelve months. If yes, you retain the ability to ship small changes yourself at no cost, which is the whole reason you built it this way. If no, every future change becomes a purchase order, and you have swapped a cheap iteration loop for an expensive one without being told that was part of the deal.

Good production work leaves the codebase documented, conventional and AI-readable. That is a deliberate choice by the engineer, not an automatic outcome — so it belongs in the brief.

## How to Decide in Five Minutes

Write down what breaks first if you do nothing for three months.

If the answer is "nobody uses it because it lacks features", hire velocity. If the answer is "someone's data leaks, or payments fail, or the site falls over", hire production. If the answer is "my client walks", get a partner who can deliver under your name.

Then hire for that answer only, and revisit in a quarter. Founders get into trouble by hiring the person they enjoyed talking to rather than the person who addresses the thing currently most likely to sink the product.

## What Belongs in the Brief, Whichever You Hire

The same brief works for both types of candidate, and writing it is the step that most improves your outcome — partly because it clarifies your own thinking and partly because it filters applicants before you speak to anyone.

**State the outcome, not the activity.** Not "improve security" but "no authenticated user can read another user's records, verified by attempting it." Outcomes can be tested; activities can only be invoiced.

**List what must not change.** The interface, the tool you want to keep editing in, the data you cannot lose. This single line prevents the rebuild conversation from arriving disguised as progress.

**Name the deadline and what it is tied to.** "Before our pilot with three clinics on 14 October" tells a candidate far more than "as soon as possible", and it lets honest people decline rather than overpromise.

**Say what happens afterwards.** Who maintains it, whether you intend to keep building yourself, whether you want a support arrangement. Engineers make different structural choices depending on the answer, and the difference is expensive to retrofit.

**Include the constraints you find embarrassing.** A small budget, a tool nobody rates, a prototype you know is messy. Candidates who react badly to those facts would have reacted badly later, at a worse moment.

**Ask for a fixed price against the outcome list.** If a candidate cannot price it, the scope is not yet clear enough — which is information about your brief rather than about them.

Five or six lines is enough. The brief is not a specification; it is a filter and a shared definition of "done", and its absence is the most common reason these engagements end in disappointment on both sides.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a "vibe coding developer" a real specialism?

It describes a real skill set — fluency with AI builders, prompt-driven iteration, and keeping a generated codebase editable. It is genuinely useful for building fast. It is not the same skill set as production engineering, and problems arise when the two are treated as interchangeable.

### Can one person do both?

Some can, and they are worth keeping. The way to find out is to ask a candidate how they would test whether your database is currently exposed, and separately how they would add a feature without breaking future edits in Lovable. Confident answers to both are rare.

### My freelancer says my prototype must be rebuilt. Is that true?

Sometimes, usually not. Ask for the specific list of what cannot be repaired in place and what the rebuild buys you. A short or vague list means you are paying again for what you already own.

### As an agency, does white-label delivery mean losing the client relationship?

No — that is the point of the arrangement. The client stays yours, the work is delivered under your brand and under NDA, and the engineering capacity appears only when a project requires it.

### How do I know which type I need right now?

Ask what breaks first if nothing changes for three months. Missing features point to a tool specialist; leaking data, failing payments or outages point to production engineering.
