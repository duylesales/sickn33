Every comparison of these tools measures the wrong thing. Feature tables, benchmark prompts and side-by-side demos answer "which is more capable", and capability is not what determines whether you finish a product.

What determines that is fit: whether the tool matches how you think about your idea, how much code you can read, and what you intend to do afterwards. A capable tool that fights your way of working produces an abandoned project; a slightly less capable one that matches it produces something you launch.

## The Question to Answer First

Before comparing anything: **can you read code?** Not write it — read it, and tell whether something looks wrong.

This single answer splits the four tools into two groups, and getting it wrong is the most common source of frustration. Someone who cannot read code working in an editor-based tool spends their time confused; someone who can, working in a prompt-only builder, spends their time fighting for control they could have had directly.

Be honest rather than aspirational. "I did a bit of HTML years ago" is a no for this purpose.

## Lovable: For the Founder Who Thinks in Screens

If you picture your product as a sequence of screens — this page, then this form, then this dashboard — Lovable matches that model closely. You describe screens and behaviour, it produces them, and the backend is wired up as a consequence rather than as a separate exercise.

**It suits** non-technical founders, people who want a working product rather than a codebase, and anyone whose idea is clearer as an interface than as a system.

**It leaves you** with more backend wired up than the alternatives, and with the specific risk that this feels finished when the access rules underneath have never been configured.

**Choose it if** your answer to the reading question was no and your product is a recognisable application: records, forms, lists, roles.

## Bolt: For the Founder Who Wants Something Tidy Early

Bolt produces a conventional, readable codebase quickly. It is close to Lovable in accessibility and different in what it leaves behind.

**It suits** founders who expect to hand the project to a developer before long, technical people who want a scaffold rather than a finished thing, and anyone who cares about what the code looks like even if they cannot read it.

**It leaves you** with a cleaner starting point and typically less of the backend connected — a data layer sketched rather than built.

**Choose it if** you want a product now and an engineer later, and you would rather that engineer inherit something tidy.

## Cursor: For Someone Who Can Read Code

Cursor is an editor. It assumes you are working in files, reviewing changes and making decisions about structure. For someone with that ability, it is the most powerful of the four by a distance, because nothing sits between you and the project.

**It suits** technical founders, developers building outside their usual stack, and anyone who wants AI assistance without giving up control.

**It leaves you** with a codebase shaped by your own decisions, which is an advantage when those decisions are good and a liability when they are inconsistent — which is why written conventions matter more here than anywhere else.

**Choose it if** you answered yes to the reading question, and accept that you are responsible for coherence over time.

## Replit: For Someone Who Needs It Running Now

Replit's distinguishing property is that everything works immediately: environment, execution, a URL, collaboration, no setup.

**It suits** people learning, teams working together on something quickly, anyone demonstrating an idea to others this week, and situations where getting something in front of a person matters more than what happens in month six.

**It leaves you** closest to "live" and furthest from "portable", because the conveniences that removed setup are also the things you cannot take with you.

**Choose it if** speed to something shareable is the constraint, and accept that a migration is likely once real customers arrive.

## What All Four Leave You

The same list, in different proportions: access rules, credential handling, payments that reconcile, deployment with rollback, backups that have been tested, monitoring, and the adversarial layer nobody prompted for.

This is the point that makes the tool comparison less important than it appears. Whichever you choose, the gap between a working prototype and a launchable product is roughly the same shape, and closing it is the same work.

## Switching Costs, Honestly

Moving between these tools generally means regenerating rather than porting. Your second version inherits none of the fixes applied to your first, and founders who move twice frequently end up with three partial products.

The one move that is genuinely cheap is from a prompt-based builder into Cursor, because you are moving from generating code to editing the code you already have. That is a progression rather than a switch, and it is the natural path for a founder who becomes more technical as the product grows.

Moving because a tool is frustrating is rarely worth it. Moving because you can name a specific thing it cannot do is.

## Using Two Deliberately

The most effective arrangement for many founders is not one tool but two with different jobs.

**Lovable or Bolt for the interface,** where describing screens is faster than writing them, and **Cursor for the backend and anything structural,** where control matters more than speed. This works provided the codebase stays conventional and the two do not undo each other, which in practice means agreeing where each is allowed to operate.

**Replit for demonstrations and experiments,** kept separate from the product itself.

Founders who do this report that the tools stop competing and start covering different halves of the work.

## A Five-Minute Self-Test

- Can you read code well enough to tell when something looks wrong? If no, rule out Cursor as a primary tool.
- Is your idea clearer as screens or as a system? Screens point to Lovable; a system points to Bolt or Cursor.
- Will someone else work on this within six months? If yes, weight code tidiness and portability.
- Do you need something shareable this week? That is Replit's case.
- Do you intend to keep building yourself after an engineer touches it? Then require a conventional, AI-readable codebase whatever you choose.

Five answers, and the choice usually makes itself.

## If You Have Already Switched Twice

Plenty of founders read this having already moved between tools, with two or three partial products and a suspicion that they are the problem. They are usually not, and the way out is the same regardless of how you arrived.

**Stop and pick the most complete version,** not the newest. The one closest to something a person could use is the one worth finishing, even if it was built in the tool you liked least.

**Write down what is missing from it** — features, not infrastructure. Six lines. This is your remaining product work.

**Choose the tool that fits the self-test above** and commit to it for that list. Not forever; just until the list is done.

**Do not regenerate what already works.** The temptation with a fresh tool is to start again because the beginning is the pleasant part. Starting again is why there are three partial products rather than one finished one.

**Then close the production gap once,** on whichever version you finished.

The pattern behind repeated switching is almost always that the friction was product complexity rather than tool limitation — the hard part arrived, and a new tool offered the feeling of progress without the difficulty. Recognising that is usually enough to break it.

## Why the Habits Matter More Than the Tool

Founders who finish products share a small set of habits, and none of them are tool-specific. Adopting them matters considerably more than which of the four you chose.

**They ship something small to a real person early.** Not a demo — something used. It reorders every subsequent priority, because real use exposes what matters and what never did.

**They read what the tool produced,** at least enough to notice when something looks unfamiliar. This is the habit that separates people who direct a tool from people who hope.

**They write decisions down.** Which database, which region, why that structure. Three lines each, kept in the project. It costs minutes and is the only defence against a codebase nobody can reason about, including a future model.

**They test as someone unfriendly.** Editing a URL, submitting a value the form did not intend, clicking twice. Ten minutes, and it finds the class of problem that no amount of building reveals.

**They stop building at the right moment.** There is a point where the next feature matters less than the fact that the product cannot safely take a customer, and recognising it is the difference between launching and iterating indefinitely.

Any of the four tools plus those five habits produces a launched product. The best tool without them produces a very good prototype.

## Real example

### A Founder Who Switched Three Times and Finished on the Fourth

Lotte Gerritsen spent five months trying to build Klantboek, a simple client-record tool for independent therapists around Utrecht. She started in Replit because a friend recommended it, moved to Cursor after reading that serious developers used it, gave up on Cursor because she could not tell whether the code it produced was correct, and moved to Bolt.

Three regenerations, none finished, and a growing conviction that she was not capable of building software.

The conversation that changed it was a question rather than advice: could she read code well enough to spot a mistake? No, clearly not — which meant Cursor had never been a sensible choice and the two weeks lost there were not a reflection of her ability.

She rebuilt once more in Lovable, which matched how she described the product — a list of clients, a session form, a notes screen, a simple report — and had a working version in nine days. The backend came wired, which was exactly what she needed and exactly the thing she could not have assembled herself.

Then she stopped and had the production work done rather than continuing to iterate: access rules so therapists could not see each other's clients, session notes classified and encrypted, hosting on her own domain, backups tested, and monitoring.

**Result:** Klantboek launched with fourteen therapists eleven weeks after she first started, and Lotte has added four features herself in Lovable since.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which AI builder is best?

The wrong question. The useful one is which fits how you think and what you can read: Lovable if you picture screens and cannot read code, Bolt if you want tidy output early, Cursor if you can read code, Replit if you need something shareable this week.

### Should I switch tools if I am frustrated?

Only if you can name the specific capability the current tool lacks. Switching regenerates rather than ports, so accumulated fixes are lost, and unnamed frustration usually follows you to the next tool.

### Can I use two tools together?

Yes, and many founders do: a prompt-based builder for the interface and Cursor for structural work, with Replit kept for demonstrations. It requires the codebase to stay conventional and an agreement about where each tool operates.

### Does the tool I choose change what production work costs?

Only marginally. Cost follows how much backend exists, whether money is involved and how much data you hold. The gap between a prototype and a launchable product is roughly the same shape whichever tool produced it.

### I cannot read code. Is Cursor unusable for me?

As a primary tool, largely yes — it assumes you can review what it writes. That is a poor fit rather than a personal failing, and a prompt-based builder will get you considerably further.
