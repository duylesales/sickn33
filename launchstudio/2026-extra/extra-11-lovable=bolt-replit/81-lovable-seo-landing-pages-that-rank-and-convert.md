There is a widespread belief that ranking and converting pull in opposite directions — that search wants long text and buyers want a short page with a button, so you must choose which visitor to disappoint.

That belief made sense fifteen years ago, when ranking was about word count. It does not now. What ranks is a page that satisfies the person who searched, and a page that satisfies the person who searched is, by definition, a page that works. The tension people experience is almost always a symptom of a different problem: one page attempting to serve four different intentions at once.

## One Intent Per Page

A visitor arrives with one of four things in mind, and each wants a different page.

**They have a problem and do not know solutions exist.** They want an answer to their question, and they will not buy today.

**They want a tool for a known task.** They want to know what your product does, what it costs, and whether it fits.

**They are comparing you with an alternative.** They want an honest comparison, including where you are weaker.

**They already know you.** They want to sign up without reading anything.

The generic "solutions" page tries to serve all four, ranks for none of them, and converts poorly because every visitor has to dig past three-quarters of the page that was not written for them. Four pages, each honest about its audience, outperform one page every time — and they are easier to write, because each has a single job.

## What the Page Must Do in the First Screen

Whatever the intent, the top of the page answers three questions immediately.

**What is this?** In the words your visitor uses, not your category name. "Record toolbox meetings and prove they happened" beats "safety compliance platform".

**Is it for me?** Name the audience concretely — for installation firms, for physiotherapy practices, for webshops shipping furniture. Specificity does not shrink your market; it makes the right visitor recognise themselves, and a visitor who recognises themselves reads on.

**What do I do next?** One primary action. Not five.

If those three are not answered above the fold, the rest of the page is irrelevant, because most visitors decide within a few seconds whether this page is about their situation.

## The Middle: Evidence Rather Than Adjectives

Everything between the top and the action is doing one job — removing the reasons not to act.

**Concrete before abstract.** A screenshot of the thing doing the thing outperforms a paragraph describing it. A two-line example of a real workflow outperforms a feature list.

**Answer the objections you actually hear.** The recurring hesitations from your sales conversations belong on the page, phrased as they are said to you: how long does setup take, does it work with the system we already have, what happens to our data if we stop, is it worth it for six people.

**Proof appropriate to your size.** Not invented logos. A named customer with a specific outcome, a real number from your own product, a short quote with a name and a town. Dutch business buyers respond strongly to a recognisable local reference and are quick to discount vague claims.

**Price, or a genuine reason there is none.** Hiding pricing costs more visitors than any price does. If it is genuinely variable, publish the range and what moves it.

## Why Thin Pages Fail Both Goals

The page with a headline, three icons and a button is the most common AI-generated marketing page, and it fails twice.

It fails for search because there is nothing on it to match a query beyond your product name. And it fails for conversion because every question a hesitant buyer has is unanswered — so they leave to find the answer and do not return.

The fix is not padding. It is adding the specific things a real buyer needs: what it does, for whom, what it costs, what it integrates with, what happens to their data, how long it takes to start, and who already uses it. That page is naturally long enough to rank, because it is genuinely useful.

## The Dutch Specifics

For a product sold in the Netherlands, four details matter more than their size suggests.

**Write in Dutch for Dutch buyers,** in their register, not translated from your English page. A translated page reads as a foreign supplier, which is a disadvantage in exactly the sectors where trust decides.

**Show real business identifiers.** A KvK number, a physical address, a VAT number, a phone number that is answered. These are read as proof that you exist, and their absence is noticed.

**Be explicit about data location** if you serve professional or public-sector buyers. "Data stored in the EU" on a landing page pre-empts a question that otherwise arrives as a spreadsheet.

**Match the payment and contract expectations of your market** — invoicing for business customers, clear notice periods, no automatic annual lock-in. Saying so on the page removes hesitation you never hear about.

## Measure the Right Two Things

Impressions tell you whether the page matched the search. Click-through tells you whether the title and description earned the visit. Conversion tells you whether the page delivered on the promise.

A page with many impressions and few clicks has a title problem — half an hour of work. A page with many clicks and no conversions has a mismatch between what the search wanted and what the page offers — usually because it targets a phrase with a different intent from the page's content. A page with no impressions has a technical or a keyword problem, which is a different article.

Diagnose in that order. Most founders rewrite the whole page when the actual fault was a title, or add testimonials when the real issue was that the page never appeared at all.

## Testing a Page When You Have No Traffic

The advice to test everything assumes thousands of visitors a month. At ninety, a split test will never reach a conclusion, and waiting for one means never improving anything.

What works at small scale is different and, in some ways, better.

**Ask five customers to read it.** Not "does this look good" — which produces politeness — but three specific questions: what do you think this does, who is it for, and what would stop you signing up. Five people, twenty minutes each, and the answers are more useful than a month of statistics. The third question in particular produces the objections your page should already be answering.

**Do the five-second test.** Show someone the top of the page for five seconds, take it away, and ask what it was. If they cannot say what it does and who it is for, no amount of persuasion further down will matter.

**Watch a real person use it.** One prospect, screen shared, narrating. Where they hesitate is where the page is unclear. This is the single highest-value hour available to a small product and almost nobody spends it.

**Use the search console instead of an A/B test.** It reports impressions and click-through per page and per query without needing volume. Change a title, wait a fortnight, compare. That is a real experiment, and at low traffic it is the only one available.

**Change one thing at a time.** With small numbers you cannot attribute anything if you rewrite the whole page. Headline, then proof, then the action. Slower, and it actually teaches you something.

**Read your own enquiries.** Every question that arrives by email is a hole in the page. A product answering the same question fifteen times has a page that should have answered it once.

The uncomfortable truth underneath all of this is that a page converting nobody at ninety visitors will also convert nobody at nine hundred. Fixing clarity first and driving traffic second is the cheaper order, and it is the opposite of what most founders do.

## Real example

### One Page for Everybody, Converting Nobody

Nienke Haverkamp built Praktijkruimte with Lovable: a service for renting treatment rooms by the hour to physiotherapists, psychologists and dietitians, with 40 rooms listed across Nijmegen, Arnhem and Wijchen.

Her site had one marketing page. It carried a headline about flexible workspace, three icons, a short paragraph and a sign-up button. It ranked for her company name and nothing else, and of the visitors it did receive, very few went further.

The page was trying to address three different audiences at once — therapists looking for a room, building owners with rooms to list, and practices wanting to sublet spare capacity — and said almost nothing concrete to any of them.

Ten business days of work: the public pages moved to server-rendered HTML with per-page metadata after it emerged that nothing beyond the home page had ever been indexed; then five pages built for five intents, written directly in Dutch — renting a treatment room by the hour for therapists, listing a room for building owners, subletting spare hours for practices, a comparison against a long-term lease including an honest section on when a lease is cheaper, and a page answering what the insurance and hygiene requirements are for a shared treatment room, which was the question her customers actually searched; each page given a concrete headline naming the audience, screenshots of the booking flow, hourly prices published, a named reference with a town, KvK and VAT details in the footer, and a statement that data is stored in the EU; structured data and a sitemap added.

**Result:** after four months the five pages produced around 1,400 visitors a month from search against roughly 90 before. The requirements page became the highest-traffic page on the site and the source of most therapist sign-ups. The honest comparison against a long-term lease converted at more than twice the rate of the sign-up page, which Nienke had assumed would be the best performer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do ranking and converting really conflict?

No. What ranks is a page that satisfies the person who searched, which is also what converts. The tension people feel usually comes from one page trying to serve several different intentions at once.

### How many landing pages should a small product have?

One per intent rather than one for everything: a problem page for people who do not know solutions exist, a product page for people looking for a tool, a comparison page, and a direct sign-up path. Each is easier to write because it has one job.

### Why does my short, clean page not work?

Because it answers nothing. A headline, three icons and a button gives a search engine nothing to match and a hesitant buyer no reason to decide. Add what a real buyer needs — price, audience, integrations, data handling, setup time, proof — and the length takes care of itself.

### What do Dutch buyers specifically look for?

A page written in Dutch rather than translated, real business identifiers such as KvK and VAT numbers and a physical address, clarity about where data is stored, and contract terms that match local expectations.

### My page ranks but nobody clicks. What is wrong?

The title and description, not the page. Many impressions with few clicks is a promise problem and is half an hour of work. Many clicks with no conversions means the page targets a phrase whose intent it does not serve.
