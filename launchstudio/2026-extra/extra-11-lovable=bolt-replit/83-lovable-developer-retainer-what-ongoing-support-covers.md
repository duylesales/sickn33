The launch is the part everybody plans for. What nobody plans for is the eighteen months afterwards, when the product is live, customers depend on it, and the person who built it has moved on to their next project.

Software does not sit still even when you do. Dependencies release security updates. Platform providers change behaviour and deprecate things. Certificates renew or fail. A third-party API you rely on publishes a new version and retires the old one. Customers find edge cases nobody anticipated. And your own business changes, which means the product has to.

A retainer is how a small company buys the certainty that somebody is watching. It is also, done badly, a monthly payment for nothing — so it is worth understanding what you are actually buying.

## What Maintenance Actually Consists Of

Four different activities that get bundled under one word.

**Keeping the lights on.** Monitoring, certificate renewals, backups verified rather than assumed, dependency updates, platform changes absorbed. Invisible when it works, which is why it is the first thing people question paying for.

**Fixing what breaks.** Errors in production, integrations that stop, data problems. Unpredictable in timing, bounded in effort.

**Answering questions.** From you and sometimes from your customers: why did this email not arrive, what happened to this record, can we export this.

**Making changes.** New features, adjustments, improvements. This is the category that expands to fill whatever hours exist, and the reason retainers need a boundary.

A good arrangement names all four and says which are covered. A vague one covers "support", and the argument arrives in month three when you ask for a feature and receive an invoice.

## The Common Structures

**Managed hosting with included maintenance.** A fixed monthly fee covering infrastructure, monitoring, backups, security updates and small fixes. LaunchStudio's version is €49 per month. Predictable, and deliberately excludes new development.

**An hours-based retainer.** A number of hours per month at an agreed rate, used for fixes or changes as you choose. Ask two questions before signing: do unused hours carry over, and what happens when you exceed them.

**On-call availability.** You pay to be able to reach somebody quickly. Suitable when the product is operationally critical, and it costs real money because it constrains somebody's life.

**Ad hoc, no retainer.** You call when something breaks and pay for the work. Cheapest until the week your developer is on holiday and your payment integration is down.

Most small products are best served by the first plus the fourth: a low fixed fee for the invisible work, and separate arrangements for changes.

## What Response Times Actually Mean

The number in a proposal is worth reading carefully, because two things are being conflated.

**Response time** is how long until someone acknowledges and begins. **Resolution time** is how long until it is fixed. Almost every agreement commits to the first and almost no founder notices.

Then ask what "four hours" means. Four working hours on a Tuesday is very different from four hours on a Sunday morning. Unless the arrangement explicitly covers outside office hours, assume it does not, and decide whether that matters — for a product used by Dutch businesses during the working day, it frequently does not, which saves you money.

Finally, ask what happens if the commitment is missed. An agreement with no consequence is a statement of intention. That is not necessarily bad from a small supplier, but you should know which you have bought.

## What a Retainer Should Include as Standard

Six things. If they are absent, the arrangement is thinner than it looks.

**Monitoring with alerts,** so a problem is detected by your supplier rather than reported by your customer.

**Backup verification,** not merely backups existing — a restore rehearsed periodically and timed.

**Security updates applied,** including dependency updates, on a stated cadence.

**A monthly summary.** What happened, what was updated, what is degrading. Two paragraphs, and it is the artefact that makes the fee visible.

**Access that survives absence.** More than one person able to reach the system, or a documented handover. A one-person supplier on holiday is a single point of failure you have paid to accept.

**Documentation kept current.** A runbook that matches reality, so any competent developer could take over.

## When a Retainer Is the Wrong Answer

Three cases worth being honest about.

**Your product is genuinely static and simple.** A brochure site with a contact form does not need a monthly relationship. Managed hosting plus occasional work is enough.

**You are changing constantly.** If you need substantial development every month, a retainer sized for maintenance will be exhausted in week one and you will argue about it. Contract for the development separately.

**You have technical capacity in-house.** Then what you want is access to expertise for specific problems, not a standing arrangement.

The signal that a retainer is right: the product is operationally important to paying customers, you cannot fix it yourself, and a day of downtime would cost more than a year of the fee.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing

What exactly is included, and what is billed separately? Response time or resolution time, during which hours? Who else can access the system if you are unavailable? Will I receive a monthly report? Is there a minimum term and a notice period? What happens to my accounts and code if we stop? And do unused hours carry over?

The last two matter most. A supplier who answers the accounts question crisply — everything is in your name, you can leave in a week — is telling you something about how they operate.

## Judging the Value After Six Months

A retainer that looks like a fee for nothing is either working perfectly or doing nothing, and those are hard to distinguish from outside.

The monthly summary is what separates them. It should show updates applied, incidents detected and handled, backups verified, and anything degrading. If six months of summaries contain nothing but "no issues", ask what was updated and when the last restore was tested. A supplier doing the work will answer immediately.

## What a Year Actually Contains

Founders question the fee because nothing appears to happen. It helps to see what a typical year looks like for a small live product, because the events are individually small and collectively unavoidable.

**Thirty to sixty dependency updates,** of which two or three address published security vulnerabilities and one will require a code change because the library altered its behaviour.

**One or two platform changes you did not choose.** A provider deprecating an API version, changing a default, retiring a feature, or altering its pricing model. Each arrives with a deadline in an email that is easy to miss.

**At least one certificate event.** Usually automatic, occasionally not — a subdomain added later, a domain moved, a renewal that failed silently.

**One integration that stops working.** A payment provider's new API version, an email service tightening authentication requirements, a partner changing a field name without telling anyone.

**One silent failure.** A scheduled job that stopped, an upload path that has been failing for a subset of files, a notification nobody received. These are found by monitoring or by a customer, and the difference in cost between those two discoveries is the entire argument for the arrangement.

**Two or three customer questions that require looking at data.** Why did this not send, what happened to this record, can we get an export.

**One genuine incident,** somewhere between an hour and a day, where something is actually broken and the question is how quickly it is noticed and how confidently it is restored.

None of these is dramatic. Together they are roughly the workload a small maintenance arrangement is priced for — and the reason a product left entirely alone for a year is never in the state its owner believes it is in. The eleven-month-old unpatched vulnerability and the nightly email that stopped in April are not unusual findings; they are the normal result of nobody being responsible.

It had been built with Lovable and taken to production by a freelancer who then became unavailable — not dramatically, just increasingly slow to reply. Erik had no arrangement in place because the product had been working.

Three things surfaced within a fortnight. A dependency used for image processing had a published security vulnerability, unpatched for eleven months, and tenants uploaded photographs of damage. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a maintenance retainer actually cover?

Four distinct things that often get bundled: keeping the lights on, fixing what breaks, answering questions, and making changes. A good agreement names all four and says which are included; a vague one says "support" and produces an argument in month three.

### Is response time the same as resolution time?

No, and almost every agreement commits only to response — how long until somebody acknowledges and begins. Also check which hours are covered, since four working hours on Tuesday differs from four hours on a Sunday.

### What should be included as standard?

Monitoring with alerts, verified backups with rehearsed restores, security and dependency updates on a stated cadence, a monthly summary, access that survives one person's absence, and documentation kept current.

### When do I not need a retainer?

When the product is simple and static, when you need substantial development every month and should contract for that separately, or when you have technical capacity in-house and only need occasional expertise.

### How do I tell whether I am getting value?

From the monthly summary. Updates applied, incidents detected and handled, backups verified, anything degrading. If six months of reports say only "no issues", ask what was updated and when a restore was last tested.
