A client asks you to take their half-built Lovable app to production. You estimate four days. With the tooling you now use, it takes you eleven hours.

At your hourly rate that is a small invoice for work that saved the client from a data breach and unlocked a contract they had been chasing for a quarter. You have been paid for your typing speed rather than for knowing which eleven hours mattered.

This is the central problem with billing hours in a field where the hours collapsed and the value did not. It is also, for a freelancer or small studio, entirely solvable — but not by raising your hourly rate.

## Why Hourly Billing Punishes Speed

Three ways it works against you.

**The better you get, the less you earn.** Experience, good tooling and a library of solved problems all reduce hours. Every improvement you make to your own practice reduces your income for the same delivered outcome.

**The client watches the wrong number.** Hours become the subject of conversation, so questions are about duration rather than result. "Why did that take six hours?" is not a question anybody benefits from answering.

**It transfers your expertise for free.** Knowing which three of thirty possible problems actually matter for this client is the thing you are worth. It takes an hour to apply and years to acquire, and an hourly invoice values it at one hour.

The honest version of this is that hourly billing prices your time, and what clients buy is a changed situation.

## Price the Outcome, Not the Effort

The alternative is a fixed price attached to a defined result.

Think about what the work is worth to them. A production readiness engagement that lets a small SaaS close an enterprise contract is worth a multiple of the days it consumes. A security fix that prevents a reportable breach is worth more than the afternoon it takes. A launch that happens six weeks earlier is worth six weeks of revenue.

None of that means charging whatever you like. It means the price lives between your cost of delivery and the value received, and that window is usually far wider than an hourly rate suggests.

Two sanity checks keep you honest. Would you be happy to do this work at this price if it took twice as long? And does the price look reasonable beside what the client's alternative costs — an agency quote, a full-time hire, or the cost of the problem continuing?

## Productise the Work You Do Repeatedly

The step that changes a freelance practice.

After a dozen engagements, you notice you are doing the same work: an assessment, an access-control fix, a production readiness pass, a handover. Give each a name, a defined list of what is included, a fixed price and a stated duration.

The effect on sales is immediate. A prospect comparing three freelancers who each offer "development, hourly" cannot distinguish them, and defaults to price. A prospect reading "Production Readiness: access rules, secrets, backups, monitoring, deployment, documented, five working days, fixed price" has been given a decision they can actually make.

## Scoping Well Enough to Quote Fixed

Fixed prices fail when the scope is vague, so the scoping conversation becomes the skill.

**Assess before you quote.** Two to four hours looking at the actual code and infrastructure, and charge for it. A paid assessment filters people who were never going to buy, gives you the information to quote accurately, and is frequently valuable enough on its own that the client would pay for nothing else.

**Write down what is not included.** The exclusions protect the price. No new features. No design changes. No mobile app. Dutch only. One integration, named.

**Define done per item.** "Access rules written and verified by attempting to bypass them from a second account" can be checked. "Improve security" cannot.

**Quote a range only where a genuine unknown exists,** and name the unknown: if the data turns out to be in this shape it is the lower figure, if that shape the higher, and here is how we find out in the first two hours.

**Price changes separately, in advance.** A short note, a price, an effect on the timeline. Most losses on fixed-price work are unmanaged changes rather than bad estimates.

## Where Fixed-Price Work Actually Loses Money

Five recurring causes, all predictable.

**Existing data that turns out to be a mess.** The migration is never the problem; the six years of inconsistent records are.

**Third-party systems you cannot control.** An integration with a client's ageing internal system can consume a week waiting for someone else's IT department. Make this an explicit exclusion or a time-boxed allowance.

**A client who cannot make decisions.** Two weeks of delay while three people disagree about a workflow. Name the decisions you need and when, in the proposal.

**Scope that arrives as bug reports.** "It should also handle recurring bookings" is a feature, not a defect, however it is phrased. Handle it politely and in writing, every time.

**Your own optimism.** Estimate the work, then ask what happens if the access rules are worse than they look. On AI-built projects they usually are, and the fifth engagement teaches you to price that in.

## Retainers Alongside Projects

Project work is lumpy. A retainer is what makes a freelance practice survivable.

After a delivery, offer ongoing maintenance: monitoring, updates, backups verified, small fixes, a monthly note. Modest per client, predictable, and it keeps you close to the products you know — which is also where the next project comes from, because you are the person who notices what needs doing next.

Keep development out of it. A maintenance retainer that quietly becomes a development contract is how freelancers end up working for a fraction of their rate and resenting a client who has done nothing wrong.

## Talking About Price Without Discomfort

Three habits worth adopting.

**Say the number plainly,** without softening it. Hesitation invites negotiation.

**Explain what it includes, not how long it takes.** If the duration comes up, give a delivery date rather than an hour count.

**When pushed on price, reduce scope rather than the number.** "For €2,400 I can do the access rules and the backups, and we can look at monitoring next month" protects your rate and gives the client a real choice. Discounting teaches them that your first number was not serious.

## Getting Paid, Which Is a Separate Skill

Pricing well and collecting reliably are different problems, and the second is what determines whether a freelance practice survives a slow quarter.

**Take a deposit.** A third up front on any fixed-price engagement is normal, expected, and filters clients who were never going to pay. A prospect who objects to a deposit is showing you something useful.

**Invoice at milestones, not at the end.** A five-day engagement invoiced entirely on completion means a month between starting work and seeing money. Deposit, midpoint, completion converts the same engagement into a manageable rhythm.

**Agree the payment term explicitly and put it on the invoice.** In the Netherlands 30 days is customary for business-to-business work, statutory limits apply to how long a term can be, and late payment carries statutory interest. You do not need to threaten anybody; stating the term and the consequence on the invoice does most of the work.

**Chase early and without embarrassment.** A friendly note on day 31 is administration. A note on day 75 is a conflict. Most late payment in small business is inattention rather than intent, and the people who pay late pay promptly when asked promptly.

**Keep a reserve.** Project income arrives in lumps. Three months of costs held aside is what lets you decline work you should decline, which is itself one of the strongest influences on what you can charge.

**Get the administrative basics right.** Correct invoices with sequential numbers, the right VAT treatment for your situation including reverse-charge arrangements where they apply, and records retained for the period Dutch law requires. Confirm the specifics with an accountant rather than guessing — this is an area where the cost of being wrong is entirely avoidable and the advice is cheap.

A practice that quotes fixed prices, invoices in stages and follows up on time is treated differently by clients than one that does not, and the difference shows up in what those clients accept without negotiating.

## Real example

### Eleven Hours of Work, €340 Invoiced

Sem Bruggeman works as a freelance vibe coding developer in Groningen, taking Lovable and Bolt projects to production for founders and small agencies. In his first year he billed hourly at €55.

The engagement that changed his pricing was a founder with a booking product for driving schools. Eleven hours of work: access rules that had allowed any logged-in user to read every booking, a service key removed from the frontend, backups configured and a restore tested, and a payment webhook given signature verification. He invoiced €605 and, after a discount he offered without being asked, collected €340.

Six weeks later the founder told him the fixed access rules had been the deciding item in a contract with a chain of eleven driving schools.

He spent a weekend restructuring. Three named packages replaced hourly billing: a paid assessment at €250 credited against any engagement that followed; a production readiness package at €2,200 covering access rules, secrets, backups with a tested restore, monitoring and a written handover, delivered in five working days; and a larger scale-up package at €5,500 for multi-tenancy, integrations and API work. Maintenance was offered separately at €65 per month. Exclusions were written down. Changes were quoted in writing before starting.

The first three prospects after the change accepted without negotiating the price. Two asked for the assessment first, and both proceeded.

**Result:** over the following eight months Sem delivered fourteen fixed-price engagements averaging €2,400 against an average of just under four working days, and holds nine maintenance clients. His revenue roughly tripled on slightly fewer working hours, and he reports that conversations with prospects are now about outcomes rather than durations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is hourly billing a problem for AI-assisted development?

Because the hours collapsed and the value did not. Every improvement in your tooling or experience reduces your income for the same result, and it prices your time rather than the changed situation the client is actually buying.

### How do I set a fixed price without guessing?

Assess first, and charge for the assessment. Two to four hours with the real code and infrastructure gives you what you need to quote accurately, filters unserious prospects, and is often valuable enough on its own.

### What must a fixed-price proposal contain?

The included items with a verifiable definition of done for each, an explicit list of exclusions, a delivery date, the price, and a written process for changes with their own price and timeline impact.

### Where does fixed-price work usually lose money?

Messy existing data, third-party systems outside your control, clients who cannot make decisions, features arriving disguised as bug reports, and your own optimism about how bad the access rules will be.

### What do I do when a client pushes back on price?

Reduce scope, not the number. Offering a smaller package at a lower price protects your rate and gives a real choice; discounting tells the client your first number was not serious.
