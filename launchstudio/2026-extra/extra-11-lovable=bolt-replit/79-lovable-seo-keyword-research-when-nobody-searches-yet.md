Most SEO advice assumes a market that already searches. Pick your category, check the volume, compare the competition, publish. That works for accounting software and running shoes.

It does not work for the product you built, because you built something that did not exist, and the phrase you use for it — the one on your home page, the one you say at networking events — is a phrase nobody types. Volume tools report zero. You conclude search is not for you, and you are wrong, because your customers are searching constantly. They are searching for the problem.

## The Category Trap

A founder builds a tool that helps construction crews document safety briefings, calls it safety-briefing software, and optimises for that.

Meanwhile the site manager with the actual problem is typing things like how to prove a toolbox meeting took place, what must be recorded for a safety inspection, or whether a photograph is sufficient evidence. He has no idea that software for this exists. He is not looking for a product; he is looking for a way out of a problem, and he will meet a product only if one has written something useful about it.

That gap is the whole opportunity. Category terms are searched by people comparing suppliers — valuable, scarce, and contested by everyone with a marketing budget. Problem terms are searched by everyone who has the problem, which is a much larger group nobody is serving.

## Where the Real Phrases Come From

Not from a tool. From people.

**Your own customers.** Ask the ones you have: what were you doing before, and what did you search when you looked for a better way. The answers are rarely what you expected and are frequently phrased in a way that makes you wince.

**Your support inbox.** Every question a customer asks is a phrase somebody else typed into a search engine. This is the highest-quality source you own and almost nobody mines it.

**Sales conversations.** The objection that comes up every time is a page. "How is this different from a spreadsheet" is a real query with real volume in most categories.

**Forums and communities where your customers complain.** Dutch trade associations, sector groups, regional business networks. Read how people describe the problem in their own words and write those words down verbatim.

**The search box on your own site,** if you have one. People tell you exactly what they expected to find.

**Search suggestions and related searches.** Type the problem, in Dutch, and read what the engine offers. That list is derived from what people actually type, which is precisely what the volume tools underreport for small niches.

## Intent Matters More Than Volume

Sort everything you have found into four kinds.

**Problem-aware:** "how do I prove X", "what are the rules for Y". High volume, early, and the foundation of everything.

**Solution-aware:** "software for X", "app to do Y". Smaller, closer to buying.

**Comparison:** "X versus Y", "alternative to Z". Small and extremely valuable.

**Branded:** your name. Trivial volume, essential to own.

A page serving the first kind brings people who did not know you existed. A page serving the third brings people about to decide. Small products should write in the order: one branded page, two or three comparison pages, then problem pages steadily — because the comparison pages convert while the problem pages accumulate.

## Dutch Phrasing Is Not a Translation

For a product sold in the Netherlands, this deserves its own attention.

Dutch professionals search in Dutch for their problem, in a register that is usually blunter and more concrete than the English equivalent. They frequently search in English for technology, which means a bilingual product needs different content per language rather than the same content twice.

Translating your English keywords is the reliable way to produce phrases nobody types. Find the Dutch phrasing from Dutch sources — your own customers, trade forums, search suggestions typed in Dutch — and write the page in that language rather than translating into it. A page written directly in Dutch about a Dutch regulation, referring to the actual rule by its actual name, will outrank a translated general page indefinitely.

Regional and sector specificity is an advantage here too. A small product can rank for "for installation companies in Gelderland" in a way it will never rank for the generic term, and the visitor arriving from that search is far more likely to buy.

## Zero Volume Is Not Zero Value

Volume tools are unreliable at the small end. They round down, they miss long phrases, and they underreport Dutch-language niches badly.

More importantly, ten visitors a month searching an exact problem you solve are worth more than a thousand searching something adjacent. For a product at €40 per month, one converted visitor per month is €480 a year from a page you wrote once.

So the test is not "is the volume high". It is: does somebody with this problem become a customer, and can I be the best answer on the internet for this exact question. For narrow professional problems, being the best answer is achievable in an afternoon, because the current best answer is usually a forum post from 2019.

## One Page, One Question

The structural rule that makes the rest work.

Each page answers one question completely. A page trying to cover five related questions ranks for none of them; five pages, each answering one, rank for five. And a page that answers its question fully — including the part where the honest answer is "you may not need software for this" — earns the trust that makes the next step possible.

This also happens to be what makes a page quotable by AI answer engines, which increasingly mediate this kind of research. Direct answers, specific facts, named regulations, clear attribution.

## Keeping It Honest

Two failures worth avoiding.

**Writing for the engine rather than the reader.** Repetitive phrasing, thin pages built around a term. It reads badly to humans and is no longer effective mechanically.

**Answering a question you do not actually know the answer to.** Particularly with regulations, where a confident wrong answer damages trust precisely among the professional readers you want. Where the rule is genuinely situation-dependent, say so and point at the authority.

## Turning Research Into a Plan

The output should be a simple list: twenty to thirty phrases, each with its intent, each mapped to one page, ordered by how close the searcher is to needing you. Then publish steadily — one page a fortnight beats ten in a week and nothing afterwards — and after three months check which pages produce impressions, which produce clicks, and which produce customers.

## Writing the Page So the Research Pays Off

A correct keyword attached to a bad page produces impressions and no customers. Six things separate a page that earns its place from one that merely exists.

**Answer in the first paragraph.** Somebody searching "does a photograph count as proof" wants a yes, a no, or an it-depends within two sentences. Making them read four paragraphs of context first sends them back to the results, which the engine notices.

**Write the title as a promise and the description as the reason to click.** The title carries the phrase people typed; the description tells them what they will get. These two lines determine whether ranking turns into visits, and they take ten minutes to write properly.

**Use the words your readers use, including the unglamorous ones.** If site managers say "toolbox" rather than "safety briefing", the page says toolbox. Writing up-register is the most common way a well-researched page fails.

**Be specific enough to be quotable.** Name the rule, give the number, state the circumstance. Specificity is what makes a page useful to a reader and what makes it citable by an answer engine, which increasingly decides whether your page is seen at all.

**Link between your pages deliberately.** A reader who arrived at a problem page should find the comparison page, and vice versa. Internal links also tell a crawler which of your pages matter and how they relate, which is free and almost universally neglected.

**Say what your product does at the end, once, honestly.** Not a sales page wearing an article's clothes. A reader who got a real answer and then learns you built something for exactly this problem converts at a rate no amount of persuasion achieves.

Then revisit. A page written two years ago about a rule that changed is worse than no page, and updating an existing page that already ranks is consistently cheaper than writing a new one — which is why a small maintained set beats a large abandoned one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What if my product category has no search volume?

Then your customers are searching for the problem rather than the category. Category terms are searched by people already comparing suppliers; problem terms are searched by everyone who has the problem, which is a far larger and less contested group.

### Where do I find the phrases people actually use?

From your own customers, your support inbox, recurring sales objections, forums where your customers complain, your site's search box, and the engine's own suggestions typed in the language your customers use.

### Can I translate my English keywords into Dutch?

That reliably produces phrases nobody types. Dutch professionals search in a blunter, more concrete register, and often in English for technology. Find Dutch phrasing from Dutch sources and write the page in Dutch rather than translating into it.

### Is a keyword with ten searches a month worth a page?

Frequently, yes. Ten people with the exact problem you solve are worth more than a thousand with an adjacent one, and being the best answer on the internet for a narrow professional question is genuinely achievable.

### How many pages should I publish, and how fast?

One page per question, published steadily — one a fortnight sustained beats ten in a week followed by nothing. Start with one branded page and two or three comparison pages, then work through problem questions by intent.
