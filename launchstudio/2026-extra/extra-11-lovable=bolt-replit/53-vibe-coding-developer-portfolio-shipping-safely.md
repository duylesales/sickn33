A portfolio of AI-built projects has a particular problem: everything in it looks impressive and none of it proves anything. Twelve screenshots of twelve polished interfaces demonstrate that you can produce polished interfaces quickly, which the client already assumed, and say nothing about the question they are actually nervous about.

That question is rarely spoken aloud. It is: *if I give this person my product, will it still work when a hundred strangers use it, and will I find out before my customers do?*

Everything useful in a portfolio for this kind of work is an answer to that.

## What Clients Are Actually Assessing

Three things, in order.

**Can you build what I described?** The easy one. Screenshots settle it in ten seconds, which is why they are necessary and insufficient.

**Does it hold up?** Whether anything you built has met real users, real money and real load, and what happened.

**Will I be able to continue without you?** Whether what you leave behind is legible to the next person, including a future AI tool.

Most portfolios answer the first and ignore the other two. The candidate who answers all three is rare enough that the answer itself is the differentiator.

## Show the Runtime, Not the Repository

The most persuasive material is evidence that something you built ran.

**Numbers, however modest.** "Used by nine clinics, around 400 bookings a month, live for fourteen months" tells a client more than any description of features. Small numbers are fine; the point is that real people used it continuously.

**Something that broke, and what you did.** This is counter-intuitive and it is the single most convincing item you can include. "The booking endpoint oversold a workshop under simultaneous requests; I moved the capacity check into an atomic database operation, added a load test to the pipeline, and it has not recurred in eleven months." Nobody with production experience believes a portfolio without an incident in it.

**Before and after, with a metric.** Page load, error rate, cost, support volume. One number moving is worth a paragraph of adjectives.

**A decision you made and why.** "Kept the generated frontend and rebuilt only the data layer, because the client needed to keep editing in Lovable." Reasoning demonstrates judgement in a way that outcomes alone do not.

## What to Include Per Project

Keep it to six lines and the same six every time.

What it does and who uses it. What you built versus what already existed — clients value honesty here more than they value credit. Which tools were used. What you did about the production concerns: access control, payments, deployment, backups. Something that went wrong. And what the client could do afterwards without you.

That last line is the one that closes engagements. A client reading "she left it documented and I added two features myself in Lovable the following month" has heard the answer to their real fear.

## The Security Section Nobody Has

If you work with AI-generated code, a short section on how you handle security is a genuine differentiator, because almost nobody in this market has one.

It does not need to be sophisticated. Four sentences describing your standard practice: that you enable and test row-level access rules per table, that privileged keys never appear in client code, that you validate server-side, and that you verify by attempting to bypass your own work rather than by assuming.

Clients cannot evaluate your code. They can absolutely evaluate whether you have a method — and having one stated puts you ahead of most of the market for AI-built work, where the honest answer is that nobody checked.

## Talking About AI Tooling Honestly

Two failure modes, both common.

**Hiding it.** Clients find out, and the discovery reads as concealment. It is also unnecessary: speed is a selling point.

**Leading with it.** A portfolio whose headline is the tool rather than the outcome invites the client to assume the tool did the work, which makes you a commodity.

The formulation that works places the tool where it belongs: you build quickly with AI tooling, you know where that approach stops, and you do the production work deliberately. That sentence is accurate, it does not apologise, and it pre-empts the question a serious client will otherwise ask in the interview.

## What to Leave Out

Projects that never had users, unless labelled as explorations. Screenshots without context. Tool logos as a substitute for capability. Claims about security you cannot describe a method for. And client work you cannot name — better to describe it anonymously with detail than to list a logo with nothing attached.

Volume works against you here. Four projects described properly outperform twelve listed, because the reader is looking for depth of evidence rather than breadth of activity.

## If You Have No Production Experience Yet

The honest starting position for many people, and it is solvable in weeks rather than years.

**Take one thing you built and actually launch it.** A small tool with ten real users teaches you — and lets you describe — more than ten prototypes. Put it on a domain, add monitoring, take a backup and restore it, and handle the first bug in production.

**Harden something existing, and document it.** Take your own prototype, run the access-control test, fix what you find, and write up what you did. That write-up is portfolio material precisely because it shows method.

**Work as a second pair of hands** on a live product for someone who has one, even briefly. Production experience transfers the moment you have seen one incident handled properly.

**Publish the checklist you work to.** A public, short list of what you do before calling something launch-ready demonstrates the same thing a case study does, and you can write it today.

## How This Changes What You Charge

Directly. The market rate for building a prototype is competitive, because many people can do it and the tools keep improving. The rate for taking something to production is considerably better, because the supply of people who can genuinely do it is small and the buyer is anxious.

Moving between those two positions is largely a matter of evidence. The same person, with the same skills, describing the same work in terms of what survived contact with users rather than what was built, competes in a different market.

## The Portfolio Itself: Format and Placement

The content matters more than the container, and a few practical decisions make the difference between material that gets read and material that gets skimmed.

**One page, not a gallery.** A prospective client reads until they have decided. Four projects with six lines each fits on one scrollable page and can be read in three minutes; a gallery of tiles requires clicking, and clicking is where attention is lost.

**Put the evidence above the screenshots.** The visual is what confirms competence; the numbers and the incident are what differentiate you. Most portfolios reverse this and bury the differentiator below a fold nobody reaches.

**Write it as prose, not as a specification.** "Built for nine clinics, live fourteen months, oversold once under simultaneous bookings and fixed with a database constraint" reads as experience. A table of technologies reads as a CV.

**Make it linkable and durable.** A page on your own domain rather than a profile on a platform you do not control, so a link you send today still works in two years when someone forwards it.

**Include one thing a client can verify.** A live product they can open, a public write-up, a named client who agreed to be named. One verifiable item makes the unverifiable ones credible.

**Say what you are looking for.** The kind of work, the stage of product, whether you take white-label engagements. Portfolios that describe capability without stating availability generate enquiries you have to filter.

**Keep a private version with more detail** — the technical specifics, the architecture decisions, the things under NDA described generically — to send when a conversation becomes serious. The public page earns the conversation; the private version wins it.

## What Clients Ask That Your Portfolio Should Pre-Answer

Three questions arrive in almost every first conversation, and a portfolio that answers them in advance shortens the conversation considerably.

**"What happens if you are unavailable?"** Every client working with an individual has this worry and few say it aloud. Addressing it directly — documented handovers as standard, code and accounts in the client's name, a named colleague or partner for continuity — removes the objection before it forms.

**"Will I be able to change it without you?"** Say plainly that you leave codebases conventional, documented and editable in the client's own tool, and that you verify it with them before finishing.

**"How do I know it is secure?"** Not a request for a certification. A description of your method — what you check, how you verify it, what you hand over — is what a non-technical buyer can actually evaluate.

Answering these in the portfolio rather than in the meeting has a second effect: it filters out the clients looking for the cheapest possible build, who are the ones least likely to value the work you do after the interface is finished.

## Real example

### A Freelancer Who Added One Paragraph and Doubled His Rate

Tim Roorda freelanced around Groningen building applications in Lovable and Cursor. His portfolio showed eleven projects, all screenshots, all beautiful. He was consistently losing work to cheaper competitors and could not explain why, because on any visual comparison his work was better.

The problem was that nothing in the portfolio addressed risk. Every prospect was choosing between candidates who all appeared to offer the same thing, so they chose on price.

He rewrote it in a weekend. Four projects instead of eleven, each with six lines covering what was built, what already existed, what he did about access control and deployment, something that broke, and what the client could do afterwards. He added a four-sentence section describing his security method, and one paragraph explaining that he builds with AI tooling and does the production work deliberately.

He also spent two weeks launching a small tool of his own properly — domain, monitoring, tested restore — so that at least one entry described something he operated rather than delivered.

**Result:** his conversion from first conversation to engagement roughly doubled over the following quarter, and he moved from competing on prototype price to quoting production work. Two clients told him the security paragraph was the reason they chose him.

> *"I was showing people beautiful screenshots and losing to people with worse screenshots and lower prices. Nobody was buying screenshots. They were buying the absence of a disaster."*
> — **Tim Roorda, Freelance developer (Groningen)**

**Cost & Timeline:** Two weekends of rewriting, plus two weeks launching one small product properly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do clients most want to see in an AI-focused portfolio?

Evidence that something you built met real users and survived. Modest numbers, an incident you handled, a before-and-after metric, and a note on what the client could do without you afterwards.

### Should I mention that I build with Lovable or Cursor?

Yes, plainly. Hiding it reads as concealment when discovered, and leading with it makes the tool the product. State that you build quickly with AI tooling and do the production work deliberately.

### Is including a project that broke a mistake?

The opposite. A portfolio without a single incident is not believable to anyone with production experience, and describing how you diagnosed and fixed one demonstrates more than five successful builds.

### How do I build evidence if everything I have made is a prototype?

Launch one small thing properly — domain, monitoring, tested backup, a real bug handled. Then harden an existing prototype and write up the method. Both produce portfolio material within weeks.

### As an agency, how do I offer production work I cannot staff?

Through a white-label production partner under NDA, so the client relationship and branding stay yours while the engineering appears only when a project needs it. It also gives you launched projects to write about afterwards.
