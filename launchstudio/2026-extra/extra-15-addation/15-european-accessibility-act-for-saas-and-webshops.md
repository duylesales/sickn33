On 28 June 2025, a new set of accessibility obligations started to apply across the European Union. The European Accessibility Act, usually shortened to EAA, requires a range of products and services sold to consumers to be accessible to people with disabilities. One of the services it covers is e-commerce, which means that many webshops, booking flows and consumer-facing apps built with AI tools now have legal accessibility requirements.

For founders, the act raises practical questions. Does it apply to my product? What does "accessible" mean in concrete terms? Is a B2B SaaS covered? And how much work is it to make an app generated by Lovable, Bolt or v0 meet the standard?

This guide answers those questions. It is a practical overview, not legal advice; for your specific situation, consult a lawyer or the relevant national authority.

## Key Takeaways

- **The EAA applies from 28 June 2025** to specific consumer products and services, including e-commerce services, consumer banking, e-books, electronic communications and parts of passenger transport.
- **Microenterprises providing services are exempt,** generally those with fewer than ten employees and annual turnover or balance sheet total not exceeding €2 million.
- **Pure B2B SaaS is usually outside direct scope,** because the act focuses on services to consumers, but business customers increasingly require accessibility anyway.
- **The technical benchmark is EN 301 549,** which for web content aligns with WCAG 2.1 level AA.
- **Accessibility is a product quality issue,** and AI-generated interfaces commonly miss labels, focus handling, contrast and keyboard support.

## What the European Accessibility Act Is

The EAA is Directive (EU) 2019/882. Its purpose is to harmonise accessibility requirements across member states, so that companies face one set of rules rather than many, and so that people with disabilities can use common products and services. Member states had to transpose it into national law, and its requirements have applied since 28 June 2025.

The act covers two groups:

**Products,** including computers and operating systems, self-service terminals such as ticket machines and payment terminals, smartphones, e-readers and certain consumer equipment for digital television.

**Services,** including:

- E-commerce services
- Consumer banking services
- Electronic communications services
- Access to audiovisual media services
- Elements of air, bus, rail and waterborne passenger transport services, such as websites, apps and ticketing
- E-books and dedicated software

For most founders building with AI tools, the relevant category is **e-commerce services**: services provided at a distance through websites and mobile apps with a view to concluding a consumer contract. In practice, that covers webshops, online booking and ordering services, and consumer subscriptions sold online.

## Who Is Covered, and Who Is Not

**Microenterprises providing services are exempt.** The directive defines a microenterprise as one employing fewer than ten persons and with an annual turnover or annual balance sheet total not exceeding €2 million. If your company meets that definition and provides a service, the EAA's service requirements do not apply to you, although accessibility remains good practice and may become relevant as you grow.

**B2B services are generally outside direct scope.** The EAA's service obligations focus on services to consumers. A SaaS product sold exclusively to businesses, such as an internal HR tool or a logistics dashboard, is usually not an e-commerce service under the act. However:

- If your SaaS powers a consumer-facing service for your customers, such as a booking widget or webshop module, your customers may need it to be accessible to meet their own obligations.
- Public-sector customers in the EU already have accessibility obligations under the Web Accessibility Directive and often require suppliers to meet WCAG.
- Larger businesses increasingly include accessibility requirements in procurement.

**Existing contracts have transition periods.** The directive allows service providers to continue providing services using products lawfully used before 28 June 2025 until a later date, and contracts agreed before that date may continue until they expire, within limits. These transitional arrangements are specific; check them for your situation rather than relying on them by default.

| Situation | Direct EAA obligation? |
| --- | --- |
| Webshop selling to consumers, 15 employees | Likely yes |
| Webshop selling to consumers, 4 employees and €800,000 turnover | Likely exempt as a microenterprise service provider |
| Consumer subscription app sold online, medium-sized company | Likely yes |
| B2B SaaS sold only to companies | Generally no, but customers may require it |
| SaaS providing a booking widget used by consumers on clients' sites | Indirectly, through clients' obligations |
| Supplier to the public sector | Public-sector accessibility rules apply to your customer |

## What "Accessible" Means in Practice

The EAA sets functional accessibility requirements, for example that information is perceivable, operable, understandable and robust, and that services provide information about how they meet those requirements. Harmonised standards translate this into testable criteria. For websites and apps, the relevant standard is **EN 301 549**, whose web requirements correspond to **WCAG 2.1 level AA**.

WCAG 2.1 AA includes 50 success criteria across four principles. The ones AI-built apps most often fail are:

| Area | What is required | Common failure in AI-built apps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text alternatives | Meaningful alt text for informative images | Missing alt text or file names as alt text |
| Form labels | Every input has a programmatic label | Placeholder text used instead of labels |
| Keyboard access | Everything usable without a mouse | Custom dropdowns and modals that trap or skip focus |
| Focus visibility | Visible focus indicator | Focus outline removed for design reasons |
| Colour contrast | 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and UI components | Light grey text on white, low-contrast buttons |
| Headings and structure | Logical heading levels and landmarks | Headings chosen for size, not structure |
| Error identification | Errors described in text, linked to fields | Red borders only |
| Status messages | Screen readers informed of updates | Toasts that are never announced |
| Resize and reflow | Usable at 400% zoom and 320 px width | Fixed-width layouts, horizontal scrolling |
| Name, role, value | Custom components expose correct semantics | Clickable `div` elements instead of buttons |

The good news is that most of these are fixable without redesigning the product. The bad news is that AI builders rarely get them right by default, because their training examples include a lot of inaccessible code and prompts rarely mention accessibility.

## Documentation the EAA Expects

Beyond the interface itself, service providers covered by the act must provide information about how their service meets the accessibility requirements. The directive describes this information in its annexes; in practice, many companies publish an accessibility statement that includes:

- The standard used, such as EN 301 549 or WCAG 2.1 AA
- Which parts of the service conform and any known limitations
- How users can report accessibility problems or request information in another format
- A description of the service's accessibility features

This information should be available in an accessible format, typically on a dedicated page linked from the footer and referenced in your terms and conditions.

## Enforcement in the Netherlands

The Netherlands transposed the EAA into national legislation, with supervision divided by sector. For e-commerce, consumer protection authorities play a role, and consumers and representative organisations can file complaints. Penalties and supervisory approaches differ between member states, so if you sell across the EU, check the rules in your main markets. The most practical approach is the same everywhere: meet WCAG 2.1 AA, publish accurate information and fix problems promptly when users report them.

## Why Accessibility Is Also Good Business

Even where the EAA does not apply, accessibility work tends to pay for itself:

- **A larger market.** The European Commission estimates around 87 million people in the EU have some form of disability, and many more benefit from accessible design temporarily or situationally, such as older users or people on mobile in bright sunlight.
- **Higher conversion.** Clear labels, visible focus, readable contrast and useful error messages make forms easier for everyone.
- **Better SEO and AI visibility.** Semantic headings, alt text and structured content help search engines and AI answer engines understand your pages.
- **Procurement readiness.** Public and enterprise buyers increasingly ask for accessibility conformance.

## How to Make an AI-Built App Accessible

**1. Audit.** Combine automated tools, which catch roughly a third of issues, with manual testing: keyboard-only navigation, a screen reader such as NVDA or VoiceOver, 200% and 400% zoom, and contrast checks.

**2. Fix shared components first.** Most problems live in a handful of components: buttons, inputs, modals, dropdowns, tabs and toasts. Fixing them once fixes every screen. Libraries built on accessible primitives, such as Radix-based components common in Lovable and v0 output, help, but customisations often break them.

**3. Fix critical journeys.** For a webshop, prioritise search, product pages, cart, checkout and account pages. For a booking service, prioritise finding availability, booking and payment.

**4. Add accessibility to your AI prompts.** Tell your builder to use semantic HTML, label every input, preserve focus outlines and meet WCAG 2.1 AA contrast. Add these rules to project instructions so new screens start accessible.

**5. Test in your pipeline.** Automated accessibility checks on key pages catch regressions before they reach users.

**6. Publish your statement** and a way to report problems, and respond to reports.

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: The Grocery Delivery Shop That Screen Readers Could Not Check Out

Thijs van der Meer runs Buurtboer, a regional grocery delivery service around Apeldoorn that connects local farms with households. With 14 employees and growing turnover, the company was not a microenterprise, and its webshop, built in Lovable on Supabase, was an e-commerce service selling to consumers. A customer who is blind emailed to say she had given up halfway through checkout because her screen reader could not find the delivery slot picker.

An accessibility audit found the problem was not isolated. The delivery slot picker was built from clickable `div` elements with no keyboard support, quantity buttons had no accessible names, form fields relied on placeholder text, error messages appeared only as red borders, the green brand colour on white failed contrast requirements, and the cart drawer trapped keyboard focus. There was no accessibility statement.

LaunchStudio rebuilt the slot picker as an accessible radio group with full keyboard support, gave every icon button an accessible name, added visible labels and text error messages linked to fields, and adjusted the brand green to a shade with sufficient contrast while keeping the visual identity. The cart drawer received proper focus management, status messages were announced to screen readers and automated accessibility checks were added to the deployment pipeline for the main pages. Engineers drafted an accessibility statement with a contact route, and added accessibility rules to Thijs's Lovable project instructions.

**Result:** The customer who reported the problem completed her first order the following week and became a regular. Checkout completion across all users rose by about 7% in the next two months, which Thijs attributes mainly to clearer error messages and labels.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the European Accessibility Act apply to my webshop?

It likely applies if you sell to consumers online and are not a microenterprise, meaning you have ten or more employees or turnover or balance sheet total above €2 million. Microenterprises providing services are exempt, though accessibility remains good practice and may be expected by customers.

### Does the EAA apply to B2B SaaS?

Generally not directly, because its service obligations focus on consumers. However, if your SaaS powers consumer-facing services for clients, or you sell to the public sector or large enterprises, customers may require WCAG 2.1 AA conformance contractually.

### Which standard should my app meet?

For websites and apps, the harmonised standard EN 301 549 is the reference, and its web requirements correspond to WCAG 2.1 level AA. Meeting WCAG 2.1 AA on your key user journeys is the practical target for most founders.

### How does Manifera's experience help with accessibility?

Manifera has built public-facing systems for 11+ years where accessibility was part of acceptance criteria. LaunchStudio applies that experience to AI-built apps by fixing shared components first, which makes accessible design the default for every future screen.

### Does accessibility help search and AI visibility?

Yes. Semantic headings, descriptive links, alt text, labelled forms and clear structure make pages easier for search engines and AI answer engines to parse and cite, so accessibility work usually improves discoverability as a side effect.
