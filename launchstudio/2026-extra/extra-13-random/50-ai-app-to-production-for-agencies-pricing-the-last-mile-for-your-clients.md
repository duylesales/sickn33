A new kind of brief is landing in agency inboxes: "I built this in Bolt over the weekend. Can you make it live?" The client is excited, the prototype looks good, and the budget expectation is shaped by how fast the prototype was made. For digital agencies and freelancers, taking a client's AI app to production is a real opportunity — and a real pricing problem. Quote too low and you absorb the risk of code you did not write. Quote too high and the client concludes agencies do not understand AI.

## Why AI App to Production Work Is Hard to Price

Three characteristics make last-mile work different from a normal build:

- **You did not write the code.** You are taking responsibility for decisions made by an AI tool and a non-technical client.
- **The problems are invisible from the demo.** Access control, payment confirmation, secrets and data handling look fine until reviewed.
- **The client anchors on the prototype.** "It took me a weekend" makes a four-week quote feel absurd, even when justified.

Pricing without a review means either padding heavily (and losing the client) or pricing tight (and losing money when the review you should have done reveals problems mid-project).

## Step 1: Price the Review Separately

The most reliable pattern is to sell the review as its own small, fixed-price product. It gives you the information to quote the rest accurately, and gives the client something concrete: a written list of findings ranked by risk. A review takes two to four days for a typical AI-built app. Many clients are surprised — and more willing to invest — once they see specific findings about their own app.

## Step 2: Price Fixes by Category, Not by Hour

After the review, price the work in categories clients understand:

- **Access and security:** server-side access control, secrets, admin protection.
- **Payments:** verified webhooks, subscriptions, refunds.
- **Data:** region, backups, deletion, retention.
- **Delivery:** domain, hosting, staging, deployment pipeline.
- **Visibility:** monitoring, error tracking, alerts.

Fixed prices per category let the client choose what to do now and what to postpone, while keeping non-negotiables (access, secrets, payments if applicable) mandatory.

## Step 3: Decide Who Carries the Risk After Launch

Launch is not the end. Decide explicitly: is there a warranty period? Is ongoing hosting and maintenance offered, and at what monthly price? Who is called when it breaks at night? Clients who built with AI often assume launch is "done"; agencies that make post-launch responsibilities clear avoid the most painful disputes.

## Step 4: Margin and Positioning

Last-mile work is a good fit for agencies that position themselves as the partner who makes AI-built products real — a relationship that often leads to design, marketing, feature development and hosting work afterwards. Price the launch as the entry point to a longer relationship rather than trying to extract full margin from it.

## In-House or Production Partner?

Many agencies do not have developers with deep experience in production hardening, database security and payment integrations — or have them fully booked on other projects. The options are:

| | In-house | Production partner (white-label) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control | Full | Shared, under your brand |
| Required expertise | Security, databases, payments, DevOps | Account management and client relationship |
| Capacity | Limited by your team | Scales with demand |
| Cost structure | Salaries, utilisation risk | Fixed price per project |
| Risk on unfamiliar stacks | Yours | Partner's |
| Client relationship | Yours | Still yours |

## A Pricing Model for Last-Mile Work

Agencies that price AI app to production work consistently tend to use a simple structure that clients understand:

| Component | What it covers | Pricing approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review | Code, database, hosting and payment review; written findings | Fixed fee, credited if the client proceeds |
| Core hardening | Access control, secrets, admin protection | Fixed per scope band (small/medium/large) |
| Payments | Webhooks, refunds, subscriptions | Fixed add-on, varies by complexity |
| Launch setup | Domain, hosting, staging, monitoring, email | Fixed add-on |
| Post-launch support | Warranty period for defects | Included for a defined period |
| Ongoing care | Hosting, updates, backups, monitoring | Monthly fee |

The structure makes quotes comparable across clients and makes margin predictable. With a production partner behind it, the agency knows its own cost for each component in advance.

## Explaining the Price to a Client Who Built It in a Weekend

The most common objection agencies hear is "but it only took me a weekend." A helpful framing: the weekend produced the part users see; production work covers the part that protects them. Show the client their own findings — "any logged-in user can see other members' workout logs," "payments are marked as successful before the provider confirms them" — and the conversation shifts from price to risk. Clients rarely argue about the cost of fixing something that could expose their customers' data.

## Protecting the Agency's Reputation

When an agency launches a client's AI-built app, the agency's name is attached to it. If the app later leaks data or loses payments, clients remember who launched it. Protect your reputation by never launching without the non-negotiables (access control, secrets, verified payments, backups, monitoring), documenting what was and was not in scope, and giving clients a clear written list of postponed items. That documentation protects both you and the client if questions arise later.

## Contracts for White-Label Arrangements

White-label partnerships work smoothly when three agreements are clear. Between agency and client: scope, price, warranties, IP and data processing responsibilities — with the agency as the contracting party. Between agency and production partner: scope per project, confidentiality, non-solicitation of the agency's clients, IP transfer to the client, and response times for defects. And a data processing chain: if the partner accesses personal data, a sub-processing arrangement consistent with the client's agreement. LaunchStudio works under NDA and does not contact the agency's clients directly unless the agency asks.

## Building a Productised Launch Service

Agencies that see repeated demand often productise the offer: a named service ("Launch Ready for AI prototypes"), a public price range, a standard review template, a checklist of included work and a standard handover pack. Productisation reduces sales effort, makes delivery predictable and positions the agency as a specialist in a growing niche. Pair it with a monthly care plan for recurring revenue.

## Handling Findings That Exceed the Budget

Sometimes the review reveals more than the client's budget allows. Offer a prioritised plan: non-negotiables now, important items in a second phase, improvements later. Put it in writing, have the client acknowledge postponed items and schedule a review date. This keeps the client safe, the project viable and the relationship intact — rather than either overselling or quietly skipping important work.

## Measuring the Offer's Success

Track a few numbers for your launch service: review-to-project conversion, average project value, delivery time against estimate, post-launch defects, conversion to care plans and follow-on work (design, marketing, features). These show whether the offer is profitable and where to improve — and they give you concrete results to share with prospective clients.

## Scoping Questions for the Client Intake

Accurate quotes start with a good intake. Agencies offering last-mile work can use a short form: which AI tool built the app; where the code and database live; who owns the accounts; number of user roles; whether payments are taken and how; integrations used; data sensitivity (health, children, financial, identity documents); expected users at launch; target date; and any known problems. Combined with repository access, this lets the review start immediately and keeps quotes consistent across clients.

## Delivery Workflow With a Production Partner

A typical white-label delivery runs like this: the agency submits the intake and access; the partner delivers review findings to the agency within days; the agency presents findings and a quote to the client under its own brand; after approval, the partner works on the client's repository and accounts, reporting daily to the agency; the agency handles client communication, content, design and launch marketing; the partner performs re-tests and prepares the handover pack, which the agency delivers. The client experiences one team; behind it, each party does what it does best.

## Upselling Without Overselling

Last-mile projects often reveal opportunities: design improvements, analytics, SEO, integrations, a mobile version. Present them as a roadmap after launch rather than bundling them into the initial scope. Clients appreciate a focused, affordable launch followed by a clear path — and are more likely to buy follow-on work from the agency that got them live safely and on time.

## Why Agencies Are Well Placed for This Market

Agencies already have what AI-native founders lack: client management, design sense, marketing skills and a reputation to protect. What many lack is production engineering capacity for AI-generated code — security, databases, payments, DevOps. A white-label production partner fills exactly that gap. As more founders and SMEs build prototypes with AI, agencies that can take them live reliably will find a steady stream of work that complements their existing services.

## A Note on Responsibility

Even in a white-label arrangement, the agency remains the client's contracting party and is responsible towards the client. Choose partners whose processes you can verify: written findings, tests, re-test reports and documentation. These artefacts let you stand behind the work confidently — and give your clients evidence that their AI app to production launch was handled with care.

## Getting Started as an Agency

The simplest way to start is with one client project. Choose a client whose AI-built app you know, request a review from a production partner, present the findings under your brand and price the work using the structure above. After one or two projects, you will know your costs, your margins and your clients' questions well enough to turn the offer into a standard service — and to recognise which prototypes are quick wins and which need a more careful plan before launch.

Roel Stam runs Pixelpolder, a six-person design and marketing agency in Heemstede. She wanted it live for her 1,100 members within a month and assumed Pixelpolder could "just publish it."

Pixelpolder's developers were front-end specialists, fully booked, and uneasy about payments and member data. Roel engaged LaunchStudio as a white-label partner. The review, delivered to Roel within three days, found that members could view other members' profiles and workout logs through the API, class capacity was enforced only in the browser, membership payments via Mollie were confirmed by redirect, and the Mollie live key was in the frontend. Roel presented the findings to the client under Pixelpolder's name, with a fixed price that included his margin, and she approved it the same day.

Over fourteen business days, LaunchStudio's engineers fixed access control, capacity, payments and secrets, migrated data to an EU region, and set up hosting, staging, monitoring and backups, with Roel as the single point of contact. Pixelpolder handled the launch campaign, app-store-style onboarding screens and member emails.

**Result:** The app launched on schedule; 780 members activated accounts in the first month, and class no-shows fell after reminders were introduced. Pixelpolder added managed hosting to the client's retainer and has since used the same arrangement for two further client apps.
