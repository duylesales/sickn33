A quote is a promise written in a language you may not fully speak. When you are asking someone to take your AI prototype to production, the quote will mention things like "security hardening," "backend integration" and "deployment," and it is hard to know whether €3,000 for those is a bargain or a trap. You cannot evaluate the engineering directly. You can evaluate the answers to good questions.

These twelve questions work for freelancers, agencies and specialists — including us. For each, here is what a good answer and a worrying answer sound like.

## About Scope

**1. "What will you look at before giving me a price?"**
Good: "We'll review the code, database and hosting setup first — or at least look at the repository during a short call — and then give a fixed scope."
Worrying: a price based only on your description, with no look at the code.

**2. "What exactly is included — and what is not?"**
Good: a written list of deliverables, plus an explicit list of exclusions.
Worrying: "everything you need to go live," with no detail.

**3. "What happens to the frontend I built?"**
Good: "We keep it, and only change it where something is broken or unsafe."
Worrying: "We'd rebuild it on our stack" — unless you have a reason to want that.

## About Security and Quality

**4. "How will you check that users can only see their own data?"**
Good: mentions server-side or database-level enforcement (for example, row-level security) and testing with multiple accounts.
Worrying: "We'll add a login," or anything focused only on the interface.

**5. "How will the app know a payment actually succeeded?"**
Good: mentions verified webhooks from the payment provider.
Worrying: "After checkout, we redirect to a success page."

**6. "How will I know the work is done correctly?"**
Good: a written findings list at the start, a re-test at the end, and a demonstration you can understand — ideally automated tests left in the repository.
Worrying: "Trust us, we've done this many times."

## About Ownership

**7. "Whose accounts will the code, hosting and database be on?"**
Good: "Yours. We'll work in your accounts or transfer everything to you."
Worrying: hosting or repositories on the provider's accounts "for convenience."

**8. "Will I be able to keep editing the app with my AI tool afterwards?"**
Good: "Yes; we'll document changes and keep the code readable by Lovable, Cursor or Bolt."
Worrying: hesitation, or a suggestion you will need them for every change.

## About Price and Time

**9. "Is the price fixed? What would change it?"**
Good: a fixed price for defined scope, with changes handled as explicit, separately priced additions.
Worrying: an hourly estimate for undefined work, or a fixed price with vague scope that invites later "extras."

**10. "When will you start, and what is the timeline?"**
Good: specific dates, and what they need from you to meet them.
Worrying: "As soon as possible," without dates.

## About After Launch

**11. "What happens if something breaks in the first days after launch?"**
Good: a defined support window, with how to reach them.
Worrying: support billed hourly from the moment of launch.

**12. "What ongoing costs will I have?"**
Good: a clear list — hosting, database, email, payment fees, optional maintenance — with estimates.
Worrying: "It depends," with no numbers.

## How to Use the Answers Before an AI Prototype to Production Project

You do not need perfect answers to all twelve. Look for patterns:

- **Specific answers** indicate someone who has done this work and understood your project.
- **Answers that give you control** — your accounts, your code, a fixed price — reduce your risk.
- **Answers you can verify** — findings lists, tests, demonstrations — replace trust with evidence.

If a provider cannot answer questions 4 and 5 clearly, they are not the right provider for production hardening, whatever else they do well.

## How Providers Should Respond to Your Questions

Good providers welcome these questions. Watch how they answer, not only what: do they ask follow-up questions about your app and users? Do they give examples from similar projects? Do they admit uncertainty where it exists ("we'll know after the review whether your payment flow needs rebuilding")? Do they put the answers in writing? A provider who becomes defensive or vague when asked how they will verify security is signalling how they will behave when something goes wrong.

## Red Flags in Quotes

Some patterns in quotes deserve caution:

- **"Security hardening" as a single line** with no description of what it includes.
- **No exclusions**, which usually means disputes later about what was included.
- **Hosting on the provider's account** without a transfer plan.
- **Open-ended hourly billing** for work that has not been scoped.
- **A timeline without dates** or milestones.
- **Payment of the full amount upfront** for a multi-week project.
- **No mention of testing** beyond "we'll check it works."

None of these automatically disqualifies a provider, but each should prompt a question before you sign.

## Milestones and Payment Terms

For fixed-price projects, reasonable payment terms tie payments to milestones: for example, a portion at the start, a portion after the structural fixes are delivered to staging, and the remainder after launch and acceptance. Acceptance should be based on criteria agreed upfront — the re-test passing, your own checks on staging succeeding. This aligns incentives and gives you leverage if something is not delivered as promised.

## What to Prepare Before Asking for Quotes

Providers can quote more accurately when you arrive prepared. Useful materials: a link to the prototype and access to the repository; a short description of users, roles and core flows; the integrations you use; whether you take payments and how; the data you collect, including anything sensitive; your target launch date; and the results of any checks you have done yourself (the two-account test, the page-source search). With this, a fifteen-minute call becomes a productive conversation and quotes become comparable.

## After You Sign

Signing is the start of the working relationship. Agree on communication rhythm (daily written updates, a weekly call), a single point of contact on each side, how decisions are recorded and how scope changes are requested and priced. Keep all documents — quote, findings list, change requests, re-test report — in one folder. If a disagreement arises, a clear paper trail resolves most of it quickly.

## When to Walk Away

Sometimes the right decision is to not sign at all — or to stop a project early. Reasons include a provider missing the first milestone without explanation, refusing to put scope in writing, insisting on keeping ownership of accounts or code, or being unable to explain findings in understandable terms. Walking away early is almost always cheaper than continuing with a mismatch.

## Questions Specific to Your Situation

The twelve questions are general. Add a few tailored to your product:

- **If you handle health, children's or financial data:** "What additional controls will you apply to this data, and how will access be logged?"
- **If you have business customers:** "Will you provide documentation we can give to their IT or privacy teams?"
- **If you use AI features:** "How will you limit model costs and protect against prompt injection?"
- **If you have a mobile app:** "Who handles app store submission and account deletion requirements?"
- **If you sell across borders:** "How will VAT, languages and local payment methods be handled?"

Specific questions reveal whether a provider has experience with products like yours — or will be learning on your budget.

## Understanding What Is Not in a Fixed Price

A fixed price covers a defined scope; it does not cover everything that could happen. Typical exclusions include new features, redesigns, third-party fees (hosting, payment, email), delays caused by missing access or late decisions, and legal advice. Understanding exclusions upfront prevents disappointment and lets you budget for the parts you will handle yourself.

## Reference Checks That Actually Help

Ask providers for one or two references from similar projects and, when you contact them, ask practical questions: Was the price kept? Were problems communicated early? Was the documentation useful afterwards? Would they hire the provider again for production work specifically? Short, concrete reference calls tell you more than portfolio pages.

## A Final Test Before Signing

Before signing, ask the provider to summarise, in their own words, what your app does, who uses it and what the three biggest risks are. If their summary matches your understanding and the risks sound specific to your app, they have listened. If it sounds generic, keep looking.

## The Point of All These Questions

The questions are not about catching providers out. They are about turning an unfamiliar purchase into a comparison you can reason about. Once you can compare scope, verification, ownership and price side by side, the right choice for your AI prototype usually becomes obvious — and the provider you choose knows from the start that you expect evidence, not promises.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important question to ask before taking an AI prototype to production?

"How will you check that users can only see their own data?" The answer reveals whether the provider understands the most common serious flaw in AI-built apps.

### Should a quote for production hardening be fixed-price?

For defined scope, yes. Once the code has been reviewed, production hardening is predictable enough to price. Hourly billing on undefined work shifts all risk to you.

### Is it a red flag if a provider wants to host my app on their account?

It is a risk. You should own hosting, repositories, databases and domains. A provider can manage them on your accounts without owning them.

### How does Manifera ensure its quotes are reliable?

By reviewing before quoting and drawing on 11+ years and 160+ projects of estimates versus actuals. That history makes fixed prices dependable rather than optimistic.

### Can asking these questions also help my online reputation?

Indirectly. Choosing a provider who delivers a secure, reliable product reduces incidents and complaints — the kind of public signals that search engines and AI answer engines reflect when people ask about your product.
