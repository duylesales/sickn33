At ten users, your AI-built SaaS is a conversation. You know everyone by name, you fix things over WhatsApp, and when something odd happens you ask the one person it happened to. At a hundred users, it becomes a system. The app has not changed, but the conditions around it have, and a specific set of AI app production problems appear almost on cue — not dramatic crashes, but slow erosion that costs you customers before you notice the pattern.

This article looks at that step from ten to a hundred, what changes, and what to fix first.

## Before: What Ten Users Hide

Ten friendly users are a remarkably forgiving environment. They rarely use the app at the same time. Their data is small. They report problems directly and forgive quickly. They mostly use the features you demoed, in the order you demoed them.

Under those conditions, an app generated by Lovable, Bolt or Cursor can look production-grade even when several of its foundations are not. Nothing is technically hiding; the conditions that would expose weaknesses simply have not occurred yet.

## After: What a Hundred Users Expose

A hundred users are not ten users multiplied. They behave differently in kind.

**They overlap.** With a hundred active accounts, several people are using the app at any given moment, and bursts happen — a Monday morning, a reminder email, a deadline. Operations that were never simultaneous now collide.

**They accumulate data.** Some of your users will be heavy users. One team will have 3,000 records while most have 30, and pages that load everything at once will slow to a crawl for exactly the customers who value you most.

**They don't tell you.** Most of the hundred will never report a problem. They just stop logging in.

**They cost money.** Every AI call, every email, every stored photo has a unit price. Multiplied by a hundred, small inefficiencies become line items.

## The Five AI App Production Problems That Show Up First

**1. Pages that get slower every week.** AI-generated database queries often fetch whole tables and filter in the browser, with no indexes on the columns being searched. With a hundred users and months of data, a dashboard that took 200 milliseconds now takes eight seconds. The fix — pagination, server-side filtering and a few well-chosen indexes — is usually a day or two of work, but only if someone notices before users give up.

**2. Emails that quietly stop arriving.** Many AI-built apps send email through a free tier or the database provider's built-in sender, which is designed for authentication emails in development, not for transactional volume. At some point you hit a daily limit and emails fail silently, or your sending domain has no SPF, DKIM and DMARC records and inbox providers start routing you to spam. Either way, password resets and invoices disappear.

**3. Two people editing the same thing.** A shared team calendar, a stock count, a list of open slots. With ten users, simultaneous edits are rare. With a hundred, the last save wins and someone's work vanishes — or two customers book the same slot.

**4. Support you cannot answer.** When a user says "my payment went through but I can't access the course," you need to look up what happened. Most AI-built apps have no admin view, no audit trail and no searchable logs, so every support question means digging through the database by hand.

**5. Bills that scale faster than revenue.** AI features that call a language model on every page load, images stored at full resolution, serverless functions that poll every few seconds — none of this matters at ten users. At a hundred, it can turn a profitable plan into a loss.

| Area | Fine at 10 users | Breaks at 100 users | Typical fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Queries | Load everything | Slow for heavy accounts | Pagination, indexes |
| Email | Free sender | Limits, spam folder | Transactional provider, DNS records |

## Diagnosing the Five Problems With Data, Not Guesswork

When growth exposes AI app production problems, the temptation is to guess: "it must be the hosting." A short diagnostic pass with data you already have usually points straight at the cause.

**For slow pages:** open your database provider's query performance view (Supabase has one built in) and sort by total time. In almost every AI-built app we review, three to five queries account for most of the database time. Run `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on each; look for sequential scans on large tables and for queries executed hundreds of times per page load.

**For missing emails:** your transactional email provider's dashboard shows delivered, bounced, deferred and spam-complaint counts. If you use the database provider's built-in sender, check its rate limits in the documentation — they are usually designed for development volumes.

**For concurrent edits:** search support messages for words like "disappeared," "reset," "changed back." Then look at the table in question: does it have an `updated_at` column the app actually checks before saving? If not, last-write-wins is almost certain.

**For support blind spots:** time how long it takes you to answer "what happened to this customer's payment?" If it takes more than five minutes, you need an admin view or an audit log.

**For runaway costs:** export last month's invoice per provider and divide by weekly active users. Compare with three months ago. A rising cost per active user is the clearest early warning.

## Optimistic Locking in Plain Terms

The concurrent-edit fix deserves a short explanation because it is so often missing. Each record carries a version number. When a user opens a record, the app remembers the version. When they save, the database only accepts the change if the version is still the same, and increments it. If someone else saved in the meantime, the second save is rejected and the user sees "this record was changed by someone else — review and try again" instead of silently overwriting. It costs a column and a condition, and it eliminates an entire class of "my changes vanished" complaints.

## Planning Capacity for the Next Step

Crossing 100 users is the right moment to set simple capacity targets for the next milestone, for example 1,000 users:

- The slowest important page should load in under two seconds for the heaviest customer.
- Email delivery should stay above 98%.
- Database CPU should stay below roughly half of its capacity at peak.
- Infrastructure cost per active user should be flat or falling.

Measure these monthly. When one drifts, you have months — not hours — to act, which is the difference between planned engineering and emergency fixes.

## Why Silent Churn Is the Real Risk

The most expensive of the five problems is invisible in your inbox. Users who hit slow pages or missing emails rarely complain; they stop logging in. By the time weekly activity drops noticeably, you have already lost many of the people who would have become your most loyal customers. That is why the fixes above are best done at 50–100 users, when the patterns first appear and the cost of correcting them is lowest.

## What Changes in How You Work at 100 Users

The move from ten to a hundred users is also a change in your own role. At ten users you are the support desk, the tester and the monitoring system. At a hundred you need tools to do those jobs for you: an error tracker that tells you about failures before customers do, an admin view that answers support questions in seconds, a weekly look at three numbers (page speed, email delivery, weekly active users) and a staging environment so new features no longer reach every customer the moment you publish them.

Founders often resist this because it feels like overhead. In practice it frees time. Every support question answered from an audit log instead of a database query, every failure seen in the error tracker before a customer writes in, is an hour back for selling and building. The scale-up founders who grow smoothly past a hundred users are rarely the ones with the most features; they are the ones who can see what their product is doing.

## What to Watch Before You Reach 100

Three indicators give early warning:

- **Median page load time on your two busiest pages**, checked weekly. If it is rising, queries need attention.
- **Email delivery rate**, visible in any serious transactional email provider. Anything below about 98% needs investigating.
- **Weekly active users as a share of signups.** A falling share, with no complaints, often means silent problems.

Google's guidance on [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) is a useful external benchmark for what "fast enough" means to real users — and to search engines.

## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: The Sports Club Dues App at Club Number 23

Sven Mulder, treasurer of a hockey club in Deventer, built Teamkas in Lovable to collect membership dues, match fees and kit payments. It worked so well for his own club that he turned it into a SaaS, and within six months 23 clubs were using it — roughly 110 club administrators and a few thousand members paying through it.

The problems arrived together at the start of the new season. The payments overview for larger clubs took over ten seconds to load because every transaction was fetched and filtered in the browser. Payment reminder emails were sent through Supabase's built-in email service and hit its limits; hundreds of reminders never went out, and clubs assumed members were ignoring them. Two treasurers at the same club editing a member's fee at the same time overwrote each other. And when a club asked why one member had been charged twice, Sven had no way to see what had happened.

LaunchStudio's engineers rewrote the heavy queries with pagination, server-side filters and indexes; moved all email to a transactional provider with a properly authenticated sending domain; added optimistic locking so conflicting edits produced a warning instead of silently overwriting; built a simple admin view with a per-member audit log; and set up monitoring on page load times and email delivery. The Lovable interface the clubs knew was left untouched.

**Result:** The payments overview dropped from over ten seconds to under one second for the largest club. Reminder delivery went from an estimated 60% to above 99%, and outstanding dues across the clubs fell by about a third in the following month. Teamkas grew to 41 clubs by the end of the season.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do AI app production problems appear at around 100 users specifically?

The number is approximate. What matters is that concurrent use, larger data sets and unit costs start to matter together. For some apps the threshold is fifty users, for others several hundred, but the pattern is consistent.

### Should I fix scalability before launching, or wait for users?

Fix what is cheap to fix early — indexes, email setup, basic monitoring — before launch. Deeper changes such as caching strategies or background queues can wait until real usage shows where they are needed.

### How do I know if my users are leaving because of performance?

Look for falling weekly activity without matching complaints, and compare page load times on the pages heavy users visit most. Silent churn plus slow pages is a strong signal.

Yes. Page speed is part of Google's ranking signals through Core Web Vitals, and slow or timing-out pages are less likely to be crawled fully or cited by AI answer engines.
