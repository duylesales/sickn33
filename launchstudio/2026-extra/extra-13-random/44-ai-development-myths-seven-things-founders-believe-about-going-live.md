Everyone says AI can build your entire app. Fewer people mention that 45% of AI-generated code contains security vulnerabilities, or that around 80% of AI-built projects never reach production. Somewhere between the hype and the statistics sit a handful of AI development myths that founders pick up from social media, tool marketing and each other. Some are partly true. Most lead to predictable surprises at launch.

Here are seven, examined one by one.

## Myth 1: "The AI Tool Handles Security"

**What is true:** AI tools generate code that follows common patterns, including some security basics — password hashing, HTTPS, standard login flows. Some tools add security scanners or warnings.

**What is not:** AI tools do not know who should see what in your app. They build what you describe, and "users see their dashboard" does not tell them to enforce, on the server, that users only see their own records. Access control, payment verification and secret handling are consistently where AI-built apps fall short.

**What to do instead:** treat security as something you verify, not something you assume. A two-account test takes five minutes; a review takes days.

## Myth 2: "If It Works in the Preview, It Works"

**What is true:** a working preview proves your idea functions and your flows make sense. That is valuable.

**What is not:** a preview is tested by one friendly user on a good connection, with test data. Production means strangers, simultaneous users, bad connections, real payments, closed tabs mid-checkout and people deliberately trying things. The preview cannot tell you how the app behaves under any of those.

**What to do instead:** test the unhappy paths — wrong input, two accounts, closed payment tabs, slow mobile connections — before real users do it for you.

## Myth 3: "Going Live Means Rebuilding It Properly"

**What is true:** some prototypes do need significant rework, usually when the data model is wrong for the business.

**What is not:** most do not. The interface generated by AI tools is usually the most finished part of the product. What is missing sits underneath — access rules, payments, hosting, monitoring — and can be added without rebuilding what users see. LaunchStudio's approach is exactly that: keep the frontend, fix what is needed, go live quickly, typically for 20% of what an agency would charge.

**What to do instead:** get a review before accepting a rebuild quote. It will tell you which situation you are in.

## Myth 4: "Nobody Will Bother Attacking My Small App"

**What is true:** hardly anyone targets a specific small app deliberately.

**What is not:** automated scanners probe every public site constantly, and curious users poke at URLs. Most breaches of small apps are not sophisticated; they exploit open doors that anyone can find in minutes.

**What to do instead:** close the well-known doors — exposed keys, predictable admin URLs, changeable IDs — before launch.

## Myth 5: "I'll Fix It After Launch When I Have Revenue"

**What is true:** many things can and should wait until after launch — performance tuning, full test suites, admin tools, advanced monitoring.

**What is not:** some problems cannot wait because their damage is irreversible: a data leak cannot be undone, and leaked keys have already been leaked. Others become much more expensive after launch: migrating a database with live customer data, repairing payment records.

**What to do instead:** split the list. Fix what is irreversible or gets more expensive before launch; postpone the rest deliberately, in writing.

## Myth 6: "Freelancers Can Fix AI Code Cheaply"

**What is true:** good freelancers exist, and some understand AI-generated code well.

**What is not:** many freelancers are unfamiliar with the specific patterns of AI-built apps and fix symptoms rather than causes. Hourly billing on undefined work can cost more than a fixed-price specialist, and quality varies widely. Founders commonly report months of back-and-forth before launching.

**What to do instead:** whoever you hire, ask how they will verify access control, payment confirmation and secrets — and insist on a written list of findings and a fixed scope.

## Myth 7: "Once It's Live, the Hard Part Is Over"

**What is true:** launch is a genuine milestone.

**What is not:** a live app needs ongoing care — dependency updates, platform changes, certificate renewals, backups that are actually checked, and someone who notices outages. AI regeneration can also silently undo earlier fixes.

**What to do instead:** decide who maintains the app — you, a developer, or a managed service — before launch, and put tests in place that catch regressions.

## Why These Myths Persist

AI development myths survive for understandable reasons. Tool marketing emphasises what the tools do brilliantly — generating working software from a description — and says less about what they leave to you. Social media rewards launch stories, not incident post-mortems. Early success reinforces the myths: a prototype with twenty friendly users genuinely does work, and nothing contradicts the belief that it is ready. And most failures are private: founders rarely publicise the week their app leaked data or double-charged customers. The result is a skewed picture in which the risks are real but mostly invisible until they are personal.

## A Reality Check You Can Run on Your Own App

For each myth, there is a quick test that tells you whether it applies to you:

| Myth | Quick test | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The tool handles security" | Two-account test: open another user's data by changing an ID | Access control needs server-side enforcement |
| "Preview works, so it works" | Close the payment tab before returning; submit a huge file; use a slow mobile connection | Unhappy paths need handling |
| "Going live means rebuilding" | Ask a reviewer which parts must change; count screens affected | Usually few or none |
| "Nobody will attack a small app" | Check logs or hosting analytics for requests to `/admin`, `/.env`, `/wp-login.php` | Automated probing is already happening |
| "Fix it after launch" | Classify each open issue: reversible or irreversible harm? | Irreversible items go first |
| "Freelancers fix AI code cheaply" | Ask how they will verify access control and payments | Vague answers mean risk |
| "Once live, done" | Who updates dependencies next month, and who gets the outage alert? | Maintenance needs an owner |

Most founders find two or three myths apply. That is normal — and it tells you exactly where to spend effort.

## The Evidence Behind the Statistics

The figures commonly cited — that a large share of AI-generated code contains vulnerabilities, and that most AI-built projects never reach production — come from a mix of academic studies, industry surveys and the experience of review teams. The precise percentages vary by study and methodology, but the direction is consistent: AI assistance increases the speed of producing code without automatically increasing its security, and many prototypes stall at the transition to real use. Academic work on AI code assistants has also found that developers using them can be more confident in insecure code than those who write it themselves — which is perhaps the most important finding for founders.

## What Replaces the Myths

Healthier beliefs are more nuanced but more useful:

- AI tools are excellent builders; security, reliability and operations are decisions you make deliberately.
- A working preview proves value; production proves safety under real conditions.
- Most prototypes need hardening, not rebuilding.
- Small apps are probed automatically; basic protections matter from the first stranger.
- Some fixes can wait; irreversible risks cannot.
- Any provider should be able to explain exactly how they will verify security.
- Launch starts operations; maintenance needs an owner and a routine.

## How Experienced Engineers Think About AI-Built Apps

Engineers who review many AI-built apps tend to share a calm view: the code is often better than expected in structure and worse than expected in boundaries — access, secrets, payments, error handling. They look first at the boundaries, because that is where the patterns repeat. For founders, this is good news: the problems are predictable, which makes them affordable to fix.

## Talking to Co-Founders and Investors About the Myths

If a co-founder or investor holds one of these beliefs, a demonstration persuades more than an argument. Run the two-account test together, close a payment tab mid-checkout, or look at your hosting logs for automated probes. Seeing the gap with their own eyes turns an abstract debate into a practical plan — and usually into agreement about where to invest before launch.

## Myths Specific to Non-Technical Founders

Two further beliefs appear often among founders who do not code. The first is "I can't judge any of this, so I have to trust whoever I hire." In reality, a handful of checks — the two-account test, the page-source search, the closed-tab payment test — let any founder verify the most important claims. The second is "security is a technical topic, so it isn't my responsibility." Legally and commercially, it is: your company is responsible for users' data and money, whatever tools and suppliers were involved. Accepting that responsibility is not a burden; it is what lets you ask the right questions and recognise good answers.

## Myths Specific to Technical Founders

Technical founders have their own versions. "I'll review everything the AI writes" breaks down under volume — agents and assistants produce more code than anyone can read carefully. "Our tests pass, so it's fine" ignores that AI-written tests often assert what the code does rather than what it should do. And "I know what I'm doing, so I don't need a review" overlooks that self-review is structurally weak: we test what we intended, not what we forgot. The fix in each case is process — guardrails, human-specified negative tests and occasional outside review — rather than more personal effort.

## Turning Myth-Busting Into a Plan

Once you know which myths applied to you, convert them into a short plan: the two or three non-negotiable fixes before launch, the maintenance owner after launch and the review cadence for future changes. Keep the plan on one page. It is the practical outcome of this article — and usually the start of a calmer launch.

## One Myth to Keep

There is one belief worth keeping: that AI tools have made building software dramatically more accessible. It is true — and it is exactly why going live safely deserves the same seriousness as building fast.

## The Pattern Behind the AI Development Myths

Each myth treats a part of the truth as the whole. AI development genuinely changes how fast products get built. It has not changed what makes software safe and dependable for other people. ## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: An Asparagus Farm That Believed Four of the Seven

Bart Hofman runs a family asparagus farm near Venlo and built Aspergeshop with v0 and a simple backend assembled with AI help: customers pre-order white asparagus in season, choose a pickup time at the farm shop, and pay online. He believed, in his own words, "that the tool took care of security, that it worked because the preview worked, that no one would hack a farm, and that I'd tidy it up after the season."

The season started, and so did the problems. Pre-orders were confirmed when the customer's browser returned from the payment page; customers who paid in their banking app and did not return had no order at pickup. The pickup-slot capacity was checked in the browser, so popular Saturday slots were overbooked by a factor of two. The order list at `/orders` — names, phone numbers, amounts — was visible to anyone who typed the address. And the payment provider's secret key was in the page source.

LaunchStudio's engineers fixed the payment confirmation with verified webhooks and reconciled orders already affected, enforced slot capacity in the database, locked the order list behind server-side authentication, rotated the key and moved it server-side, and set up an uptime check and backups. The storefront Bart had built in v0 stayed exactly as it was.

**Result:** For the rest of the season, Aspergeshop handled around 3,100 pre-orders without overbooked slots or missing orders, and Saturday queues at the farm shop shrank noticeably because pickups were spread evenly. Bart now reviews his app with LaunchStudio each February, before the next season.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do AI coding tools make apps insecure?

Not deliberately. They generate code that works for the scenario described, which often omits access control, payment verification and proper secret handling. The result is frequently insecure unless someone checks.

### Is it better to rebuild an AI prototype from scratch before launch?

Rarely. Most prototypes can go live by adding what is missing underneath the existing interface. A review shows whether yours is one of the exceptions.

### What should I fix before launch, and what can wait?

Fix what is irreversible or gets more expensive later — access control, secrets, payment verification, backups, data location. Performance tuning, broad test suites and admin tools can usually wait.

### Why does Herre Roelevink say the challenge has shifted?

Because AI made turning ideas into software fast and cheap, while making software safe and dependable still requires experienced architecture and security work. Manifera's decade of enterprise delivery sits in that second area.
