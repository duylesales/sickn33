"The AI wrote it" is not a defence anyone accepts. When an AI-built app leaks customer data, charges people twice or loses a booking that cost a customer money, the question of who is responsible lands quickly — and usually on the founder. Understanding liability before taking an AI app to production helps you make sensible choices about terms, contracts, insurance and, most importantly, the engineering that prevents incidents in the first place.

This is a general overview, not legal advice. Liability depends on your contracts, your customers (consumers or businesses) and the law of the countries involved.

## The Founder's Company Is Usually First in Line

To your customers, your company provided the service. It does not matter to them that code was generated by Lovable, Bolt or Cursor, that a freelancer made changes, or that the hosting provider had an outage. Claims, complaints and regulatory questions come to you. You may have recourse against suppliers, but only as far as your contracts with them allow — and most AI tool and hosting terms limit their liability severely.

## What AI Tools and Platforms Typically Accept

AI coding tools, hosting providers and backend services generally provide their services "as is," exclude or cap liability, and place responsibility for how you use their output on you. In practice, you should assume you cannot pass the consequences of generated code back to the tool that generated it.

## Where Liability Can Arise After AI App to Production

**Data protection.** Under GDPR, your company is typically the controller of your users' personal data. A breach can lead to notification duties, regulatory action and claims from affected individuals. If you process data for business customers, you are their processor and contractually liable to them as well.

**Contract.** Your terms of service with customers define what you promise and how liability is limited. With business customers, limitations are broadly possible; with consumers, many limitations are restricted by consumer law.

**Consumer law.** Consumers have rights regarding digital content and services that conform to the contract. Faulty digital services can trigger remedies such as repair, price reduction or termination.

**Product liability.** The EU's revised Product Liability Directive expressly treats software as a product, extending no-fault liability for defective products causing certain types of damage to software — with national implementation following. This primarily concerns damage such as personal injury, property damage and data loss for consumers, and deserves attention for apps that control physical things or safety-relevant processes.

**Payment errors.** Double charges and incorrect billing lead to refunds, chargebacks and, if repeated, problems with your payment provider.

## Reducing Exposure: Four Layers

**1. Engineering.** The most effective liability control is preventing incidents: access control, verified payments, tested backups, monitoring and a proper release process. Everything else is damage limitation.

**2. Contracts.** Clear terms of service with appropriate limitations for business customers, accurate consumer information, data processing agreements where you are a processor, and contracts with freelancers and suppliers that assign IP and define responsibilities.

**3. Documentation.** Records of what you did to secure the system — reviews, fixes, tests, backup restores — show diligence if something goes wrong.

**4. Insurance.** Professional indemnity and cyber insurance can cover some costs of incidents and claims. Insurers increasingly ask about security measures such as MFA, backups and incident plans.

## When a Freelancer or Agency Built Part of It

If someone else built or changed your app, your contract with them determines what you can claim. Many informal arrangements have no written terms at all. For future work, agree on scope, acceptance, warranties and liability in writing — and keep ownership of all accounts and code.

## Mapping Who Is Responsible for What

When taking an AI app to production, it helps to draw the chain of responsibility explicitly:

| Party | Typical role | What your contract with them usually says | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Your customers | Receive your service | Your terms of service | You answer to them first |
| Your company | Provider, often data controller | — | Carries most liability |
| AI coding tool | Generated code | "As is," liability limited | Little recourse |
| Hosting, database, email providers | Infrastructure processors | Service levels, limited liability | Limited recourse for outages |
| Payment provider | Processes payments | Merchant agreement | Rules you must follow |
| Freelancer or agency | Built or changed parts | Your agreement with them (if any) | Recourse depends on the contract |
| Production partner (e.g. LaunchStudio) | Hardening, hosting | Fixed scope, warranties, support | Defined responsibilities |

The pattern is clear: your company sits at the centre, facing customers on one side and suppliers with limited liability on the other. Your protection comes mainly from preventing incidents and from contracts that reflect what each party actually does.

## Writing Terms That Match Your Product

Terms of service generated by AI tools often promise too much ("guaranteed availability," "fully secure") or exclude too much (all liability, which consumer law may not allow). Better terms describe the service accurately, set realistic availability expectations without guarantees you cannot measure, limit liability in ways appropriate to business or consumer customers, explain how data is handled with reference to the privacy notice, describe how disputes and complaints are handled and state which law applies. For consumers, include required information on withdrawal rights and conformity. A lawyer can review AI-drafted terms against your actual service in a few hours.

## Service Levels for Business Customers

Business customers may ask for availability commitments. Before offering one, make sure you can measure it (external uptime monitoring), define what counts (planned maintenance excluded, for example), have the infrastructure and support to meet it and cap the remedy (for example service credits rather than open-ended damages). Commitments you cannot measure or meet become liabilities; modest, well-defined commitments build trust.

## Documentation as Liability Protection

In many disputes, what matters is whether you acted reasonably. Documentation shows it: records of security reviews and fixes, test results, backup restore tests, incident logs with response actions, change logs and communications with customers. Keep these organised and dated. If a regulator or court ever examines an incident, the question will not only be "what went wrong?" but "what did you do to prevent it, and how did you respond?"

## Insurance: What to Ask

When discussing professional indemnity and cyber insurance, ask brokers: does the policy cover claims arising from software you provide as a service; are data breaches, notification costs and regulatory investigations covered; are incidents caused by third-party providers included; what security measures does the insurer require (MFA, backups, patching); and are claims related to AI-generated code or AI features excluded? Answers vary widely, and small startups are often surprised by exclusions.

## Contracts With Freelancers and Agencies

For future development work, agree in writing: scope and acceptance criteria; warranty period for defects; IP assignment; confidentiality and data protection obligations; responsibility for security of their deliverables; liability limits appropriate to the project size; and handover of code, credentials and documentation. This does not guarantee compensation if something goes wrong, but it clarifies expectations and gives you recourse where the supplier genuinely failed to deliver what was agreed.

## Incident Response and Liability

How you respond to an incident affects liability. Prompt containment, honest communication, correct notification (to the data protection authority within 72 hours where required, and to affected people where the risk is high), and evidence of remediation all reduce harm and reflect well on your conduct. Delay, denial or silence tend to increase both damage and exposure. Prepare the procedure before you need it.

## AI Features and Liability

Apps that use AI features — recommendations, summaries, generated content — add a further dimension. If an AI feature gives wrong advice, generates harmful content or makes a decision about a person, questions arise about responsibility. Reduce exposure by labelling AI output clearly, avoiding claims that AI output is always accurate, keeping humans in the loop for consequential decisions, documenting the AI features and their limitations, and following the transparency requirements of the EU AI Act where they apply. Your terms should describe AI features honestly and limit reliance where appropriate — but, as always, prevention and transparency protect more than disclaimers.

## Physical-World Consequences

Liability risks grow when software affects the physical world: equipment deliveries, lock codes, bookings of transport or accommodation, device control. A missed delivery or an unlocked door is more than a data problem. For such apps, invest especially in correctness under concurrency (no double bookings), clear notifications to all parties, audit trails and fallback procedures — and check that your insurance covers consequential losses where relevant. The revised EU Product Liability Directive also brings software closer to traditional product liability for certain damage, which makes these precautions more important over time.

## A Liability Readiness Checklist

Before scaling: terms of service reviewed against the actual product; privacy notice accurate; processing agreements with suppliers and business customers; contracts with freelancers and agencies including IP and warranties; availability commitments only where measurable; documentation of security work; incident procedure prepared; insurance reviewed for software, data breaches and AI-related exclusions; and the most common incident causes — access control, payments, concurrency — addressed in the product.

## First Step

Read your current terms of service and privacy notice side by side with what your app actually does. Every promise that the product cannot keep is a liability you created with a single sentence — and the easiest one to remove.

## Why "The AI Wrote It" Changes Nothing — and Everything

Legally, using AI to build software changes little: your company still provides the service and answers for it. Practically, it changes a lot: code is produced faster than anyone reviews it, suppliers' liability is limited and the founder often understands the product's behaviour less deeply than a traditional developer would. That combination makes prevention, documentation and honest terms more important, not less. Founders who accept this early build products that customers, insurers and investors can rely on. Those who assume the tool carries some of the responsibility discover, usually during an incident, that it carries none — and that the questions land on their desk alone.

## Remember

Liability follows the promises you make and the care you can show. Promise only what your product does, and keep evidence of the care you take.

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: A Party Rental Platform and a Missed Wedding Delivery

Marco Heijnen, who rents out party tents, tables and lighting in Winterswijk, built Verhuurkalender in Lovable: customers book equipment for dates, pay a deposit and choose delivery slots; three other rental companies in the Achterhoek joined the platform. One Saturday, a wedding couple's tent never arrived: the booking had been overwritten by a second booking for the same tent, because availability was not locked, and neither the couple nor the rental company had been notified.

The couple demanded compensation for the emergency replacement tent. Marco found that his terms of service, generated by an AI tool, promised "guaranteed availability" and contained no limitation of liability; there was no written agreement with the freelancer who had adjusted the booking logic; he had no insurance covering the platform; and he could not prove what had happened because there were no logs. He settled with the couple out of his own pocket.

Marco worked with a lawyer on new terms that accurately described the service and limited liability appropriately for business partners, signed agreements with the partner rental companies, and took out professional indemnity and cyber insurance — for which the insurer asked for exactly the documentation LaunchStudio had produced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the AI tool liable if its generated code causes damage?

Generally not in practice. AI tools' terms typically limit liability and place responsibility for use of the output on you. Assume your company carries the responsibility.

### Can I limit my liability in my terms of service?

With business customers, limitations are broadly possible within legal bounds. With consumers, many limitations are restricted by consumer law. Have a lawyer draft terms that match what your app actually does.

### Does the new EU Product Liability Directive apply to software?

Yes, the revised directive treats software as a product, extending no-fault liability for certain defects causing damage such as personal injury, property damage or data loss, with national implementation to follow.

### How does documentation help if something goes wrong?

Records of reviews, fixes, tests and backup restores show that you took reasonable measures, which matters to regulators, customers, insurers and in disputes.

### Does reducing incidents affect how my product is presented online?

Yes. Fewer incidents mean fewer complaints and negative reviews — the signals search engines and AI assistants use when describing and recommending products.
