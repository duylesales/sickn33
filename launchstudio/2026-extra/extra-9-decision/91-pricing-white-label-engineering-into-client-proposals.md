A €3,100 subcontractor invoice turns into a €9,500 line item on the client-facing proposal. Look at those two numbers side by side and the honest reaction is discomfort — that's a 3x markup, and 3x is the number that makes agency owners quietly change the line item's name to something vaguer before hitting send. It shouldn't be. A markup that size is not padding if the €6,400 gap is actually accounted for by real costs the client never sees: the scoping call, the two rounds of internal QA, the client update calls, the risk you're carrying by putting your name on someone else's engineering, and the margin that keeps your agency solvent between projects. The number isn't the problem. Not knowing what's inside it is.

This is the specific skill this article is about: turning a fixed subcontractor cost — in LaunchStudio's case, typically €800 to €7,500 depending on scope — into a single, defensible client-facing number that doesn't read as inflated and doesn't quietly eat your margin either. Most agencies get this wrong in one of two directions, and both are expensive.

## The Two Ways Agencies Get This Wrong

The first failure mode is under-pricing: quoting the client something close to the subcontractor's actual cost, plus a token 15% to "cover overhead," because a bigger number feels uncomfortable to defend in the sales call. This feels honest in the moment and is quietly ruinous over a quarter. That 15% doesn't come close to covering the actual hours an account manager spends on scoping calls, client check-ins, revision cycles, and the invoice-chasing that surrounds every subcontracted project — hours that are real, billable-equivalent work even though no invoice exists for them. An agency running this pattern across ten projects a year is running ten projects that lose money the moment you account for the owner's or PM's actual time, dressed up as ten projects that "broke even."

The second failure mode is the opposite: pricing as if the agency built everything from scratch, quoting client-facing numbers in the traditional agency range of €20,000 to €500,000 for what is, underneath, a fixed-scope €2,500–€7,500 subcontracted engagement. This works exactly until a client asks a detailed technical question the agency can't answer from first-hand knowledge, or compares notes with another founder who got a similar scope done for a fraction of the price elsewhere. Overpricing on a resold service is a trust problem waiting for a trigger, and in a market where founders increasingly talk to each other about what launch work actually costs, that trigger arrives faster than agencies expect.

## The Method: Cost-Plus, But the "Plus" Has Line Items

The workable middle sits on a cost-plus model where every component of the "plus" is something you could name out loud to the client if asked, because eventually someone will ask. Start with the actual subcontractor cost — say €3,100 for a Launch & Grow-tier engagement covering payments, auth, and hosting on a client's Lovable prototype. Add, explicitly, four categories: your scoping and discovery time (typically 2–4 hours at your effective rate), your project management overhead across the engagement (call cadence, status updates, the Slack channel you're monitoring daily for two to three weeks), a revision buffer (because client-requested changes during the build are near-universal, not exceptional), and your margin — the number that makes the whole exercise worth running instead of just introducing the client to an engineer directly and stepping back.

Priced this way, that €3,100 engagement might reasonably become: €3,100 subcontractor cost, €450 for scoping and PM time, €300 revision buffer, €1,650 margin (roughly 35% on the loaded cost) — landing at a client-facing quote near €5,500. That's a markup of roughly 1.8x on the raw subcontractor number, which is defensible in detail if a sophisticated client asks, and which still leaves the agency with real margin rather than a rounding error. Compare that to the 3x figure from the opening — the difference isn't that 3x is always wrong, it's that 3x with no itemized justification behind it is a guess dressed as a quote, and guesses are what erode client trust the first time a number gets questioned.

## Bundling: Presenting One Number Instead of a Cost Breakdown

The line-item build-up above is how you should calculate the number internally. It is almost never how you should present it to the client. A proposal that itemizes "subcontractor fee: €3,100, our margin: €1,650" invites exactly the wrong conversation — a client negotiating your margin down line by line, which is not a negotiation you want to have and not one that reflects the actual value you're providing as the party who found the partner, scoped the work, carries the relationship risk, and will field every question during the build.

The better presentation bundles the engagement into a single deliverable-based number, described by outcome rather than by cost source: "Production-ready launch: payments, authentication, secure hosting, and two weeks of post-launch support — €5,500, fixed price, live in three weeks." This framing sells the same underlying work without inviting a line-by-line cost audit, and it matches how your client is already accustomed to buying agency services — by outcome and timeline, not by your internal cost stack. If a client does ask what's included in the number, describe scope and deliverables, not vendor cost breakdowns; "that figure covers the full engineering build, our project oversight through delivery, and a two-week post-launch support window" is both true and appropriately opaque.

## Anchoring Against the Market, Not Against Your Cost

The other lever worth using deliberately is anchoring the client's expectation against the market rate before you ever state your number, because a €5,500 quote lands completely differently depending on what the client believes the alternative costs. If the client has already gotten a quote from a traditional agency — realistically €20,000 to €500,000+ for the equivalent scope done as a rebuild — your number looks like exceptional value by comparison, even carrying a healthy margin. If the client has no reference point at all, it's worth giving them one honestly during the sales conversation: mention, briefly, that traditional agencies typically quote five to ten times this range for comparable production work, and that freelancers in the €5,000–€20,000 band frequently can't handle AI-generated codebases reliably in the first place. This isn't inflating your own number — it's giving the client the same market context they'd eventually find on their own, on your terms and in your framing rather than discovered later and read as a surprise.

## When to Show Your Work Instead of Hiding It

There's one scenario where the itemized breakdown is the right move instead of the bundled number: a sophisticated client, often one who's run engineering projects before or who explicitly asks how the pricing was built. With this client, hiding the cost structure reads as evasive rather than professional, and it can cost you the deal outright. In that specific conversation, walking through the real structure — subcontractor cost, your oversight and scoping time, your margin — builds more trust than a single opaque number, precisely because this client would have kept probing until they got the breakdown anyway. Reading which client you're in front of, sophisticated-and-probing versus outcome-and-timeline-focused, is itself part of the pricing skill, not a separate one.

## Where the Margin Actually Comes From

It's worth being explicit, at least with yourself, about what that 30–40% margin on top of subcontractor cost is actually buying the client, because if you can't articulate it you'll struggle to defend it under pressure. It's buying: a single point of accountability instead of a founder managing an unfamiliar vendor relationship directly; someone who already speaks the client's business language translating between the client's goals and the engineer's technical questions; absorbed risk, since if the engagement runs into trouble, it's your account on the line and your reputation managing the fallout, not the client's first direct experience with an unknown subcontractor; and continuity, since you're the party still answering the phone after the engagement ends, for the next feature request, the next question, the next project. None of that is free to provide, and none of it should be priced as if it were.

## What Happens When Scope Changes After You've Quoted

Fixed-price quoting only stays honest if you've also decided, before the engagement starts, what happens when the client asks for something outside the original scope — because on a subcontracted build, scope creep hits your margin twice: once against the subcontractor's fixed price, which typically doesn't flex without a change order, and once against your own unpaid hours managing the conversation about why it doesn't. The agencies that handle this cleanly build the boundary into the proposal itself, in plain language the client reads before signing: "This quote covers [specific deliverables]. Requests outside this scope are handled as a separate change order, quoted before work begins." That single sentence does more to protect margin over the life of an engagement than any markup percentage, because it converts an awkward mid-project renegotiation into a pre-agreed process the client already expects.

When a change request does come in — and on client-facing work, it reliably does, usually somewhere around the midpoint of the build once the client sees real progress and starts noticing what else they want — the move is to price it the same way you priced the original scope: get the actual cost of the added work from your subcontractor first, then apply the same cost-plus logic rather than picking a number that feels proportionate. A client asking to add a referral program mid-build isn't asking for a favor, and treating it as a quick, informally-priced add-on is exactly how a well-margined project quietly becomes a break-even one by the time it ships. The agencies that protect margin best over a full year of white-label work are rarely the ones with the highest markup on paper — they're the ones with the tightest discipline around what a "yes" to a scope change actually costs before they say it.

## Real example

### An Agency Partner in Action: The Proposal That Almost Read as Padded

Tessa Vermaas runs a four-person branding-and-web studio in Utrecht that had started fielding requests from clients who'd built Bolt prototypes and wanted them launched properly. Her first attempt at pricing a white-label engagement — a €2,800 subcontractor quote turned into a flat €8,000 client number — felt uncomfortable even to her, and when a prospective client asked "what exactly am I paying for," she didn't have a confident answer beyond "the full build."

She restructured using a cost-plus method with named categories: subcontractor cost, her own scoping and account management time across the three-week engagement, a revision buffer, and a defined margin — landing at €5,200 for a comparable scope, presented to the client as a single outcome-based number rather than a cost breakdown. The next client who asked "what am I paying for" got a confident, specific answer about deliverables and timeline instead of a shrug.

**Result:** Tessa's studio closed the next two white-label proposals without a single pricing objection, and she now runs every subcontracted engagement through the same four-category build-up before quoting, cutting her proposal-preparation time by roughly half since she's no longer guessing at a number each time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 2x markup on subcontractor cost normal for white-label engineering work?

Markups in the 1.5x to 2.5x range on the raw subcontractor cost are common and defensible when they're built from named components — scoping time, project management, a revision buffer, and margin — rather than picked as a round number. What matters isn't the multiplier itself, it's whether you can account for what it covers if a client asks.

### Should I ever show the client the raw subcontractor invoice?

Generally no — present a single bundled, outcome-based number rather than a cost breakdown, since itemizing invites the client to negotiate your margin directly rather than evaluating the deliverable. The exception is a sophisticated client who explicitly asks how the number was built; with that client, transparency about structure builds more trust than an opaque figure.

### How do I know if my margin is too thin to be sustainable?

If your margin doesn't cover your actual scoping, communication, and revision-handling hours at a realistic hourly rate, you're running the engagement at a loss even if the invoice technically clears above your subcontractor cost. Track your actual hours on two or three engagements and compare against your stated margin to find out.

### What if the client directly compares my price to a traditional agency quote?

That comparison works in your favor if you frame it clearly: traditional agencies typically quote €20,000 to €500,000+ for comparable scope because they rebuild from scratch, while a white-label engagement built on a fixed-cost subcontracted partner delivers the same production outcome at a fraction of that cost and timeline.

### Does the pricing method change for a repeat client versus a new one?

The underlying cost-plus structure stays the same, but the presentation can shift — repeat clients who've already seen your delivery quality typically need less market-anchoring and respond well to a straightforward fixed-tier number, since the trust-building work happened on the first engagement.
