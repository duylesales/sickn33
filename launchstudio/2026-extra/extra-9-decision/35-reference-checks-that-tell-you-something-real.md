Anouk had done everything right. Three shortlisted partners, two reference calls each, a scoring sheet. Every referee said the same six things: responsive, professional, good communication, delivered on time, would work with them again, really nice people. She hung up from the sixth call knowing precisely as much as she had before the first, signed with the partner whose founder she'd liked most, and spent month three discovering that the two senior engineers she'd met during sales had been rotated onto a larger account in week two.

The problem wasn't that the referees lied. They didn't. The problem is that a reference call, run the normal way, is structurally incapable of producing information: the vendor chooses who you speak to, the referee has agreed to be helpful, and the questions — "were you happy with the work?" — have exactly one socially available answer. You have to redesign the whole exercise before it can tell you anything.

## Ask for Specific Kinds of References, Not "References"

The single highest-yield change happens before any call. Instead of "can you send me two references," send this:

> Could you put me in touch with three clients: one from a project that ran into trouble or over schedule, one from at least eighteen months ago who's still running what you built, and one where the scope changed significantly mid-project?

Three things now happen, and all of them are useful.

First, you learn something from the response itself. A partner who has been doing this for years has had projects go sideways — that's not a defect, it's arithmetic. One who says "we've never had a project run into trouble" is either new or not being straight with you. One who says "let me ask Bram whether he's willing to talk about the Q2 project, it got difficult in the middle" has just told you they're comfortable being examined.

Second, the eighteen-month reference is the one nobody thinks to ask for and the one that matters most to a scale-up. A client three weeks post-launch can tell you the delivery was smooth. A client eighteen months post-launch can tell you whether the code was maintainable, whether another developer could pick it up, whether the thing has fallen over, and what the running costs turned out to be.

Third, the scope-change reference gets you to the commercial question that actually predicts your experience: what happens when reality diverges from the plan, which it will.

## Find Two References Nobody Nominated

Vendor-supplied references are a filtered sample. Balance them with one or two you sourced yourself — this is normal diligence at any size and takes about forty minutes.

- Their portfolio and case-study pages name clients. Those clients have staff on LinkedIn.
- Public repositories, commit histories and open-source contributions reveal who actually worked on what.
- Dutch and EU B2B review platforms, plus a plain search for the company name alongside words like "handover", "invoice" or "contract", occasionally surface something.
- If the vendor is an agency, ask your own network: the Benelux software services market is small enough that two degrees of separation usually exists.

A cold outreach note that works, because it's short and asks for very little:

> Hi [name] — I'm evaluating [vendor] for a similar piece of work and saw you worked with them on [project]. Would you have ten minutes for two or three honest questions? Happy to return the favour any time, and nothing you say goes back to them.

Roughly one in three people say yes, and the ones who do are markedly more candid than nominated referees, because they owe the vendor nothing.

## The Call Script: Ask About Events, Not Feelings

The design principle is simple. Questions about feelings ("were you satisfied?") get you a social answer. Questions about **specific past events** get you a narrative, and narratives contain facts the referee didn't plan to give you. Every question below is past tense and asks for something that happened.

Open by taking the pressure off: *"I'm not looking for a verdict — I'm trying to understand what working with them is actually like, including the boring parts. Anything unhelpful I'll just discard."*

Then:

1. **"Walk me through the week the project was most at risk. What happened, and what did they do?"**
2. **"What ended up being your job that you'd assumed would be theirs?"**
3. **"How did the final invoice compare to the original quote, and what caused the difference?"**
4. **"Who actually did the work — was it the people you met during sales, and did the team change during the project?"**
5. **"Think of the worst bug you reported. How long from reporting to fixed?"**
6. **"What did they say no to?"**
7. **"After the project closed, you needed something. What happened?"**
8. **"Six months later, could another developer pick up what they built? Did anyone try?"**
9. **"If you were signing the contract again, what one thing would you write differently?"**
10. **"Who else were you considering, and why did you choose them?"**
11. **"Would you hire them again for the same work? Would you hire them for something twice the size?"**
12. **"What haven't I asked that I should have?"**

Question 6 is the sleeper. A partner who never said no to anything either didn't understand the brief or planned to renegotiate later; a referee who says *"they refused to build the reporting module the way we asked and explained why, and they were right"* has just described the single most valuable property in an engineering partner.

Question 11 is deliberately two questions. "Would you hire them again" gets a yes almost universally. The gap between the two answers is where the real assessment lives — *"for the same work absolutely, for something twice the size I'd probably want a bigger team"* is a precise and honest statement about capacity that no one would have volunteered.

Question 12 gets used at the end of every good diligence call in every industry, and it works because by minute twenty the referee has stopped performing.

## Reading the Answers

You're listening for three things.

**The specificity gradient.** Genuine experience produces detail without effort: names, dates, amounts, a particular Tuesday. When a referee's answers stay abstract across several questions, either the project was smaller than presented or their involvement was peripheral. Ask directly and kindly: "How closely were you working with them day to day?"

**The hedge.** "To be fair," "in fairness to them," "I mean, they weren't the problem" — these phrases nearly always precede or follow the thing the referee is trying not to say. Don't pounce. Stay quiet for three seconds and they'll usually keep talking.

**Problem plus resolution.** The best possible reference is not a glowing one. It's someone who describes a genuine failure and then how it was handled: *"They underestimated the migration by about two weeks. They told us in week two rather than week five, gave us the choice of cutting scope or paying for the overrun, and didn't argue when we picked the first one."* That answer tells you what happens when things go wrong, which is the only thing you actually needed to learn.

## What References Cannot Tell You

Calibrate your expectations, because over-weighting references produces its own errors.

A reference describes a specific team on a specific project at a specific time. In an agency of any size, the people are the product, and the people who did that project may be busy. Hence question 4 — and hence a follow-up worth asking the vendor directly: *"Name the two people who'd do my work, and tell me what else they're on for the next six weeks."*

References also can't validate technical quality. A delighted client with no engineer on staff cannot tell you whether the database schema will hold at 10,000 users. Reference checks assess reliability, communication and commercial behaviour; a code review — yours, or an independent developer's — assesses the engineering. Do both, and don't let a strong reference substitute for looking at the work.

## For a Scale-Up, Add the Operating Questions

If you already have paying customers, your risk profile isn't "will it get built" — it's "what happens at 3am on a Sunday." Three additional questions, asked of the eighteen-month reference specifically:

- **"Has the system had an outage since launch, and what did they do?"** You're listening for whether a human answered, how fast, and whether anything was learned.
- **"When you asked for a change eight months later, how long did it take them to remember how it worked?"** This is a documentation question in disguise. A partner who needs two weeks to re-orient in their own codebase left you a codebase nobody can maintain.
- **"How much of what they built have you since replaced?"** A small amount is healthy. A lot, quickly, means it didn't fit.

Finally, verify the referee exists: a real name, a company email domain that matches the client, camera on if it's a video call. Fabricated references are rare but not unheard of, and the check costs nothing.

## Real example

### A Scale-Up Founder in Action: The Reference Who Mentioned the Rotation

Joris Kamphuis, founder of a 340-customer field-service SaaS in Almere, was choosing between two partners to take over payment infrastructure and multi-tenant data isolation on a product his team had originally shipped from a Bolt prototype. Both had polished proposals within €1,400 of each other after normalisation.

He asked each for a reference from a project that had gone wrong. One partner sent another happy recent client instead; the other put him in touch with a founder whose migration had run three weeks long. On that call, Joris asked question 4 — who actually did the work — and learned that the lead engineer had been swapped out at week two, but that the partner had flagged it in advance and kept the second engineer on the project through to handover eleven months later. He also learned, from question 8, that a new developer had picked up the codebase in a day and a half using the written architecture note.

**Result:** Joris hired the second partner, wrote a named-engineer clause into the agreement based on what he'd heard, and shipped tenant isolation and Stripe subscription billing in fourteen working days — with the same two engineers throughout.

> *"The reference who told me about the engineer swap is the reason I hired them. The one who sent me another happy client is the reason I didn't hire the other."*
> — **Joris Kamphuis, Founder, Veldwerk Suite (Almere)**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many reference calls are enough before deciding?

Two vendor-supplied and one you found yourself is a sound baseline for an engagement in the low thousands; add the eighteen-month reference for anything you'll depend on operationally. Beyond four, answers start repeating and you're better off spending the time on a technical review of the actual code.

### Is contacting a vendor's clients without asking them first inappropriate?

Contacting people in public roles at public clients is ordinary diligence, and most buyers do it. What's not appropriate is implying the vendor put you in touch, or repeating what you heard back to the vendor in a way that identifies the source.

### What if a partner is genuinely new and doesn't have eighteen-month references?

Then say so and adjust: ask for the longest-running client they have, ask what the individual engineers did before this company, and consider a small paid pilot instead of a large first engagement. Newness isn't disqualifying, but it should change the structure of the deal rather than be waved through.

### A reference gave one mildly negative answer. How much weight should that carry?

Usually less than you'd think, and it's often a positive signal about the referee's honesty rather than the vendor's competence. Weight patterns, not incidents: one referee mentioning a slow response is noise, three referees independently describing the same behaviour is a finding.

### Should I ask for references in writing instead of calls to save time?

Written references are almost always useless, because the effort of typing pushes people toward safe generalities. Ten minutes on a phone call with the hedge-listening technique produces more than any amount of email, and it's faster for the referee too.
