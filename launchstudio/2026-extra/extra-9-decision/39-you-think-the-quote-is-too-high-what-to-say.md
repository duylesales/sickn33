Here's the email most founders draft and then delete:

> Hi Mark, thanks for the quote. To be honest it's quite a bit more than I was expecting for what I thought was a fairly small job. Is there any flexibility on the price?

It gets deleted because it feels rude, and then a worse thing happens: the founder says nothing at all for nine days, the vendor assumes they've lost the deal, and a project that could have worked at €2,400 dies at €4,100 without either party ever discovering that.

The email isn't rude. It's just badly constructed, because it asks for a discount rather than a solution — and a discount is the one thing you should least want. A vendor who knocks 20% off without changing anything has told you the original number was arbitrary, and someone still has to absorb that 20% somewhere you can't see. What you actually want is a different *shape* of deal at a price you can pay.

## First, Work Out Which "Too High" This Is

The phrase covers four completely different situations, and each needs a different sentence.

**Too high versus my budget.** You have €2,000 and the quote says €5,200. This is a constraint, not a disagreement — and it's the easiest one to solve, because it's the only one where naming the number helps you.

**Too high versus another quote.** You've got €5,200 and €2,900 for what look like the same words. That's usually a scope difference rather than a price difference, and asking for a discount will get you the expensive vendor's scope at a middling price rather than clarity about the gap.

**Too high versus what I expected.** You had a number in your head from a blog post or a friend. Your expectation might be wrong, or the quote might be — but it's an information problem, not a negotiation.

**Too high versus what it's worth to me.** The product might genuinely not justify €5,200 yet. That's a legitimate conclusion, and the right response is to buy less, not to buy the same thing cheaper.

Say which one it is out loud. Vendors respond very differently to "I have €2,000" than to "that seems like a lot," and the first gets you a real conversation within a day.

## Six Scripts You Can Send As Written

**1. The budget-first script** (for a constraint)

> Thanks — the scope looks right, but my realistic budget for this phase is €2,000. Rather than asking you to discount, could you tell me what you'd deliver for €2,000, what you'd leave out, and which of the excluded items you'd be nervous about launching without?

This is the single most useful sentence in the entire process. It respects the vendor's pricing, it hands them the design problem, and the last clause gets you a risk opinion for free. Naming a number does cost you a little leverage; at these amounts, the speed and honesty you buy in exchange is worth more.

**2. The tiering script** (when you don't know what things should cost)

> Could you send this as three options: (a) the minimum you'd be comfortable launching with, (b) what you'd recommend, and (c) everything we discussed? With a one-line note on what the risk is at each level.

Three prices teach you the shape of the cost curve. You'll usually find one expensive item is driving most of the total, and that item is often something you can defer.

**3. The driver script** (when the number is surprising)

> Which two items make up the biggest share of this, and what makes them expensive? I'm not pushing back, I'm trying to understand where the work is.

A good answer sounds like: *"Payments is about 40% of it, because a subscription flow isn't just a checkout button — it's failed payments, cancellations, refunds, and making sure the server confirms a payment rather than the browser. And the multi-user permissions are another 25%, because right now any logged-in user can read every other user's records."* Now you know something. A bad answer restates the line items in a different order.

**4. The phasing script** (when you need revenue before you can fund the rest)

> Can we split this so phase one gets me live and taking money, and phase two happens once there's revenue? What's the smallest phase one that isn't reckless?

Sequencing genuinely reduces cost, unlike discounting, because it lets some work be informed by real usage instead of guesses. Watch out for one thing: ask whether phase one plus phase two later costs more in total than doing both now, and by how much. A little more is normal; a lot more means the split is artificial.

**5. The scope-trade script** (when you have time but not money)

> What could I take off your plate to reduce this — writing the content, setting up the accounts, doing the manual testing myself, handling support in the first weeks?

Sometimes real. Often less than you'd hope, because supervising a client's work isn't free. But the honest version of this answer is diagnostic: a vendor who says *"content and account setup, sure — testing no, because if you find a bug in week three I'm debugging it anyway"* is thinking about the actual work rather than about closing you.

**6. The walk-away script** (keep the door open)

> This is more than I can commit right now, so I'm going to hold off. If it's useful, the number that would work for me at this stage is around €X — and if there's a version at that level I'd genuinely like to hear it. Either way, thank you for the detail in the proposal.

Roughly a third of the time this produces a workable counter-offer within two days. It also costs you nothing socially, which matters in a market as small as the Benelux, where you'll meet these people again.

## What Not to Say

**"My nephew says this is a week's work."** It might well be a week's work — for the coding. It isn't for the testing, the payment edge cases, the deployment, the handover and the accountability. This sentence signals that you'll relitigate every estimate, and good vendors quietly deprioritise you.

**"Another agency quoted half."** Fine to mention, but on its own it just invites a matched price on an unmatched scope. Send the competing *scope* instead and ask them to price it or explain the difference — you'll learn far more.

**"Can you do it for a share of revenue?"** At €800–€7,500, almost never worth it for either side. You're offering an uncertain future payment in place of a small certain one, taking on a partner with an opinion about your product, and complicating your cap table or accounts for the sake of a few thousand euros.

**"Just give me your best price."** This asks the vendor to bid against themselves. You'll get either a small token reduction or a quietly diminished scope.

## How to Read the Response

**Good responses trade scope for price and name the consequence.** *"€2,000 gets you auth, server-side permissions and deployment. That leaves out subscription billing — you'd take payments manually for now — and there'd be no staging environment, so early fixes go straight to the live site. I'd accept both of those; I wouldn't cut the permissions work."*

**Bad response: the free discount.** Same scope, 20% less, no explanation. Either the first number was invented, or something is about to be quietly removed. Ask directly: "what changed in the work?"

**Bad response: the invisible cut.** The price drops and so do testing, documentation and the support window — the three things that never announce themselves and always cost you later. Compare the revised document to the original line by line rather than reading the new total and feeling relieved.

**Bad response: the ultimatum.** "This price is only valid until Friday." Deadline pressure on a four-figure engagement is a sales technique, not a capacity constraint.

## Calibration: Is It Actually High?

Some sense of the market, so "too high" can be a fact rather than a feeling. For taking an AI-generated prototype to production — security, auth, payments, database, hosting — traditional agencies quote €20,000 to €500,000 and usually want to rebuild your frontend, which is where most of that money goes. Independent freelancers land between €5,000 and €20,000 with wide quality variance and a real risk they've never read AI-generated code before. Focused last-mile work, keeping the frontend you built, runs roughly €800–€3,500 fixed for a launch-ready scope, at one to three weeks.

Then add the part nobody quotes: €20–€80 per month in running costs for hosting, database, transactional email and monitoring once you're live. A quote that ignores this isn't cheaper, it's just quieter.

Three signs a specific number is genuinely too high for what's on offer: the scope includes a rebuild of a frontend that already works; the price is expressed in hours with no verifiable outcomes attached; or nothing in the proposal demonstrates that anyone read your code, which means the number is a guess with a margin for being wrong built into it.

And one sign you should pay the higher number anyway: the expensive vendor found a real problem during scoping that the cheap one didn't mention. Someone who identified that your payment amount comes from the browser, or that your database has no permission rules, has already demonstrated the thing you're paying for.

## The Version of This That Works

Say which "too high" you mean. Name your number if it's a constraint. Ask for tiers rather than a discount. Read the response for whether scope moved with the price. And treat a vendor's willingness to say *"below this level I wouldn't launch"* as the most valuable sentence in the exchange — it's the one that tells you they're thinking about your outcome rather than your signature.

## Real example

### A Founder in Action: The €4,900 Quote That Became a €1,900 Phase One

Wouter Klaassen, a former ski instructor running a small tour business from Amersfoort, built PisteMate in Bolt — a booking and group-chat tool for ski trip organisers — and received a €4,900 quote three weeks before the winter season opened. He had €2,200.

His first draft email asked whether there was flexibility on price. He deleted it and sent the budget-first script instead, naming €2,200 and asking what would be excluded and what the vendor would be nervous about. The reply came the same afternoon: for €1,900 he could have server-side booking permissions, deployment and a hardened signup, launching with Mollie payment links sent manually rather than an automated subscription flow. The vendor added one line — *"I'd cut the automated billing without hesitation; I wouldn't cut the permissions, because right now any organiser can open any other group's trip."*

Wouter took phase one, ran the season on manual payment links with forty-one bookings, and commissioned automated billing in April with revenue in hand.

**Result:** PisteMate launched eight working days later for €1,900, took its first booking within a week, and added automated subscription billing four months later for a further €1,400 — €3,300 total, spread across two seasons.

> *"I nearly walked away over a number. One honest email got me a version I could afford and a straight answer about which corner I wasn't allowed to cut."*
> — **Wouter Klaassen, Founder, PisteMate (Amersfoort)**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is naming my budget a mistake because they'll just quote exactly that?

Some will, and at four-figure amounts that's a fair trade for the speed and honesty you get back. The protection isn't secrecy — it's asking what's excluded and what they'd be nervous about omitting, which reveals whether the number was designed around your constraint or just matched to it.

### What if I genuinely can't afford even the minimum tier?

Then wait or reduce further, and be careful about which corner gets cut. Launching without a staging environment or automated billing is uncomfortable but survivable; launching without server-side permissions on a multi-user app means one curious customer can read everyone else's data.

### Is it acceptable to go back after accepting a quote and ask for a reduction?

It damages trust badly and rarely works. If your circumstances changed, say exactly that and ask to defer part of the scope rather than to re-price agreed work — the first is a business conversation, the second reads as an attempt to renegotiate after the fact.

### Do vendors expect negotiation, the way a car dealer would?

Mostly no, at this end of the market. Small fixed-price engineering quotes are usually built from an estimate plus a modest risk buffer, so there isn't a hidden 30% to give away — which is exactly why scope conversations get results where discount requests don't.

### How long should I wait for a response before assuming they're not interested?

Two business days for a substantive reply is reasonable, and a same-day acknowledgement is common from teams with capacity. If a vendor takes a week to answer a pricing question while trying to win your business, that's your first data point about response times after they have it.
