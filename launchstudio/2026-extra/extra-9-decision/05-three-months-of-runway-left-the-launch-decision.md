It's 01:40 and you have two tabs open. One is your bank account: €14,200. The other is a spreadsheet where you have typed your monthly outgoings four different ways trying to make the answer come out differently, and it keeps saying the same thing — a little under three months.

Somewhere in the middle of that is a prototype that works when you demo it and isn't safe to hand to strangers, and a quote for a few thousand euros to change that. The question you are actually turning over at 01:40 is not "is this a good product." It is: *do I spend a fifth of everything I have left on making it launchable, or do I hold the cash and hope?*

That question has a structure. Here it is.

## First: Get the Real Number, Not the Comfortable One

Before any branch, fix the denominator, because most founders at this stage are working from a runway figure that is quietly optimistic.

Count everything that leaves your account monthly, including the parts you mentally exclude: the tooling subscriptions, the accountant, your own drawings or the salary you're not taking but eventually must, the domain and hosting, the VAT you owe next quarter but have already spent. Then subtract any money that is committed but not yet spent — the deposit, the conference ticket.

Then apply the honest cut: whatever your spreadsheet says, you have about two weeks less. Something always arrives.

Call it twelve weeks. Twelve weeks is not "three months" in the loose sense; it is roughly sixty working days, and any plan you make from here has to fit inside sixty working days with something to show at the end. If you cannot name what "something to show" means for you specifically — revenue, a signed pilot, an investor conversation with real usage data behind it — stop here and name it, because every branch below routes through it.

## Branch One: Is There Someone Who Would Pay You This Month?

Not "would people find this valuable." Not "I have 400 people on a waiting list." A specific human or company, with a name, who has said something close to *yes, I'd pay for that* — and who you could email tomorrow.

**If yes**, you are not making a launch decision. You are making a *conversion* decision, and the calculation is comparatively easy: what is between that person and paying you, and what does removing it cost? This is the strongest position to be in at twelve weeks, because the spend has a named payer attached to it. Go to branch two.

**If no**, spending on production hardening is almost certainly the wrong use of your remaining cash, and this is the point most worth being blunt about. Hardening converts a working prototype into a sellable product; it does not create demand. If nobody has said yes yet, the constraint isn't your backend. Spend the next three weeks on twenty sales conversations using the prototype you already have — a demo you drive yourself, on a screen you control, needs none of this work — and come back to this article when someone says yes. Twenty conversations cost you nothing but time, and they will either produce branch one or produce the answer that saves you from spending your last €14,000 on the wrong thing.

## Branch Two: What Is Actually Between Them and Paying?

Founders skip this step and buy "production readiness" as a bundle. At twelve weeks you cannot afford a bundle. You need the specific blocker.

Ask the person who said yes what they need before money moves. The answers cluster into four, and they cost very different amounts:

**"I need to be able to give my team logins."** This is accounts plus the boundary between them — the €250 user-accounts line, and more importantly the €500 security work that makes those accounts mean something. Real, and mid-priced.

**"I need to know my data is safe here."** Sometimes this is a genuine security requirement; often it is a request for a document — a privacy policy, a processing agreement, a plain-English page describing where data lives. Ask which. One costs a few thousand euros, the other costs an afternoon and a template.

**"I need to pay you by card / on a subscription."** Frequently the most expensive answer, and the most avoidable. Ask whether they'd pay an invoice instead. Dutch and EU B2B customers almost always will. This one answer can move a project from €3,000 to €1,200.

**"I need it to not fall over."** Hosting, deployment, a domain that isn't a preview URL, and someone to call. The €200 deployment line plus the €49/month support layer.

Write down which of these you actually face. If it is one, you are looking at €800–€2,000. If it is three, you are looking at €2,500–€3,500. The bundle price and the blocker price are frequently a factor of two apart, and at twelve weeks that factor is the whole decision.

## Branch Three: Does It Fit the Two Constraints?

Now test the quote against both walls at once.

**The cash wall.** A defensible ceiling at twelve weeks is about 20–25% of remaining runway on a single engineering spend. At €14,200, that is €2,800–€3,500 — which, not coincidentally, is exactly where the Launch Ready band tops out. Above 25%, you are shortening your own decision window to buy a product you then have too little time to sell.

**The time wall.** Whatever you buy has to be live with at least six weeks of selling left. A 1–3 week fixed-price delivery starting now leaves you eight or nine weeks. A three-month agency engagement does not fit inside your runway at all, at any price — which is why the €20,000+ quotes aren't really an option you're declining, they're an option you don't have.

If the quote clears both walls, take it. If it clears neither, go to the cutting section below. If it clears one, negotiate the scope until it clears both — a fixed-price scope is a conversation about what's in it, and a good one will tell you what to remove.

## The 20% Rule, and Why It Isn't Arbitrary

Twenty percent is not a superstition. It is what leaves you enough to survive the gap between launching and being paid.

Work it through. Spend €2,800 of €14,200 and you have €11,400, which at your burn is roughly nine and a half weeks. Your product goes live in two, leaving seven and a half weeks to sell, invoice, and — this is the part founders forget — actually receive money. EU B2B payment terms of 30 days are normal. An invoice sent in week four is money in the account in week eight. That fits, barely.

Spend €6,000 instead and you have €8,200, about six and a half weeks, minus two to build, leaving four and a half weeks to sell and collect. A 30-day invoice sent in week three lands after you've run out. The product would be finished and the company wouldn't.

That is the real reason for the ceiling: not the size of the spend, but the collection lag on the other side of it. Any spend that pushes your remaining weeks below "delivery time plus selling time plus payment terms" is a spend that cannot pay for itself in time, regardless of how good the work is.

## What to Cut to Make It Fit

If the number doesn't fit, cut scope before you cut quality — and cut it in this order.

**Cut payments first.** Invoice manually. Ten customers invoiced by hand is two hours a month of your time and saves €400 plus the surrounding state-machine work, which is the single largest removable block in most quotes.

**Cut email automation.** Send welcome and confirmation emails yourself from your own inbox until it stops being feasible. At under fifty customers it is entirely feasible, and it makes your onboarding better, not worse.

**Cut features that hold sensitive data.** If your product has a module that stores documents or health information and another that doesn't, launch the second one. Sensitive data is the thing that makes hardening expensive; removing it from scope removes the cost with it.

**Cut the second user type.** Products that serve two sides — providers and clients, employers and applicants — cost roughly twice as much to secure. Launch one side and operate the other manually.

**Do not cut the boundary between users.** This is the floor. If your product holds anything one customer would object to another seeing, that work stays in scope even if everything else goes. There is no cheap version of it, only an absent one, and at twelve weeks of runway you cannot absorb the cost of finding out.

## The Two Ways This Goes Wrong

**Spending everything.** The founder who puts €9,000 of €14,000 into a beautiful, fully hardened product and launches with three weeks of cash. They are not launching a company; they are launching a lottery ticket with a two-week window to hit. The product is usually fine. The timing never is.

**Spending nothing.** The founder who protects the balance, keeps deciding, and reaches week ten with €5,000 and the same unlaunched prototype. This one is more common and feels more responsible, which is why it is the more dangerous of the two. Deciding is not free — it costs a week of runway per week, and at the end of it you have neither the money nor the product.

Both failures come from the same place: treating cash as the only variable. Cash and weeks deplete together, and the only thing that converts either into more of itself is a live product with a customer attached.

## If the Tree Says Don't Launch

Sometimes it does, and the honest options at that point are real ones rather than failure.

Sell the pilot before you build it: a paid pilot at a discount, contract signed, delivery in six weeks, with the customer's payment funding the hardening. This is the strongest outcome available at twelve weeks and it is more achievable than it sounds — customers who want your product often prefer paying early to waiting.

Or extend the runway: take contract work for six weeks, cut your own drawings, or reduce burn to buy the time to do this properly. Or raise a small, unromantic amount from people who already know you, on the strength of a specific plan — "€10,000, live in three weeks, first invoices in week six" is a far more fundable sentence than "€50,000 to keep going."

What is not an option is continuing to deliberate while the number falls. At twelve weeks, the decision itself has a price, and it comes out of the same account.

## Real example

### A Founder in Action: The Question That Cut the Quote in Half

Ilse Beekman had eleven weeks of runway and a €3,600 quote when she stopped to work out what her one interested customer actually needed. She had built Zorgrooster in Lovable — a scheduling tool for small home-care teams in Zwolle — and a regional care provider had told her they'd take it for four teams if it was "properly set up."

She asked what "properly set up" meant. The answer was three things: individual logins for their coordinators, certainty that one team's client schedules weren't visible to another team, and an invoice she could send to their finance department. Card payments, which accounted for a large part of the original quote, were not on the list — their procurement department preferred an invoice on 30-day terms and said so plainly.

The revised scope removed the payment integration and the automated email sequence entirely, and put everything into accounts, team-scoped authorization enforced at the database rather than in the interface, and deployment on Zorgrooster's own domain with SSL. €1,750 against €3,600 — under 15% of Ilse's remaining cash rather than a third of it.

**Result:** Zorgrooster went live in nine business days, the care provider signed for four teams in week three, and Ilse invoiced €2,400 for the first quarter — money in the account in week seven, with five weeks of runway still behind it. She added Mollie payments four months later, funded by revenue.

> *"The quote wasn't wrong. It was for a product I couldn't afford yet. Asking my one customer what they actually needed turned out to be the cheapest thing I did that month."*
> — **Ilse Beekman, Founder, Zorgrooster (Zwolle)**

**Cost & Timeline:** €1,750 (Launch Ready Package, accounts, team-scoped authorization and deployment) — live in 9 business days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is 20% of remaining runway a real rule or just a rough guide?

It is a guide derived from a hard constraint: you need enough weeks left after launching to sell and to actually be paid, and EU B2B payment terms of 30 days mean collection lags invoicing by a month. The percentage matters less than the arithmetic behind it, which is delivery time plus selling time plus payment terms against your remaining weeks.

### What if I have a waiting list but nobody has explicitly agreed to pay?

A waiting list is interest, not a named payer, and interest converts at rates you cannot predict from your last €14,000. Three weeks of direct conversations that turn one waiting-list name into a stated yes will de-risk the spend more than any amount of engineering will.

### Can I ask a customer to pay for the work that makes the product ready for them?

Frequently, yes, and it is the strongest position available at low runway. A discounted paid pilot with a signed contract and a delivery date in six weeks converts your customer's budget into your engineering budget, and customers who genuinely want the product usually prefer that to waiting indefinitely.

### Which single item should never be cut to fit a low-runway budget?

The boundary between users. If your product holds data one customer would object to another seeing, that work stays regardless of what else goes, because there is no reduced version of it — only its absence, discovered later at a cost a twelve-week runway cannot absorb.

### Does a shorter runway mean I should choose a cheaper provider rather than a smaller scope?

Usually the opposite. Reducing scope keeps quality intact on a smaller surface, while reducing price at constant scope tends to buy partially finished work across several categories, which at low runway is the one outcome you cannot afford to discover after launch.
