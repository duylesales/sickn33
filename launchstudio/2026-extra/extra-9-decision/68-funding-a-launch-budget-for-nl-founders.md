You don't need €50,000 to launch. You need something in the €800–€7,500 range to get an AI-built prototype production-ready, and there are more ways to find that amount than most non-technical founders realize, ranging from tax credits you may already qualify for without knowing it, to microcredit designed specifically for people banks won't lend to, to simply saving up over a few months without touching any of it. This is a factual walk-through of the main routes available to founders in the Netherlands, with one consistent caveat repeated throughout: every program below changes its terms, eligibility, and amounts over time, and the only reliable source for current details is the official body running it — treat everything here as a starting map, not a final answer.

## Start With What You Actually Need

Before exploring funding routes, it's worth sitting with the actual number, because it's smaller than the funding conversation usually implies. A scoped production-readiness engagement for an AI-built prototype typically runs €800–€3,500 for foundational fixes (security, working payments, working data, tested deployment), or €2,500–€7,500 plus roughly €49/month if ongoing managed hosting and support are included. That's a meaningfully different funding problem than raising a seed round — it's closer to "can I find a few thousand euros over the next month or two" than "can I convince an investor to bet on my company." Reframing the question this way changes which of the routes below are actually proportionate to reach for, and rules out some heavier options (like formal investment) that carry costs — equity, board involvement, reporting obligations — disproportionate to what's actually needed at this stage.

## WBSO: The R&D Tax Credit Most Founders Don't Know They Qualify For

WBSO (Wet Bevordering Speur- en Ontwikkelingswerk) is a Dutch tax credit scheme that reduces the wage tax and social security contributions a company pays on hours spent on qualifying research and development work — including, in many cases, software development work that meets the scheme's definition of technically novel development. It's administered by RVO (the Netherlands Enterprise Agency), and founders — including sole proprietors and small companies, not just larger R&D departments — can apply for hours spent building genuinely new technical functionality. The practical relevance here is narrower than it might sound: WBSO reduces the cost of development labor you're already paying for (your own time, if structured correctly, or a contractor's), rather than providing a cash grant toward a fixed-price service purchase, and whether a specific project qualifies depends on RVO's assessment of what counts as qualifying R&D versus routine development work. It's genuinely worth checking your eligibility directly on RVO's website or through an accountant familiar with the scheme, because eligibility rules, credit percentages, and application deadlines change and are set centrally — nothing in this article should be treated as a substitute for that direct check.

## Regional and Provincial Innovation Subsidies

Beyond the national WBSO scheme, most Dutch provinces and several regional development agencies (ROMs — regional development corporations, such as those covering the greater Amsterdam area, the eastern Netherlands, and others) run their own innovation subsidy and sometimes co-investment programs aimed at early-stage companies within their region. These vary considerably by province in what they fund, how much, and under what conditions — some are grants toward specific development costs, others are co-investment funds that take a small equity or convertible-note stake rather than a straightforward grant. Because these programs are regional, the right first step is identifying which ROM or provincial economic development body covers your registered business address and checking their current open programs directly, since availability and terms shift with government budget cycles and aren't standardized nationally the way WBSO is. This is a genuinely useful category for founders willing to spend a few hours researching rather than assuming a single national program covers everyone — the money is real, but it takes some searching to find the specific regional scheme that applies to your situation.

## Qredits: Microcredit for Founders Who Can't Get a Bank Loan

Qredits is a Dutch nonprofit microcredit organization specifically set up to lend to small businesses and starting entrepreneurs who typically can't access traditional bank financing — a category that includes most solo, pre-revenue, non-technical founders with no collateral or track record a conventional bank would lend against. Qredits loans are smaller and structured differently from a bank loan, often including access to coaching or mentorship alongside the credit itself, and the application process is generally built around evaluating a business plan and the founder's ability to execute rather than requiring the collateral a bank would demand. For a founder whose entire funding gap is the €800–€7,500 range needed to get a prototype production-ready, a Qredits loan is proportionate in a way a bank loan or formal investment usually isn't — it's a route worth investigating specifically because it was built for exactly this kind of gap, though current loan amounts, interest rates, and eligibility criteria should always be confirmed directly on Qredits' own site rather than assumed from any general description.

## Angel and Informal Investment: What It Costs You Beyond Money

Angel investors and informal investment from friends, family, or a personal network are a real route for some founders, but it's worth being honest about what this route costs beyond the money itself. Equity given up at the earliest, lowest-valuation stage of a company is disproportionately expensive in the long run compared to the same euro amount raised later at a higher valuation — a founder who gives up a meaningful equity stake to fund a €3,000 hardening engagement is trading a large future percentage of the company for a comparatively small, short-term need that other routes could cover without dilution. Informal investment from friends or family carries a different but equally real cost: a failed or underperforming product doesn't just cost money in that scenario, it costs a personal relationship, and that risk deserves the same seriousness as any formal financing decision, not less scrutiny just because the paperwork is simpler. Angel investment makes more sense when the funding need is substantially larger than a launch budget — hiring a team, sustained runway, meaningful marketing spend — than when the actual gap is a few thousand euros to get an existing prototype safely into production.

## Bootstrapping: Why It's Achievable Without Any of the Above

It's worth stating plainly: for many founders, the most straightforward route to a €2,000–€3,500 launch budget is simply saving toward it over a few months, without touching any formal funding mechanism at all. This isn't a consolation option — it's frequently the fastest and least complicated path, because it avoids application timelines (WBSO and regional subsidies both involve real processing time), avoids equity dilution, and avoids debt. The tradeoff is purely about timing: bootstrapping takes as long as it takes to save the amount, while the other routes trade application effort and, in some cases, equity or repayment obligations, for potentially faster access to the money. For a founder with any existing income or savings runway, comparing "three months of saving €1,000/month" against "an application process of uncertain timeline and outcome" is a genuinely reasonable comparison to make explicitly rather than assuming external funding is automatically the faster or better route.

## What to Verify Before You Apply to Anything

Every program described above changes over time — amounts, eligibility criteria, application windows, and even whether a program exists at all shift with government and organizational budget cycles, sometimes year to year. Before investing time in any application, verify directly: WBSO details through RVO's official site, regional subsidies through your specific province or ROM's current program listings, and Qredits terms through their own site rather than a summary. None of these should be applied for based on a description in an article, including this one — the application itself is where current, binding terms live, and a program's rules today may not match what was true even a few months ago.

## A Sequencing Strategy: Launch First, Apply Second

For most non-technical founders with an AI-built prototype and a genuine €800–€7,500 gap, a reasonable sequencing is to treat funding research and the launch itself as parallel tracks rather than a strict dependency — start a WBSO or regional subsidy application if it looks like a fit, while also pricing out what a scoped, fixed-price production-readiness engagement would actually cost, since fixed pricing after a scoping call often turns out to be smaller than the range a founder was mentally budgeting for before getting a real quote. Waiting for a grant or loan to be approved before even getting a quote can add months of delay to a launch that a modest amount of savings, or a Qredits loan proportionate to the actual need, could resolve considerably faster. The funding route matters less than getting an accurate number for what's actually needed first — many founders discover the real gap is smaller, and more fundable through simpler means, than they assumed before asking.

## Combining Routes Instead of Picking Just One

None of these options are mutually exclusive, and for a founder with a slightly larger gap to fund — closer to the €7,500 end of a Launch & Grow engagement, say — combining routes is often more realistic than finding one program that covers the whole amount. A common pattern looks like part savings, part a small Qredits loan sized to the actual remaining gap rather than a larger amount than needed, with a WBSO application running in parallel for a later development phase that genuinely qualifies as R&D rather than the initial production-readiness work itself. The key discipline is sizing each piece to an actual, quoted need rather than over-applying for more than necessary simply because a program exists — a smaller, well-justified Qredits loan is easier to secure and repay than a larger one requested because the exact cost wasn't known yet at application time. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to be a registered company in the Netherlands to apply for WBSO or Qredits?

Generally yes, both are aimed at Dutch-registered businesses including sole proprietorships, but exact registration and eligibility requirements should be confirmed directly with RVO and Qredits respectively, since they can differ by business structure.

### How long does a typical WBSO or regional subsidy application take to resolve?

Timelines vary by program and change over time, but application and decision periods of several weeks to a few months are common for these kinds of schemes — always check the current stated timeline on the administering body's own site before assuming a specific duration.

### Is it realistic to bootstrap an €800–€3,500 launch budget without any external funding?

For many founders with some existing income or modest savings, yes — a few months of setting aside a portion of income often covers this range without the application effort, equity dilution, or debt that other routes involve.

### Should I wait for a grant decision before getting a quote for my production-readiness work?

No — getting a fixed-price quote first tells you the actual size of the gap you're trying to fund, which often turns out smaller than assumed, and pursuing funding and quoting in parallel avoids adding months of delay to a launch that might not need to wait at all.

### Does taking angel investment make sense just to cover a launch hardening budget?

Usually not — equity given up at an early, low valuation for a few thousand euros of funding need is a disproportionately expensive trade compared to bootstrapping, a Qredits loan, or a relevant subsidy, and angel investment tends to make more sense for larger, ongoing funding needs.
