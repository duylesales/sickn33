A SOC 2 Type II report takes a minimum of six months to produce, start to finish, and that's the fast case. The observation period alone — the stretch of time an auditor watches your actual security controls operate in practice, not just exist on paper — is typically three to twelve months by itself, meaning a founder who decides today that they need SOC 2 is realistically looking at close to a year before a report exists, and a first-year cost, including audit fees, compliance tooling, and internal time, that commonly runs €15,000–€40,000 for a small team, sometimes more. That number surprises most founders who've heard "SOC 2" mentioned casually in a sales call, as if it were a form to fill out. It isn't. And the harder, more useful question isn't "how do we get SOC 2" — it's "do we actually need it yet, or does something faster and cheaper satisfy what's really being asked of us right now."

## What a Security Questionnaire Is Actually Checking For

When an enterprise prospect's security team sends a lengthy questionnaire, it's tempting to read it as "they want SOC 2," because SOC 2 is the certification most founders have heard of. In practice, most questionnaires are checking for evidence of specific security practices — encryption in transit and at rest, documented access controls, incident response procedures, employee security training, vendor risk management — and SOC 2 is simply one accepted way to prove those practices exist, not the only one. A buyer's procurement or security team is generally trying to answer "does this vendor take security seriously and can they show it," and for a huge share of mid-market deals, a well-prepared, honest, detailed response to their questionnaire — backed by real documented policies rather than a formal certification — clears that bar. It's only the more sophisticated, typically larger or more regulated buyers (financial services, healthcare, government-adjacent) who treat SOC 2 or ISO 27001 as a hard, non-negotiable gate rather than one acceptable form of evidence among several.

## The Real Timeline: SOC 2 Type I vs. Type II

SOC 2 comes in two meaningfully different flavors, and conflating them is where a lot of founders miscalculate their timeline. A Type I report assesses whether your security controls are designed correctly *at a single point in time* — essentially, a snapshot audit that your policies and systems exist and are configured as described. This can realistically be achieved in six to ten weeks from a standing start, assuming the underlying controls are actually in reasonably good shape already, and it's a real, useful artifact many buyers will accept, at least as a credible interim signal. A Type II report, the one enterprise buyers usually mean when they say "SOC 2" without qualification, assesses whether those same controls operated effectively over an observation period — commonly three months for a first report, six to twelve months for subsequent annual reports — meaning the clock doesn't start until your controls are already in place and being followed consistently, and it doesn't finish until that entire window has elapsed and been audited. A founder targeting Type II should budget close to a year end-to-end: weeks to implement missing controls, three-plus months of observation, then several more weeks for the auditor to complete testing and issue the report.

## ISO 27001: A Different Shape, a Similar Weight

ISO 27001, more common as the expected standard in European and UK enterprise deals than in the US market, follows a different structure but carries a comparably serious timeline and cost. Rather than an observation period proving controls worked over time, ISO 27001 certification requires building a full Information Security Management System (ISMS) — documented risk assessments, a defined scope, a set of policies mapped to the standard's specific control set (Annex A) — followed by a two-stage external audit. For a small SaaS team starting from limited documentation, six to nine months to first certification is a realistic estimate, with first-year costs, including consultant support (frequently used, since ISO 27001's documentation requirements are dense and easy to get wrong without experience) and the certification audit itself, often landing in a similar €15,000–€35,000 range to SOC 2, with ongoing annual surveillance audits after the first certification adding a smaller but recurring cost.

## The Cheaper Interim Answers That Satisfy Most Buyers Sooner

For the substantial majority of scale-up SaaS founders, three interim steps — each achievable in weeks rather than months, and each a fraction of a full certification's cost — satisfy what's actually being asked of them right now, and are worth exhausting before committing to a formal certification track. First, a genuinely well-prepared security questionnaire response, backed by real, current, honestly-described practices rather than aspirational ones — encryption status, access control policies, backup and recovery procedures, incident response steps — answers the majority of what mid-market buyers ask, and preparing one thoroughly, once, creates a reusable template that speeds up every future deal. Second, a third-party penetration test report — a focused, time-boxed engagement (commonly one to two weeks, a few thousand euros for a small application) where a security firm actively attempts to find and document exploitable vulnerabilities in your product — is concrete, credible evidence many buyers accept as a meaningful trust signal on its own, especially paired with evidence the findings were actually fixed. Third, a documented set of core security policies — access control, incident response, data retention, acceptable use, vendor management — written honestly to reflect what you actually do (not aspirational policy theater copied from a template and never followed) demonstrates the kind of operational maturity SOC 2 eventually certifies, months before the formal audit could ever conclude.

## Compliance Automation Tools: What They Actually Save You

If a formal certification does become the right call, the tooling landscape has shifted meaningfully in the last few years in ways that change the cost math worth knowing about before budgeting. Platforms like Vanta, Drata, and Secureframe — typically €5,000–€15,000 a year depending on team size — continuously monitor your actual infrastructure (cloud provider configuration, employee device compliance, access controls) against SOC 2 or ISO 27001 control requirements, flag gaps automatically, and generate much of the evidence an auditor needs, rather than a team manually screenshotting configuration settings and assembling spreadsheets by hand. This doesn't replace the audit itself — you still need an independent auditor to issue the actual report — but it meaningfully compresses the preparation timeline and reduces the ongoing burden of staying in compliance between audits, since gaps get flagged as they appear rather than discovered in a scramble before the next annual review. For a small team, the honest trade-off is worth naming: these tools are a real, recurring cost, and they're most worth adopting once you've decided certification is genuinely necessary, not as a way to "get ready just in case," since the subscription cost accrues whether or not a deal is actually waiting on the outcome.

## When the Full Certification Genuinely Becomes Necessary

None of this argues that SOC 2 or ISO 27001 never matter — for a specific, recognizable set of situations, the full certification is the right investment, and delaying it becomes the more expensive choice. If your target market is concentrated among large enterprises, financial institutions, healthcare organizations, or government-adjacent buyers who treat certification as a genuine hard gate rather than one accepted signal among several, the interim measures above will eventually stop being sufficient, and it's worth starting the certification clock deliberately rather than reactively once a deal is already stalled on it. If you've lost, or come close to losing, more than one qualified deal specifically because a formal certification was a non-negotiable requirement, that's a strong, concrete signal the investment has crossed from "nice to have" to "revenue-blocking." And if your growth strategy specifically targets the segment of the market where SOC 2 or ISO 27001 has become table stakes rather than a differentiator — true in a growing share of B2B SaaS categories — starting early, before it's urgent, avoids the worst version of this timeline: a live deal stalled for a year waiting on an audit that could have already been underway.

## Sequencing the Investment So It Isn't Wasted

The founders who get the most value from SOC 2 or ISO 27001, when they do pursue it, are the ones who've already done the interim work described above, because the policies, access controls, and documented practices built for a strong questionnaire response and a clean pen test report are largely the same underlying controls a formal audit assesses — meaning that work isn't wasted or duplicated, it's the foundation the certification builds on. Founders who skip straight to hiring a compliance consultant and starting a formal audit process without this groundwork often spend the first two months of the engagement building the same basic controls a good interim response would have required anyway, just under audit pressure and at consultant hourly rates rather than at their own pace. Sequencing interim readiness first, formal certification once a specific deal or market segment genuinely requires it, is both cheaper and lower-stress than reversing that order.

It's also worth being honest about who inside a small company actually carries this work, because that person is rarely a dedicated compliance hire. In practice, the founder or a technical lead ends up owning it alongside their existing responsibilities, which is exactly why the interim path — bounded, achievable in weeks, with a clear finish line — tends to actually get completed, while a vague, open-ended "we should get SOC 2 eventually" goal tends to sit unstarted for quarters, precisely because nobody can find a clean block of time to begin something that large without a forcing deadline. Treating readiness as a series of small, scoped deliverables rather than one enormous compliance project is as much a project-management decision as a technical one.

Wouter Dijkstra's expense-management platform, Declareer, had lost one enterprise deal to a competitor with SOC 2 certification, and his instinct was to start a formal Type II audit process immediately, budgeting nearly a year and tens of thousands of euros for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a SOC 2 Type II report really cost a small team in the first year?

Commonly €15,000–€40,000 including audit fees, compliance tooling, and internal or consultant time to implement missing controls, though costs vary based on how much groundwork — documented policies, access controls — already exists before the process starts.

### Is SOC 2 Type I ever a reasonable substitute for Type II?

For some buyers, yes, at least as an interim signal — Type I confirms controls are designed correctly at a point in time and can be achieved in six to ten weeks, considerably faster than Type II's multi-month observation period, though it's a weaker form of evidence than a full Type II report.

### Do European buyers generally prefer ISO 27001 over SOC 2?

There's a meaningful tendency in that direction, especially outside the US, but it's not universal — some European enterprise buyers accept either, or a strong interim security posture, so it's worth clarifying what a specific buyer's procurement team actually requires before committing to one certification track.

### What's the fastest thing I can do this month if a deal is stalled on a security questionnaire?

Commission a focused penetration test and put together documented, honest security policies covering access control, incident response, and data handling — both are achievable in a few weeks and satisfy a large share of what mid-market buyers are actually checking for.

### Can the work done for a pen test and policy documentation be reused if we pursue SOC 2 later?

Yes, largely — the access controls, documented policies, and technical fixes from a strong interim readiness pass form much of the foundation a formal SOC 2 or ISO 27001 audit assesses, so the investment isn't wasted if certification becomes necessary down the line.
