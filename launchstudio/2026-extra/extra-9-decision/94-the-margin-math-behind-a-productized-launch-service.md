Your 40% margin isn't 40%. Almost nobody's is, on productized launch work — not because the pricing math is wrong on paper, but because the number was calculated against the wrong ledger in the first place. Ask most agency owners how much margin they're clearing on a white-label launch engagement and you'll get an answer built from two line items: the client invoice and the subcontractor bill. Subtract one from the other, divide by the invoice, and you get a clean, reassuring percentage. It's also incomplete in a way that quietly determines whether the service is actually profitable or just looks profitable on a spreadsheet that doesn't account for where the owner's Tuesday afternoons went.

This article runs the full ledger — every cost category that belongs in a real margin calculation on a productized launch offer, including the ones that never show up on an invoice because nobody sends themselves a bill for their own time.

## The Two-Line Calculation Everyone Runs

Take a representative engagement: a client's Bolt prototype needs payments, hosting, and security hardening, subcontracted at LaunchStudio's Launch & Grow tier for €4,800. Quoted to the client at €7,500, the two-line math looks clean: €2,700 gross margin, a 36% margin rate against the client invoice. Run this across twelve engagements a year and the spreadsheet says something like €32,400 in annual margin from the productized service line — a number that looks like a legitimately good business, and one that's about to be revealed as significantly overstated the moment you account for what actually happened during those twelve engagements beyond the two invoices.

## The Ledger Everyone Skips: Unbillable Hours

Here's what the two-line calculation leaves out, itemized, because each of these categories is real time an owner or account manager spent that doesn't appear as a cost anywhere in the clean 36% number.

**Scoping and discovery**, run properly per the checklist any responsible agency should use before quoting, takes one to two hours per engagement — reviewing the prototype, testing authentication and permissions, and translating findings into a defensible quote. At even a modest €75/hour internal rate, that's €75–€150 of unlogged cost per project.

**Client communication across the engagement** — the kickoff call, the two or three check-ins during a two-to-three-week build, the questions relayed between client and subcontractor, the status updates nobody explicitly bills for — typically runs three to five hours over the life of a project. At the same internal rate, €225–€375.

**Revision handling.** Client-requested changes during the build are close to universal, not exceptional, and managing them — relaying the request accurately, getting a revised estimate from the subcontractor if the change is material, communicating the impact back to the client — adds another one to three hours depending on how much back-and-forth the specific client generates. €75–€225.

**Project management overhead** that doesn't fit neatly into the categories above: chasing the subcontractor for a status update, chasing the client for a decision they're sitting on, handling the inevitable one project per quarter that runs slightly behind schedule and needs active management to keep the client calm about it. A conservative estimate across a full year of engagements averages another one to two hours per project. €75–€150.

**Invoicing, contracts, and admin** specific to this service line — generating the client contract, chasing payment, reconciling the subcontractor invoice against what was billed. Often overlooked entirely because it feels like background business operations rather than project cost, but it's real time attributable to running this specific service. €50–€100 per engagement.

Summed, the realistic unbillable-hours cost per engagement lands somewhere between €500 and €900 — not a rounding error against a €2,700 gross margin, but somewhere between 18% and 33% of it.

A sixth category worth naming separately, because it's the one agencies most often forget entirely until it happens: **handling the engagement that goes sideways.** Not every project runs cleanly — a client changes their mind on scope mid-build, a subcontractor hits an unexpected technical wrinkle, or a launch reveals a bug that needs urgent post-launch attention outside the support window. Budget for this as a portfolio-level cost rather than a per-project one: assume one in every eight to ten engagements will require meaningfully more owner time than the rest, and fold a small contingency — a percentage point or two of margin — across the whole service line to cover it, rather than being surprised each time it happens as if it were a one-off exception.

## Recalculating the Real Number

Apply the midpoint of that range, roughly €700 per engagement, against the earlier example: €2,700 gross margin minus €700 in unbillable hours leaves €2,000 in actual margin — a real rate of 27%, not the 36% the two-line calculation suggested. Across twelve engagements a year, that's €24,000 in actual annual margin from the service line, not €32,400. The gap, roughly €8,400 a year in this example, isn't lost money exactly — it's money the owner or account manager earned in the form of their own time, which is real compensation, but it's compensation that was invisible in the pricing model and therefore never factored into whether the service line was actually worth running at the price it was quoted.

This matters practically in two ways. First, it changes whether a given markup is actually sustainable — a 20% markup on subcontractor cost that looked thin but survivable in the two-line calculation may be genuinely underwater once unbillable hours are counted honestly, meaning some agencies running this service are effectively paying themselves a low or negative hourly rate for their own project management time without realizing it. Second, it changes how you should price the next tier of complexity — an engagement requiring more client hand-holding, more revision cycles, or a client new to working with agencies at all should carry a materially higher margin built in, not the same markup percentage applied uniformly regardless of how much unbillable time a specific client relationship actually consumes.

## Building Unbillable Hours Into the Price, Not Absorbing Them

The fix isn't heroic time-tracking discipline, though tracking actual hours on three or four engagements to calibrate your own numbers is worth doing once. The fix is pricing the unbillable-hours category explicitly into the cost-plus structure from the start, the same way a [defensible client proposal](https://launchstudio.eu/en/#contact) itemizes scoping time and revision buffers rather than folding everything into an undifferentiated margin figure. Treat the €500–€900 unbillable-hours range as its own line in your internal cost-plus build-up, on top of subcontractor cost, before you apply your target margin percentage — this way the margin you're actually protecting is margin on top of your fully loaded cost, not margin that gets silently eaten by categories you never priced for in the first place.

## Where Productization Genuinely Helps the Math

None of this means productized launch services are a bad business — the unbillable-hours cost, while real, shrinks meaningfully with volume and repetition in a way that fully bespoke project work never does. The second and third time you run the scoping checklist on a similar prototype, it takes less time because you've built pattern recognition for what to check first. The second and third time you handle a client revision request through the same process, the communication is more efficient because you've built the template language already. This is the actual economic case for productizing — not that the unbillable hours disappear, but that they compress with repetition, which is a structural advantage a fully custom quote-from-scratch engagement never gets to claim. An agency running its tenth white-label launch engagement genuinely does have lower unbillable-hours cost than its first, which is a real margin improvement worth capturing deliberately in how the offer is packaged, a topic covered in more depth in a companion piece on [building a repeatable launch offer](https://launchstudio.eu/en/#packages).

## A Worked Comparison: Bespoke Quoting vs. a Productized Tier

The clearest way to see the margin impact of productization is to run the same twelve-engagement year two ways. Under bespoke quoting — every project scoped and priced individually from a blank template — assume the fuller nine-hour unbillable-hours estimate per engagement applies consistently, because nothing about the process has been streamlined yet: roughly €675 per project at a €75 internal rate, or €8,100 across twelve engagements, against the same €32,400 gross margin figure from the two-line calculation. Real margin: €24,300, an effective rate of 25%.

Under a productized tier structure — three fixed packages instead of bespoke quotes, a standard scoping checklist, templated contract and communication language — the same twelve engagements might realistically run at five unbillable hours each by the second half of the year, once the process has compressed: roughly €375 per project, or €4,500 across twelve engagements. Real margin: €27,900, an effective rate of 29%. The four-point margin improvement, worth roughly €3,600 in this example, isn't from charging clients more. It's entirely a function of unbillable hours shrinking because the process itself got faster to run — the same underlying economic force behind productization generally, made concrete against a specific number instead of treated as a vague efficiency claim.

## The Honest Margin Target

Once unbillable hours are priced in explicitly, a realistic target margin on a productized launch service, calculated against the fully loaded cost including subcontractor fee and internal time, sits in the 25–35% range for most agencies — not the 40–50% figures that circulate informally in agency-owner conversations and that rarely survive contact with an honest hourly accounting. That's not a disappointing number; it's a sustainable one, provided the volume is there to make the fixed overhead of running the service line — the relationship with a reliable technical partner, the templated contract language, the scoping checklist — worth maintaining across enough engagements per year to matter.

## Real example

### An Agency Partner in Action: The Spreadsheet That Told the Wrong Story

Koen Dijkstra runs a six-person web agency in Breda that had been offering a productized launch service for a year, tracking margin with a simple two-column spreadsheet: client invoice, subcontractor cost. The number looked healthy — a steady 38% average margin across nineteen engagements — until Koen ran an actual time-tracking exercise across four representative projects to prepare for a partner meeting about scaling the service line.

The exercise found an average of 6.5 unbilled hours per engagement across scoping, client communication, and revision handling — at Koen's own effective rate, roughly €650 per project that had never appeared in the spreadsheet at all. Recalculated honestly, the service line's real margin was closer to 24%, and two of the nineteen engagements, the ones with the most demanding first-time clients, had actually run at a loss once his own time was counted.

**Result:** Koen restructured his pricing to explicitly include a scoping-and-management line item in every quote, raised his baseline client price by roughly 15%, and began flagging first-time, high-touch clients for a higher margin tier upfront rather than applying a uniform markup regardless of expected hand-holding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's a realistic margin target for a productized launch service once unbillable hours are counted?

Most agencies land in the 25–35% range against fully loaded cost, including subcontractor fee and internal time, once scoping, client communication, and revision handling are priced in rather than absorbed silently.

### How much time should I budget for unbillable hours per engagement?

A reasonable planning range is five to eight hours per engagement across scoping, communication, and revision handling for a typical fixed-price project, though this compresses with repetition as your process for a given type of engagement matures.

### Does productizing the offer actually reduce these unbillable hours over time?

Yes, meaningfully — the second and later engagements of a similar type benefit from pattern recognition in scoping and templated communication for common revision requests, which is the real economic argument for productization rather than a marketing claim.

### Should I charge different clients different margins based on how much hand-holding they need?

It's reasonable to build a higher margin tier for clients you can identify upfront as likely to need more communication and revision cycles — first-time agency clients, non-technical founders, or clients with a history of scope creep — rather than applying a flat markup regardless of expected effort.

### How do I start tracking my real margin without adding a lot of admin overhead?

Time-track three or four representative engagements honestly, covering scoping through delivery, to calibrate a realistic unbillable-hours figure for your specific process — you don't need ongoing detailed time tracking on every project once you have that baseline number to build into your standard pricing.
