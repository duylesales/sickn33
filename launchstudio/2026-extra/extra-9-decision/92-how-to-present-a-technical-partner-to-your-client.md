Do you tell your client who actually built their backend?

There's no universally correct answer, and any agency owner who tells you there is has probably only ever run one kind of client relationship. The honest answer is that the right disclosure posture changes by client, by project risk, and by how much of your own credibility you're willing to stake on work you didn't personally do — and getting it wrong in either direction costs you something real. Stay invisible with the wrong client and you're one direct technical question away from an awkward, credibility-damaging stumble. Over-disclose with the wrong client and you've handed them a reason to wonder why they're paying you at all instead of going straight to your subcontractor.

This decision deserves more deliberation than most agencies give it, because it's usually made by default — whatever felt natural on the first white-label project becomes the unexamined habit for every one after it, regardless of whether the client or the risk profile actually matches.

## The Three Postures, Defined Precisely

**Invisible white-label** means the client never learns a subcontractor exists. Every communication, every deliverable, every line of the invoice comes from you. This is the posture most agencies default to instinctively, because it protects the cleanest version of the client relationship and preserves 100% of your positioning as the technical authority in the room.

**Named delivery partner** means the client knows a specialist partner is doing the technical build, under your project management and your account ownership. You might say, plainly, "we work with a production engineering partner for the backend hardening — they handle security, payments, and hosting under our direction, and we manage the whole relationship for you." The client relationship stays yours; the technical credit is shared.

**Introduced specialist** means you make a formal, structured introduction — the client works with the technical partner somewhat directly for a defined portion of the engagement, typically the scoping call and technical Q&A, while you retain the commercial relationship, the invoicing, and the overall account. This is the most transparent posture and the one that surrenders the most direct control over the technical conversation.

## When Invisible Is the Right Call

Invisible white-label is the correct posture when three conditions hold together: the client hired you specifically for your brand, creative direction, or strategic relationship rather than for engineering credibility; the technical risk in the project is low enough that a client technical question is unlikely to arise in a form you can't field yourself with basic preparation; and the engagement is small enough, typically the €800–€3,500 Launch Ready range, that the coordination overhead of a more transparent posture isn't worth it for either party. A branding agency delivering a client's Lovable prototype as a finished, launched product — where the client's actual relationship is with your creative team and the backend work is a means to an end, not the point of the engagement — is the textbook case for staying fully invisible. The risk with this posture isn't detection; clients rarely find out and rarely care if the work is good. The risk is being caught flat-footed by a specific technical question you can't answer from first-hand knowledge, in a moment where hesitation reads as incompetence rather than as a reasonable person needing to check with their team.

## When Naming the Partner Actually Builds Trust

Named delivery partner is the right call once project risk rises — anything touching payments, regulated data, or an enterprise client's own procurement process — because at that risk level, a sophisticated client's trust increases, not decreases, when they learn a specialist production partner backed by real engineering depth is involved. This is a common misjudgment among agencies newer to white-label work: the instinct is that naming a subcontractor makes the agency look less capable, when in a higher-stakes engagement it more often signals the opposite — that the agency is assembling the right team for the job rather than stretching generalist capability over specialized, high-consequence work. Naming a partner like LaunchStudio, backed by Manifera's 11+ years of production engineering experience, can be a credibility asset in a sales conversation with a client who's sophisticated enough to know that security and payments infrastructure is a different skill set from frontend and brand work — and pretending otherwise, with this specific client, tends to read as either naive or evasive once they ask a detail-level question you can't answer.

The mechanics of naming a partner without diminishing your own role matter here: frame it as your sourcing and quality-control decision, not as an admission you couldn't do it yourself. "We partner with a specialist production engineering team for backend hardening — same process as us bringing in a motion designer for video work, we manage it end to end" keeps you positioned as the party who assembled and is accountable for the right team, not as a reseller stepping aside.

## When to Make a Formal Introduction Instead

Introduced specialist is worth choosing deliberately, rather than falling into by accident, in two specific situations. First, when the client explicitly needs to validate technical capability themselves before committing — an enterprise buyer whose procurement process requires a security questionnaire answered by someone with hands-on production experience, not relayed secondhand through you, as discussed in a companion piece on [verifying a partner can handle compliance needs](https://launchstudio.eu/en/#contact). Second, when the scope and duration of the engagement are large enough that ongoing direct technical communication between the client and the specialist is simply more efficient than routing every question through you as a translation layer — a Launch & Grow engagement with a multi-week build and a client who wants weekly technical updates is a reasonable candidate.

The risk with this posture is real and worth naming honestly: introducing the client to the specialist creates a direct relationship that could, in theory, bypass you on a future project. In practice this risk is smaller than agencies fear, because the commercial relationship, the invoicing, the account history, and the client's broader trust in your judgment as the party who found and vetted the specialist in the first place all still run through you — but it's a real trade-off, not a nonexistent one, and it belongs in your decision rather than being ignored.

## A Decision Framework You Can Actually Run

Run these three questions before every white-label engagement, in order, rather than defaulting to whatever posture you used last time. First: what's the actual technical risk in this project — does it touch payments, regulated data, or a client procurement process that will eventually demand specifics you'd need to relay secondhand? Higher risk pushes toward naming or introducing. Second: what does this specific client value about the relationship — your creative and strategic judgment, or evidence of deep technical capability? A client who hired you for brand work rarely benefits from technical transparency they didn't ask for. Third: what's your own comfort fielding detailed technical questions without a specialist in the room? If the honest answer is "not very," that's a strong signal toward naming the partner rather than betting the relationship on staying invisible and hoping the question never comes.

## The Middle Ground Most Agencies Miss

There's a fourth option worth naming because it's underused: situational disclosure, where you default to invisible for routine communication but have a named-partner explanation ready if a specific technical question actually arrives, rather than deciding the posture is fixed for the whole engagement. This isn't inconsistency — it's simply not volunteering information the client hasn't asked for while being fully prepared to be transparent the moment they do. The failure mode this avoids is the agency that commits hard to "always invisible" and then improvises badly, visibly, under pressure, in the exact moment a confident answer mattered most. Deciding in advance what you'd say if asked — even while defaulting to not volunteering it — removes the improvisation risk without forcing a transparency posture the client never needed.

## What Your Contract Should Say Regardless of Posture

One thing shouldn't vary by disclosure posture: what's actually in writing between you and your client about who's doing the work. Even under a fully invisible posture, your service agreement should reserve your right to subcontract technical delivery — a standard clause, not a confession, and one most clients never read closely but that protects you completely if the question ever does come up directly. An agency that stays invisible in conversation but has no subcontracting language in the contract is taking on unnecessary legal exposure for no real relationship benefit; the client's trust isn't protected by the omission, only your conversational comfort is, and that's a bad trade against actual contractual risk. This is a separate question from disclosure posture in conversation, and it's worth getting a lawyer to review once rather than assuming a generic template covers it — the clause needs to explicitly permit subcontracting while keeping you as the accountable party for deliverables, timeline, and quality.

## How Client Sophistication Should Shift Your Default

A useful shortcut, if the three-question framework above feels like too much to run on every single deal: let client sophistication set your default, then adjust for risk. A first-time founder client with no engineering background and a low-risk project rarely benefits from technical transparency they didn't ask for — invisible is the low-friction default. A repeat enterprise client, a technically literate co-founder, or anyone who's run vendor relationships before will generally probe enough that named or introduced saves you the awkward improvisation later. This isn't a hard rule, since a low-sophistication client on a high-risk payments project still warrants naming the partner regardless of how unlikely they are to ask — but as a starting heuristic before you apply the full framework, sophistication plus risk together get you to the right posture faster than either alone.

Whichever posture you choose, decide it deliberately, per client and per project, rather than by habit — because the cost of the wrong posture is never dramatic in the moment, it's a slow erosion of trust the first time a client notices the mismatch between what they were told and what was actually happening underneath.

## Real example

### An Agency Partner in Action: The Question That Forced a Posture Change

Bram Oudshoorn runs a small product-design studio in Eindhoven that had always stayed fully invisible on the backend work it subcontracted, including a fintech client's expense-tracking app built on Bolt. Midway through the engagement, the client — a former engineer himself — asked a specific, detailed question about how token refresh was being handled on the payment integration. Bram, who'd relayed the work secondhand from his subcontractor without fully understanding it, didn't have a confident answer, and the pause before his non-answer was more damaging to the relationship than the honest truth would have been.

After that project, Bram shifted his default posture for any engagement touching payments or regulated data to named delivery partner, introducing his production engineering team by name in the kickoff call rather than waiting to be asked. His next fintech-adjacent client, briefed upfront that a specialist backend team was handling the security-critical work under his account management, asked more technical questions overall — and Bram simply routed them to a short call with the specialist rather than guessing.

**Result:** Bram's studio has closed three fintech-adjacent engagements since adopting the named-partner posture for higher-risk projects, with client feedback specifically citing confidence in the technical depth as a reason for choosing his studio over a competing agency that stayed vague about who was actually doing the work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will naming a subcontractor make my client think I'm just a reseller?

Only if you frame it that way. Presented as a sourcing and quality-control decision — "we bring in a specialist team for backend hardening, same as we would for any specialized skill, and we manage the relationship end to end" — naming a partner reinforces that you assembled the right team, rather than signaling you couldn't do the work yourself.

### What happens if a client finds out about an invisible subcontractor after the fact?

Most clients don't react negatively to discovering good work was delivered by a vetted specialist team, as long as the quality was there and they weren't misled about anything material like pricing or ownership. The relationship risk comes from the client feeling deceived about something that mattered to their decision, not from the mere existence of a subcontractor.

### Can I switch postures partway through an engagement?

Yes, and situational disclosure — staying invisible by default but having a named-partner explanation ready if a technical question arrives — is a reasonable middle ground rather than committing to one posture for the entire relationship regardless of how the project evolves.

### Does the disclosure posture affect who owns the client relationship afterward?

It can, particularly with introduced specialist, where the client develops some direct familiarity with the technical partner. Keeping invoicing, account ownership, and the primary communication channel with you, even in a named or introduced posture, is the clearest way to protect the relationship long-term.

### Is there a posture that works safely across almost every client?

Named delivery partner is the safest general default for any project involving payments, regulated data, or meaningful technical risk, because it builds credibility with sophisticated clients while still keeping you positioned as the accountable party managing the engagement.
