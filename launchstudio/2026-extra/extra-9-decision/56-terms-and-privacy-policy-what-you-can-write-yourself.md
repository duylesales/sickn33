Niels spent forty minutes staring at a blank "Terms of Service" page template his AI coding tool generated as a placeholder, trying to decide whether to just delete the whole thing, paste in a competitor's terms with the company name swapped out, or pay a lawyer several hundred euros he didn't really have yet for a document almost nobody reads before clicking "I agree." All three options felt wrong for slightly different reasons, and none of the founder forums he checked gave him a straight answer about which parts actually mattered versus which parts were legal theater. This is a genuinely common moment, and it deserves a genuinely specific answer rather than "just use a template" or "always hire a lawyer" — because the honest truth sits in between: most of a startup's Terms of Service and Privacy Policy is boilerplate a careful founder can draft correctly themselves, and a smaller, specific set of clauses carries enough real risk that skipping legal review on them is a false economy.

Here's the actual line, drawn clause by clause rather than as a blanket rule.

## What a Privacy Policy Legally Has to Contain — And Why That's More Writable Than It Sounds

A GDPR-compliant privacy policy has a required structure, not an open-ended one, which is actually good news for a founder trying to draft it themselves: what personal data you collect, why (the lawful basis for each category), who you share it with (your processors — the same vendor list covered in a companion decision about Data Processing Agreements), how long you keep it, and what rights users have (access, deletion, portability, objection) and how to exercise them. Because this is a factual document describing what your product actually does, rather than a document requiring legal drafting judgment, a founder who has already made the underlying decisions — what data is collected, what lawful basis applies to each category, which vendors process it — can write an accurate privacy policy themselves, in plain language, without a lawyer's involvement. The risk in DIY privacy policies isn't usually the writing; it's inaccuracy — a policy that claims data isn't shared with third parties when an analytics tool is quietly sharing it, or a policy copied from a template describing a different product's data flows entirely. The fix is sequencing: do the data inventory first (what you collect, why, who touches it), then write the policy as a factual description of that inventory, rather than starting from a generic template and hoping it happens to match your actual product.

## What Terms of Service Actually Needs to Cover for a Small SaaS Product

Terms of Service is a different kind of document — it's a contract, not a disclosure, which means it's establishing legally binding obligations between you and your users rather than just describing facts. The core sections a small SaaS product genuinely needs: what the service is and what it doesn't guarantee (an availability/uptime disclaimer, especially important for an early-stage product without a formal SLA), acceptable use restrictions (what users can't do with your product), account termination rights (yours and theirs), payment terms if applicable (billing cycle, refund policy, what happens on non-payment), intellectual property ownership (who owns user-generated content versus your platform), and a liability limitation clause capping your exposure if something goes wrong. Several of these — acceptable use, account termination, describing the service accurately — are things a founder can draft confidently themselves, because they're describing product decisions you've already made, not inventing new legal protections. The liability limitation and intellectual property clauses are where the line starts to blur, covered next.

## The Specific Clauses Where DIY Genuinely Gets Risky

Three categories of clauses carry disproportionate risk relative to how easy they are to get subtly wrong, and they're worth naming specifically rather than leaving the "get a lawyer" advice vague. **Liability limitation and indemnification** — the clause capping how much you can be sued for and under what circumstances — has real enforceability nuances that vary by jurisdiction, and a poorly drafted version can either fail to protect you when it matters or, if too aggressive, become unenforceable entirely because courts in several EU jurisdictions won't uphold liability waivers that go too far, particularly against consumers. **Intellectual property ownership for user-generated or AI-assisted content** — if your product lets users upload content, or your product itself uses AI to generate content on a user's behalf, who owns what gets legally murkier than founders expect, especially with AI-generated output where copyright law itself is still unsettled in several jurisdictions; getting this clause wrong can create real disputes later, particularly if the product grows and content ownership becomes commercially significant. **Any clause addressing consumer rights specifically** — if you sell to individual consumers rather than exclusively B2B, EU consumer protection law imposes mandatory rights (cooling-off periods for digital purchases, specific cancellation and refund entitlements) that override anything your terms claim, and a DIY terms document that contradicts mandatory consumer law isn't just risky, parts of it are simply unenforceable regardless of what it says. These three categories are where a one-time paid legal review — not a full custom drafting engagement, just a review of a solid template you've already adapted — earns back its cost by catching the specific clause that would otherwise cause a real problem.

## The Template-Plus-Review Approach: The Middle Path Most Founders Miss

The false choice founders often present themselves is "write it all myself for free" versus "pay a lawyer to draft everything from scratch for €2,000-plus." There's a middle path that captures most of the cost savings of the first option and most of the risk protection of the second: start from a reputable template (several exist specifically built for SaaS startups, often available through startup legal service providers at low or no cost), adapt the factual sections yourself — what your product does, what data you collect, your actual pricing and cancellation mechanics — and then pay for a focused, paid review rather than a full drafting engagement, specifically asking the lawyer to check the liability, IP, and consumer-rights clauses against your actual business model. This kind of scoped review is typically a fraction of the cost of full custom drafting, because you're paying for judgment on the few clauses that need it, not for the lawyer's time writing sections you could write accurately yourself. Framing the ask this way — "I've drafted this, please review these three specific risk areas" — also tends to get a faster, cheaper response from a lawyer than an open-ended "can you write my terms of service," because the scope is already bounded.

## Keeping the Documents Honest as the Product Changes

The most common failure mode isn't a bad initial document — it's a good initial document that quietly goes stale as the product changes. A founder who adds a new payment processor, a new AI feature, or a new data-sharing integration six months after launch and doesn't update the privacy policy to reflect it has created exactly the accuracy gap that turns a defensible document into a liability — not because the original policy was wrong, but because it no longer describes the actual product. A practical habit: treat "does this change require a terms or privacy policy update" as a standing question in your own launch checklist for new features, the same way a founder might already ask "does this need a database migration" — it takes thirty seconds to ask and catches drift before it accumulates into a genuinely outdated document nobody remembers to revisit.

## Consumer vs. B2B: Why the Same Product Might Need Different Terms

A decision founders often skip entirely: does your terms of service need to account for individual consumer users, business users, or both, and does that distinction change what's legally required? If your SaaS product sells exclusively to registered businesses (B2B), you have more contractual freedom — commercial parties are assumed to be more sophisticated and mandatory consumer protections largely don't apply. If any meaningful share of your users are individual consumers, mandatory EU consumer rights kick in regardless of what your terms say, and those rights need to be reflected accurately rather than contracted around. Some products serve both (a freelancer signing up individually versus a small agency's team account), which means the terms need to either address both cases explicitly or the product needs a clear registration path that distinguishes them. This is a decision worth making deliberately at drafting time, because retrofitting a consumer-rights section into terms written with only B2B assumptions in mind is a bigger rewrite than building it in from the start.

## When to Actually Pick Up the Phone and Call a Lawyer

Beyond the specific clauses named above, there are situational triggers worth treating as a hard stop on DIY: you're about to close a funding round and investors' legal due diligence will scrutinize these documents directly; you're entering a regulated vertical (health, finance, legal services) where sector-specific rules layer on top of general SaaS terms; you're expanding into a new country with materially different consumer protection law; or you've already had a dispute or complaint that makes the theoretical risk suddenly concrete. Outside those triggers, a careful founder working from a solid template with a scoped professional review on the three risk clauses named above has a genuinely defensible document, not a corner-cutting one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just copy a competitor's terms of service and privacy policy and change the company name?

No — beyond being a copyright issue, a competitor's documents describe their specific data flows, features, and business model, which almost certainly don't match yours exactly, and an inaccurate policy is worse than a slightly imperfect original one because it creates a real gap between what you've legally represented and what your product actually does.

### How much does a scoped legal review of a self-drafted terms document typically cost?

A focused review of specific risk clauses (liability, IP, consumer rights) on a document you've already drafted from a solid template is typically a fraction of the cost of full custom drafting, since the lawyer is reviewing rather than writing from scratch — get a specific quote by describing exactly what you want reviewed rather than asking for an open-ended engagement.

### Do I need separate terms of service for a mobile app versus a web app?

Not necessarily separate documents, but the terms need to account for any platform-specific differences — app store payment terms, mobile-specific data collection like device identifiers, or push notification consent — that a web-only terms document might not address, so review this specifically if you're adding a mobile app to an existing web product.

### What's the actual risk of just launching with an imperfect, self-drafted document versus waiting for a lawyer?

For most early-stage products, launching with an honest, accurate, template-based document you've drafted carefully is lower risk than delaying launch indefinitely waiting for full custom legal drafting — the documented decisions in this article, done conscientiously, cover the large majority of what a small SaaS product needs; the specific risk clauses named above are where the calculation shifts toward getting help before launch, not after.

### How often should I revisit my terms and privacy policy after the initial launch?

Treat every new feature, vendor, or data flow change as a trigger to check whether the documents still accurately describe the product, rather than reviewing on a fixed calendar schedule — as Niels's example shows, the risk isn't usually in the original document, it's in the gap that opens up between an unchanged document and a product that kept evolving.
