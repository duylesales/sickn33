It's Thursday, 22:15. You got home at 19:00, ate, dealt with something domestic, and opened the laptop at 21:30. By the time you've remembered where you left off on Sunday, it's 22:15, and you have maybe seventy usable minutes before the quality of what you produce turns negative.

This is the actual unit of a nights-and-weekends launch: not "evenings and weekends" as a phrase, but seventy minutes on a Thursday and a contested three hours on a Sunday morning. Every plan you make has to be denominated in those, and almost none are — which is why side-project launches slip not by weeks but by quarters.

What follows isn't a task list. Task lists assume time. This is a calendar of four decisions, each with a date attached, designed so that a founder with a job can tell — on a specific evening — whether to continue, adjust, or stop.

## Price Your Week Honestly First

Write down the hours you will genuinely have, then cut them by a third.

Most employed founders start with a figure like fifteen hours a week. The realistic number, sustained over three months rather than during the first enthusiastic fortnight, is closer to six to eight — because of the reload cost. Every session on a codebase you touch twice a week begins with fifteen to twenty minutes of remembering what you were doing, and that overhead is proportionally brutal at seventy-minute sessions. Four short sessions a week can deliver less than one long one.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, batch: two longer blocks beat five short ones, so protect a Sunday morning rather than scattering weeknights. Second, and more important for what follows, *anything with an unpredictable duration is a bad fit for your calendar*. You can plan a feature. You cannot plan "find out why the payment webhook is failing intermittently," and that category of work is exactly what production readiness consists of. This is the structural reason employed founders should buy the hardening and build the product, rather than the reverse.

## Week 0: The Conversation You Have Before Anything Else

Settle the employment questions before you write a line of code or take a euro, because they are cheap to check now and expensive to discover after you have customers.

Three things to look for in your contract. A **side-activities clause** (nevenwerkzaamhedenbeding): since the Dutch implementation of the EU transparent working conditions directive in August 2022, an employer generally cannot prohibit side activities without objective justification — but "generally cannot" is not "does not," and the clause may still require notification. Read what yours actually says rather than what you assume.

An **intellectual property clause**. Under Dutch copyright law, work created in the course of employment can belong to the employer, and many contracts extend that further. If your product is adjacent to what you do at work, or if you have ever opened the laptop during working hours, get clarity in writing. A single email from your employer confirming they make no claim is worth more than any amount of hoping.

A **non-compete** (concurrentiebeding). If your product serves the same market as your employer, this is the one that can genuinely stop the project.

The uncomfortable recommendation: if your contract is ambiguous and your product is anywhere near your employer's field, spend €300 on an hour with an employment lawyer now. It is the cheapest line item in this entire article and the only one that can void everything else.

## The Shape: Four Gates, Not Twelve Weeks of Grind

The failure mode for employed founders is not giving up. It is continuing indefinitely — eighteen months of Thursdays, a product that is always six weeks away, and no moment at which anyone decided anything. Gates fix that by putting the decision on the calendar rather than leaving it to mood.

Four dates. Put them in the same calendar as your job.

## Gate One, End of Week 2: Is Anyone Waiting?

The only question: have you had five real conversations with potential customers, and did anyone say something that resembles yes?

Two weeks is enough for five conversations even at six hours a week, because conversations fit in lunch breaks and your product doesn't need to be finished to have them — a screen recording or a demo you drive yourself is sufficient. This gate exists first deliberately: it is the cheapest thing to test, and everything after it costs real money.

**Pass:** someone specific expressed intent. Continue.
**Fail:** five conversations, no traction, no clear "I'd use this if it did X." Do not proceed to spending. Either run five more conversations with a different audience, or stop — and stopping here has cost you two weeks of evenings and nothing else, which is a good outcome, not a failed one.

## Gate Two, End of Week 4: What Do You Keep and What Do You Buy?

By now you know what the interested party needs. This gate decides the split, and for an employed founder the principle is sharper than for anyone else: **buy the work whose duration you cannot predict, keep the work you can do in seventy-minute blocks.**

Predictable, keep-it work: product logic, copy, the screens, onboarding flow, pricing page, whatever your AI builder handles well. These are satisfying, bounded, and safe to do tired-ish.

Unpredictable, buy-it work: the authorization sweep across every route, secrets that shouldn't be in the browser, the payment state machine, deployment and SSL, backups with a tested restore. Every one of these is open-ended by nature — you don't know how many places are wrong until you've looked at all of them — and open-ended work does not fit in a Thursday evening.

This is where the fixed-price model actually matters more to you than to a full-time founder. A fixed scope with a 1–3 week delivery is a block of calendar you do not have to staff. **Pass:** you have a written split and a fixed quote. **Fail:** you're still planning to do all of it yourself over the winter. That plan has a name, and the name is Gate Four arriving with nothing to decide.

## Gate Three, End of Week 8: Live, Quietly

Live means: your own domain, real accounts, one or more actual users who are not you, and money either received or invoiced. Not "launched" in the announcement sense — quietly live, which is exactly the right posture for someone with an employer.

By week eight the bought work has been delivered (weeks five through seven), and weeks seven and eight are yours: the last product touches, the onboarding email you send by hand, the first users let in one at a time.

The most valuable habit here is deliberately low-volume onboarding. Five users you personally welcomed will teach you more than fifty who signed up unattended, and — practically — five is a number you can support from a phone during a lunch break. An employed founder who launches loudly and gets two hundred signups on a Tuesday has created an operational emergency they cannot attend to until 19:00.

**Pass:** live, with users. **Fail:** slipped. Ask which gate the slip actually came from — it is almost always Gate Two, where the buy/keep split was made too optimistically.

## Gate Four, End of Week 12: The Number That Decides the Next Quarter

This is the gate the whole calendar exists to reach, and it needs a number chosen *in week zero*, not negotiated in week twelve when you're emotionally invested.

Pick your threshold in advance. Something like: three paying customers, or €500 in monthly recurring revenue, or one signed annual contract. The specific figure matters less than the fact that you committed to it before you knew the outcome.

**Above the threshold:** the project has earned the next quarter, and now the harder conversation starts — reduced hours, unpaid leave, a four-day week, or the resignation date. Note that Dutch notice periods are typically one month, so the calendar for that decision starts earlier than it feels like it does.

**Below the threshold but moving:** one more twelve-week cycle, with the same gates and a revised threshold. Explicitly one more, not indefinitely.

**Below the threshold and flat:** stop, or park it. This is allowed, and it is worth saying plainly to an audience that mostly reads the opposite: a side project that reached "live with real users and no traction" has produced a genuine answer, which is more than most side projects ever manage.

## What You Cannot Cover Alone With a Job

There's one category an employed founder is structurally unable to handle, and it's worth naming because it changes what you should buy.

Production incidents happen during working hours. Your database hits a connection limit at 11:20 on a Wednesday; a certificate expires on a Monday morning; a customer's login stops working while you are in a meeting you cannot leave. A full-time founder loses two hours to that. You lose eight, minimum, and your customer experiences an outage that lasts a working day.

## Real example

### A Nights-and-Weekends Founder in Action: The Gate That Nearly Didn't Happen

Nadia el Amrani worked four days a week as a procurement officer in Breda and spent Thursday evenings and Sunday mornings on Tarievenwijzer, a Lovable-built tool that helps small municipalities compare supplier contract rates. She had been at it for seven months when she wrote down her first honest hours-per-week figure: five and a half, not the fifteen she had been planning against.

She reset with four gates. Gate one produced three interested municipalities in two weeks, one of which said it would pay for a pilot. Gate two was where the calendar earned its keep: her plan had been to spend the winter learning to do user accounts and permissions herself. The scope split she wrote instead kept the comparison logic and the reports — bounded, satisfying, doable on a Thursday — and bought the parts with no predictable end: role separation between municipal users and supplier users, an exposed spreadsheet-parsing API key rotated and moved server-side, and deployment on her own domain with monitoring.

Tarievenwijzer went live in week nine, one week past gate three, with two municipalities and eleven named users onboarded by hand over three Sunday mornings. Gate four was a threshold she had written in week zero: €600 monthly recurring. She reached €740.

**Result:** Nadia negotiated a three-day working week with her employer in month five rather than resigning outright, and the €49/month support layer covered two incidents that occurred while she was at her desk job — including a certificate renewal failure on a Monday morning she only learned about from the resolution message.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many hours a week should I actually plan for around a full-time job?

Take your optimistic figure and cut it by roughly a third — most employed founders sustain six to eight hours a week over three months, not the fifteen they plan for. The gap comes largely from reload time, since every short session on an unfamiliar-again codebase starts with fifteen minutes of remembering where you were.

### What should I check in my employment contract before starting?

The side-activities clause, the intellectual property clause and any non-compete. Dutch law has restricted blanket bans on side activities since August 2022, but notification requirements and IP assignment terms still vary, and an hour with an employment lawyer is the cheapest insurance in the whole project.

### Should I tell my employer I'm building something?

That depends on your contract and your relationship, but if your contract requires notification, comply with it in writing rather than relying on informal goodwill. Founders who later negotiate reduced hours generally find the conversation easier when the project was never a secret.

### Which work should an employed founder never take on personally?

Anything with an unpredictable duration — an authorization sweep, chasing an intermittent payment bug, a database migration. Seventy-minute evening sessions suit bounded work, and open-ended investigation in that format is where side projects lose entire quarters.

### Is a monthly support subscription worth it for a very small product?

For an employed founder specifically, it usually is, because the constraint isn't the size of the product but your availability during working hours. A certificate expiry or a database limit hit at 11:00 costs a full-time founder two hours and costs you a full working day of customer-visible downtime.
