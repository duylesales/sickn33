Two agencies get the same inquiry the same week: a founder with a Bolt-built booking platform who wants it launch-ready in a month. The first agency's account manager opens a blank proposal template and starts from scratch — a scoping call, three days of internal discussion about how to price it, a custom document built specifically for this client. The second agency's account manager pulls up three tiers on a single page, asks the founder four qualifying questions on the discovery call, and has a proposal in the founder's inbox by end of day. Same underlying service. Wildly different sales cycle, and — this is the part that surprises agencies who haven't made the switch — a meaningfully different margin, because the second agency isn't rebuilding its pricing logic and its internal process from zero every single time.

This is the case for productizing a launch offer instead of bespoke-quoting it project by project, and the practical guide to what actually belongs in each tier, how to price it, and where bespoke quoting still earns its keep.

## Bespoke Quoting vs. Fixed Tiers: What Actually Changes

Bespoke quoting feels more precise, and in a narrow sense it is — every quote is built against the specific project in front of you. But precision has a cost that rarely gets counted honestly: every bespoke quote requires a full scoping-to-pricing cycle from scratch, every client sales conversation is a new negotiation without an anchor, and every internal handoff between sales and delivery requires re-explaining a custom scope rather than referencing a known package. Fixed tiers trade a small amount of pricing precision — some projects will be slightly over-scoped for their tier, some slightly under — for a large reduction in sales friction, internal coordination time, and the unbillable hours that quietly erode margin on bespoke work, a dynamic covered in more depth in a companion piece on [the margin math behind a productized launch service](https://launchstudio.eu/en/#packages).

The trade generally favors tiers once an agency is running more than four or five of these engagements a year — below that volume, the setup cost of building a proper tier structure may not pay back fast enough to be worth it, and bespoke quoting remains the more sensible default.

## Designing the Tiers: Three Levels, Not Five

The temptation when productizing is to build too many tiers, trying to capture every possible variation of client need — this backfires, because more tiers means more decision fatigue for the client and more edge cases where a project doesn't cleanly fit any of them. Three tiers, built around genuinely distinct scopes rather than arbitrary price points, works better in practice.

**Tier one — Launch Fix.** For prototypes that are close to production-ready and need targeted hardening: security review and fixes, basic hosting and deployment, essential data and API corrections. This maps closely to LaunchStudio's own €800–€3,500 Launch Ready range as the subcontracted cost input, and is the right tier for a client whose scoping pass came back mostly clean with a few specific, boundable gaps.

**Tier two — Launch & Grow.** For prototypes needing more substantial backend work: full payment integration, authentication and permissions built out properly, managed hosting with monitoring and backups. This maps to the €2,500–€7,500 range plus the ongoing €49/month managed plan, and is the right tier for a client whose scoping pass surfaced structural gaps — hardcoded data, missing server-side permission checks, an unconfigured payment flow.

**Tier three — Custom Build.** For anything that exceeds the boundaries of the first two tiers: prototypes requiring significant rebuilding, unusual regulatory requirements, or scope that a scoping pass reveals to be structurally larger than a fixed tier can responsibly cover. This tier is intentionally not fixed-price on the menu — it's the honest acknowledgment that some projects genuinely need bespoke quoting, and forcing them into a tier that doesn't fit them is worse for both parties than admitting a scope needs individual pricing.

## Pricing Each Tier With Margin Built In, Not Bolted On

Price each tier using the full cost-plus method from the start, not the two-line calculation that undercounts unbillable hours — subcontractor cost, scoping and project management time (which compresses with repetition once a tier is well-established, as covered in the margin math discussion), a revision buffer, and target margin. For tier one, built on a lower subcontractor cost, a client-facing price in the €2,500–€4,500 range is typical once the full loaded cost is priced in properly. For tier two, a client-facing price in the €5,500–€10,000 range, reflecting both the higher subcontractor cost and the additional coordination a larger build typically requires. These are starting ranges, not universal numbers — the right figures for your agency depend on your market, your positioning, and your actual measured unbillable-hours cost, which is worth calculating from real time-tracking data rather than guessing.

Resist the temptation to price the tiers as thin variations of each other — tier two should feel like a genuinely bigger, more comprehensive offer than tier one, not just a higher number for a similar scope, because a client comparing the two tiers should be able to see clearly what the additional spend actually buys.

## What to Put on the Client-Facing Menu

The client-facing description of each tier should be entirely outcome-based, matching the bundled-pricing approach that works best for client presentation generally: "Launch Fix: get your working prototype production-secure and live on your own domain in one to two weeks, fixed price." Avoid describing tiers by the internal cost components that went into pricing them — the client is buying an outcome and a timeline, not a breakdown of your subcontractor relationship. Include a short, plain-language description of what's covered and, critically, what determines which tier a given project needs — this does real qualifying work before a sales call even happens, since a founder reading "Tier one is for prototypes with working authentication and data storage that just need hardening" can often self-select correctly, saving a scoping call on projects that are obviously tier two or three from the start.

## Handling the Project That Doesn't Fit Cleanly

Even a well-designed three-tier structure will regularly encounter projects that sit at the boundary — mostly a tier-one scope with one tier-two-level gap, like a solid prototype that happens to need a full payment build from scratch. The workable approach is a base tier price plus a named add-on, rather than bumping the entire project to the next tier or discounting the next tier down — "Tier one base, plus a payment integration add-on at [fixed additional price]" keeps the tier structure intact while still pricing the actual scope accurately. Building two or three common add-ons into your menu alongside the core tiers — payment integration, a compliance/DPA review, an expedited timeline — covers most of the boundary cases without requiring a bespoke quote every time one comes up.

## Standardizing the Delivery Process, Not Just the Price

The margin gains from productizing come almost entirely from the process compressing with repetition, which means the tier structure only pays off if the internal delivery process behind each tier is standardized too — not just the client-facing price and description. Build a checklist for each tier that your team runs every time: the same scoping questions in the same order, the same kickoff email template, the same status-update cadence, the same handoff format to your technical partner. This is unglamorous work, and it's tempting to skip it once the pricing menu exists, but a standardized price without a standardized process just means every project still consumes the same unpredictable internal time it always did — the client sees a tidy menu, while your team is still improvising the delivery every single time behind the scenes.

A useful discipline here: after your first five or six engagements at a given tier, sit down and write out the actual steps that happened, in order, across all of them — not the steps you intended to follow, the steps that actually occurred. The patterns that repeat across most of those engagements become your standard checklist; the ones that varied significantly are your signal for where the tier boundary might need adjusting, or where an add-on should exist instead of treating that variation as a one-off exception every time.

## Selling the Menu Without It Feeling Like a Downgrade

One objection agencies raise when considering tiers: doesn't a fixed menu make bespoke, high-touch service feel like something clients are being denied? In practice this concern rarely materializes, because the menu structure doesn't remove your ability to have a genuine, consultative conversation with the client — it just means that conversation is about which tier fits, and what add-ons make sense, rather than about pricing from a blank page. Clients generally experience a clear menu as more professional and more trustworthy than an opaque bespoke quote, not less, because a published tier structure signals that you've done this enough times to know what it actually costs — which is, not coincidentally, exactly true.

## When to Break the Tier and Quote Bespoke Anyway

A repeatable offer only stays credible if you're honest about its limits, and forcing every project into a tier it doesn't actually fit is how a productized offer quietly becomes an unprofitable one. The signal to break from the tier structure and quote bespoke: a scoping pass reveals scope more than roughly 50% larger than the tier's typical range, the client has unusual regulatory requirements that need individual assessment rather than a template answer, or the project involves integration complexity — multiple external systems, an unusual tech stack, a partial migration between AI tools — that the standard tiers weren't designed to price. Naming this threshold explicitly, even just internally, prevents the slow scope-creep where "close enough to tier two" gradually becomes "actually a custom project being sold at a tier-two price," which is exactly the pattern that erodes margin silently across a growing service line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many client projects a year justify building a fixed-tier menu?

Roughly four to five or more per year is a reasonable threshold — below that volume, the time invested in building and refining a proper tier structure may not pay back fast enough to be worth it compared to bespoke quoting.

### What if a project falls between two tiers?

Use a base tier price plus a named, fixed-price add-on for the specific gap, rather than bumping the whole project to the next tier or discounting the higher tier down. Building two or three common add-ons into your menu covers most boundary cases without a bespoke quote.

### Should the tiers be visible publicly on my website, or only shared in sales conversations?

Either works, but publishing at least indicative ranges publicly tends to pre-qualify inbound leads more effectively, since founders can self-select toward or away from your services based on budget before ever reaching out.

### How often should I revisit and adjust my tier pricing?

Review it at least twice a year against your actual delivered-project data — subcontractor cost changes, your measured unbillable-hours figure, and win rates at current pricing should all inform whether a tier needs adjusting.

### Does a fixed-tier structure work for agencies with only one or two clients doing this kind of work?

It's less essential at that volume, since the sales-cycle time savings matter less when you're not running the pricing conversation often. Bespoke quoting remains reasonable until volume grows enough to justify the setup investment.
