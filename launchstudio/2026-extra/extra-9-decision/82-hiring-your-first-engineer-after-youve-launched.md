"Just hire someone senior," a fellow founder told Sanne Willems over drinks, the way people dispense advice that's true and completely useless at the same time. Sanne's product, a scheduling tool for physiotherapy practices, had 600 paying clinics and a backend she'd built almost entirely in Cursor with AI doing most of the actual coding. She didn't disagree with the advice. She just had no idea how to act on it — she couldn't read a candidate's take-home test, couldn't tell a confident answer from a correct one in a technical interview, and had already watched one promising-looking hire from a previous role turn out to be someone who interviewed well and produced almost nothing for four months. "Hire senior" doesn't tell you how to *evaluate* senior when you can't assess the thing you're hiring for. That gap — not the decision to hire, but the mechanics of hiring well without the skill being hired for — is where most first technical hires actually go wrong.

## Why "Just Hire Someone Good" Isn't Actionable Advice

The advice founders get about their first engineering hire is almost universally about *what* to look for — culture fit, ownership mentality, communication skills — and almost never about *how* to verify technical competence when you can't read code yourself. This isn't a small gap. A candidate who interviews confidently, uses the right vocabulary, and has an impressive-sounding resume can still be someone who struggles with the actual, unglamorous work of maintaining a production codebase, and a non-technical founder has no reliable way to tell the difference from a conversation alone. The uncomfortable reality is that technical hiring is one of the few decisions in early-stage SaaS where being a smart, experienced founder in every other dimension provides almost no protection against making an expensive mistake, because the skill required to evaluate the hire is exactly the skill you're trying to hire for.

## The Interview You Can't Run Alone — and Who Can Run It With You

The single highest-leverage move available to a non-technical founder making a first engineering hire is not learning to code well enough to evaluate candidates — that's neither realistic nor necessary — it's bringing in someone who already can, for the specific hours it takes to run a technical interview and review a take-home test. This doesn't require a recruiter retainer or a co-founder search; a development partner who already understands your codebase, because they did earlier hardening or feature work on it, can sit in on a technical interview, review a candidate's take-home submission against your actual codebase's patterns and conventions, and give you a plain-language verdict you can act on. This is a structural reason a relationship with a partner like LaunchStudio — backed by Manifera's team of 120+ engineers — tends to compound in value over time rather than being a one-off engagement: the same team that hardened your product for launch already has context on your stack, and can evaluate whether a candidate would actually fit it, in a way a generic technical recruiter without that context cannot.

## Five Signals That Matter More Than a Resume

When you can't judge code quality directly, shift your attention to signals you *can* judge, because they correlate strongly with the technical competence you can't assess directly. First, how a candidate explains a past project to a non-technical audience — someone who can make their own work understandable to you in the interview will also be able to explain trade-offs to you after they're hired, which matters enormously when you're the one signing off on technical decisions. Second, whether they ask about your existing codebase and its history before proposing solutions, rather than jumping straight to "I'd rebuild this in [their preferred framework]" — a rebuild instinct in an interview is a strong predictor of a rebuild instinct on the job, which is rarely what a working product with paying customers needs. Third, how they talk about AI-generated code specifically, since your codebase likely has Lovable, Bolt, or Cursor fingerprints throughout it — a candidate who dismisses it wholesale ("this all needs to be thrown out") is a different hire than one who can describe what's salvageable and what isn't. Fourth, references who can speak to what happened *after* a project shipped, not just whether it shipped — ongoing maintenance burden, incident response, and how the candidate handled being wrong are far more predictive than launch-day success stories. Fifth, and most concretely: ask every reference the same specific question — "would you hire them again, today, knowing what you know now" — and listen for hesitation more than the words themselves.

## The Take-Home Test That Actually Predicts Job Performance

A generic algorithm puzzle or greenfield take-home test predicts almost nothing about how a candidate will perform maintaining your actual, messy, AI-assisted production codebase — and it's also a test you have no way to grade yourself. A far more useful exercise, and one a contract partner can help you design and grade, is a small, real, bounded task taken directly from your own backlog: fix an actual (low-stakes) bug, add a small feature to an existing module, or review a real pull request and write feedback on it. This does two things a generic test can't: it shows you how the candidate handles your specific codebase's conventions and quirks, and it gives whoever is grading it on your behalf something concrete and comparable to evaluate rather than an abstract, context-free exercise. Pay candidates for this work — a few hundred euros for two or three hours respects their time and signals that you take hiring seriously, which matters more than founders expect to the caliber of candidate willing to complete it thoroughly. Ask your reviewer to score the submission against a short, written rubric agreed in advance — did the solution respect existing conventions, is the change scoped appropriately rather than over-engineered, would a reasonable reviewer approve this pull request without extensive rework — rather than a vague overall impression, so you get a comparable, defensible answer across every candidate rather than a gut feeling that's hard to act on or explain to a co-founder.

## What Engineers Actually Cost in the Netherlands Right Now

Founders consistently underestimate the full cost of a technical hire because they anchor on the gross salary number alone. In the Netherlands, a mid-level backend or full-stack engineer commands roughly €65,000–€80,000 gross annually, a senior engineer €85,000–€110,000+, and a junior €45,000–€55,000 — but the gross salary is only the starting point. Dutch employment law adds a mandatory 8% holiday allowance on top of salary, plus employer social contributions and pension costs that typically run an additional 20–30% of gross salary, meaning the fully-loaded cost of a €75,000 mid-level hire lands closer to €95,000–€100,000 once benefits, employer taxes, and standard perks (laptop, home-office stipend, training budget) are included. Add a recruiter fee if you use one — typically 15–25% of first-year salary for a specialized technical search — and the true cost of getting one engineer through their first year, before they've shipped anything independently, routinely exceeds €110,000. This isn't a reason to avoid hiring; it's the number that should actually be compared against the cost of continued contract or partner engineering work when deciding whether you're ready.

## Structuring the Offer So a Bad Fit Isn't a Permanent Mistake

Dutch employment contracts give you more room to structure a safe first hire than founders often realize, and it's worth using deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever template a lawyer hands you. A standard proeftijd (probation period) of one month for contracts under two years, or two months for longer and permanent contracts, allows either side to end the relationship with immediate effect and minimal formality — use the full length available, and treat it as an active evaluation window rather than a formality, with a real check-in at two and four weeks rather than silence until it's nearly over. A fixed-term contract of six or twelve months as the first agreement, rather than jumping straight to permanent, is common practice in the Dutch market and gives you a second, lower-stakes evaluation point before a longer-term commitment; candidates worth hiring rarely balk at this, since it's standard rather than a sign of distrust. Equity or a bonus tied to specific, concrete milestones — not vague "help us grow" language — is also worth considering for an early technical hire, since it aligns incentives during exactly the period where you have the least ability to supervise the quality of their work directly.

## What to Do Before the Offer Goes Out

Two steps, done before an offer is extended, prevent most of the expensive mistakes founders make on a first technical hire. First, have your technical evaluator — internal or contracted — do a short, honest assessment of your current codebase's state and document known issues, so the new hire's first weeks are spent orienting against a known map rather than discovering surprises alone and silently, which is a common cause of early technical hires quietly disengaging. Second, define what "done" looks like for their first 30, 60, and 90 days in writing before they start — a specific small feature shipped, a documented understanding of the deployment process, ownership of one clearly-scoped area — because a first technical hire without concrete early milestones tends to drift for months before a founder without the skill to evaluate their output notices anything is wrong.

## When Hiring Isn't Actually the Right Next Step Yet

Not every SaaS founder asking "should I hire my first engineer" is actually ready to, and a genuinely useful gut check is whether the work in front of you is *steady and ongoing* or *front-loaded and finite* — an in-house hire earns their fully-loaded cost back through continuous, compounding ownership of a growing codebase, while a defined batch of hardening, feature, or scaling work is often better and more cheaply handled by continuing with a contract partner already familiar with your stack, without taking on a six-figure annual commitment and the management overhead of someone you can't yet fully evaluate. The two paths aren't mutually exclusive either — several LaunchStudio clients bring in their first in-house engineer specifically to work *alongside* the team that did their original hardening, using the existing partner relationship as the technical reference point during the new hire's onboarding rather than starting that evaluation from zero.

## Real example

### A Utrecht Founder Makes Her First Technical Hire With Backup

Sanne Willems, from the opening of this article, ultimately asked the LaunchStudio team that had hardened her scheduling platform to sit in on the final technical interview for her top two candidates and review both take-home submissions against her actual codebase.

The stronger resume, on paper, turned out to be the weaker fit: the candidate's take-home solution ignored the existing authentication pattern entirely and introduced a second, redundant session-handling approach — a red flag Sanne would never have caught herself, but one the reviewing engineer flagged within twenty minutes of reading the diff. The second candidate's solution was less polished but worked cleanly within the existing structure, and the interview review specifically praised how they asked clarifying questions about the codebase's history before writing any code.

**Result:** Sanne hired the second candidate, who shipped a real feature — automated appointment reminders — independently within five weeks of starting, with no significant issues traced back to unfamiliarity with the existing codebase.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a contract development partner really evaluate a candidate for a role that isn't with them?

Yes — reviewing a technical interview or take-home test is a bounded, hourly engagement distinct from ongoing project work, and a partner who already understands your codebase can typically turn around a candidate review within a day or two.

### Should I hire a generalist or someone who specializes in my exact tech stack?

Prioritize someone comfortable reading and extending existing code over someone who only wants to write greenfield code in their preferred stack — your first hire will spend far more time maintaining and extending what exists than starting fresh projects.

### How much should I expect to pay for a take-home test review from an outside engineer?

A focused review of one or two candidates' submissions, including a plain-language written verdict, is typically a small fixed-fee or a few hours of hourly work — modest compared to the cost of a bad hire, which routinely runs into tens of thousands of euros in lost time and rework.

### What's the biggest mistake non-technical founders make when hiring their first engineer?

Judging candidates primarily on interview confidence and resume pedigree, because those are the only signals a non-technical founder can naturally evaluate — even though they correlate weakly with how someone performs maintaining a real, messy production codebase.

### Is it better to hire full-time or bring on a contractor first to test the fit?

A short, paid contract engagement on a real, bounded piece of work is often a lower-risk way to evaluate fit before committing to a full-time salary and benefits package, and several candidates who look strong on paper reveal themselves clearly, one way or the other, within a few weeks of real work.
