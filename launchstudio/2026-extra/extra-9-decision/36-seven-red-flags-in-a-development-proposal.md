The received wisdom is that the red flag is the price: if a quote comes in suspiciously low, something's wrong. It's a comforting rule because it's easy to apply, and it's mostly false. Cheap quotes are sometimes cheap because the work is genuinely narrow, and expensive quotes go wrong constantly — arguably more often, because a large budget buys more room to drift.

The reliable warning signs are structural, and they're all visible to someone who has never written a line of code. They're about what a proposal *doesn't* say, who ends up holding things, and whether the person writing it appears to have looked at your product. Here are seven, in rough order of how much they tend to cost when ignored — each with what to say, and, importantly, when it's actually fine.

## 1. The Proposal Never Mentions Your Product Specifically

Open the document and search for a word that only your app would contain. The name of your core object — "consignment," "rehearsal," "grading rubric," "tenant deposit." Then search for your prototype's tooling: Lovable, Bolt, Cursor, Supabase, whatever you actually used.

If neither appears, you're holding a template. It might be a good template written by competent people, but it means nobody has engaged with your specific situation, and everything downstream — the price, the timeline, the scope — is a guess dressed up in formatting.

**What to say:** "Before we go further, can you tell me the two things about my codebase that will make this harder or easier than a typical project?" A team that has looked will answer in thirty seconds.

**When it's fine:** Genuinely never. Even a €900 engagement deserves a paragraph that could only have been written about your product.

## 2. A Rebuild Is Recommended Before Anyone Opened the Repository

"We'd recommend rebuilding this properly" is the most expensive sentence in the industry, and it arrives astonishingly often before the vendor has been given access to anything.

The reason is not usually malice. Reading unfamiliar code — especially code generated by prompting, with no consistent conventions — is slow, unglamorous work. Rebuilding is faster for the developer and bills more hours. It also throws away the one thing you actually got right: an interface you designed by iterating with real users.

**What to say:** "What specifically can't be adapted, and what would it cost to patch that part instead of replacing everything?" A legitimate rebuild recommendation names a specific structural problem — usually the data model can't represent something the business needs — and can price both paths.

**When it's fine:** When they've read the code and can point at the thing. Some prototypes genuinely need replacing. The flag isn't the recommendation, it's the sequencing.

## 3. Hours Without Outcomes

"Backend development: 120–160 hours @ €85/hour."

This is a proposal to sell you time. Time is a real input, but it's not a deliverable, and a document built entirely from hour estimates has no definition of finished. When hour 160 arrives and password reset still doesn't work, both parties are technically correct and one of them is you.

**What to say:** "Can you restate this as outcomes I could verify — things I'd be able to do at the end?" You want lines like *"a new user can sign up, verify their email, and reset a forgotten password on the live site."*

**When it's fine:** For genuinely exploratory work — investigating a bug nobody understands, or a research spike — hourly is honest and fixed-price would be a fiction. In that case ask for a cap and a checkpoint: "twelve hours, then we talk before continuing."

## 4. Nothing Is Excluded

Read the proposal looking for the word "not." Good proposals contain a short, unglamorous exclusions list: *not included — mobile apps, content and copywriting, GDPR legal review, SEO, load testing above 500 concurrent users, third-party subscription costs.*

A proposal with no exclusions is a proposal where every gap is a future argument. Vendors who have been burned a few times write exclusions voluntarily, which is why the presence of an exclusions list is one of the strongest positive signals in the entire document.

**What to say:** "What's explicitly not included? I'd rather know now than find out in week three." Nobody has ever reacted badly to that question.

**When it's fine:** On a very small, single-item engagement — "add password reset, €400" — an exclusions list would be silly. Scale the expectation to the size of the job.

## 5. You Don't Own Things From Day One

Look for where your assets will live. The phrases to find: whose GitHub organisation holds the repository, whose account runs the hosting and database, whose name is on the payment provider, and when ownership transfers.

"Code transferred on final payment" sounds reasonable and usually is. "Deployed to our managed infrastructure" is where founders get stuck: two years later you want to move, and you discover that moving means paying the incumbent to help you leave, or rebuilding what you already own but can't reach. There's also a quieter version — the app runs in *your* accounts, but only one person has the credentials and it isn't you.

**What to say:** "On day one, whose name is on the GitHub account, the database, the hosting and the Stripe account? And can you write into the agreement that I have admin access to all of them throughout?"

**When it's fine:** Holding the *repository* until final payment is a normal protection against non-payment on a first engagement, provided the transfer is contractually automatic and you have read access throughout. Holding your *production accounts* indefinitely is a different thing and worth pushing back on.

## 6. The Team Is Anonymous

"Our team of senior developers" is not a team. Two or three people will do your work, their identities are knowable, and their availability is the single largest determinant of whether your project goes well.

The failure mode here is the bait-and-switch that nobody intends: you meet impressive people during sales, sign, and get whoever was free. It's rarely dishonest — the impressive people got pulled onto a bigger account — but the effect on you is identical.

**What to say:** "Who are the two people who'd do this work, what else are they on for the next six weeks, and what happens if one of them is reassigned?" Then ask a reference the same thing about their project.

**When it's fine:** Larger firms genuinely can't guarantee individuals across long engagements, and honest ones say so while committing to continuity — one named lead who stays for the whole project even if support around them rotates. That's a reasonable structure. "Our senior team" with no names and no commitment is not.

## 7. Everything After Launch Is a Blank

Launch is not the end of the project; it's the beginning of the part where real users find things. A proposal that stops at "go live" leaves the most predictable expense in the whole endeavour undefined.

What you're looking for: how many days of bug fixing are included, what counts as a bug (the software not doing what the scope said it does — as opposed to a new feature you thought of afterwards), what the response time is, what happens on day 31, and what ongoing hosting and monitoring cost per month.

**What to say:** "What's covered for the first thirty days, at what response time, and what does month two cost me if I want you available?"

**When it's fine:** Zero included support is defensible if it's stated plainly and priced — "no support included; ad hoc work at €95/hour with a two-business-day response." That's an honest deal. Silence isn't.

## Four Things That Look Like Flags and Aren't

Being able to dismiss false alarms matters as much as spotting real ones, because founders regularly reject good partners for the wrong reasons.

**A low price.** LaunchStudio-style last-mile work runs €800–€3,500 against agency quotes of €20,000+ for the same outcome, and the gap is scope and rebuild-avoidance, not corner-cutting. Judge the scope, not the number.

**A short proposal.** Three tight pages with acceptance criteria beat fourteen pages of methodology. Length is often inversely correlated with specificity.

**A deposit request.** 30–50% up front is standard for small engagements and protects both sides. 100% up front is the version worth resisting.

**A remote or offshore team.** Distributed delivery has been standard practice for a decade; the questions that matter are overlap hours, who your named contact is, and response times — not postcodes.

**A boilerplate contract.** The legal agreement is *supposed* to be boilerplate — payment terms, liability, termination and IP assignment don't need to be creative, and a bespoke contract for a €2,000 engagement mostly means legal fees somebody is paying for. The creativity belongs in the scope annex. Founders sometimes get this exactly backwards: they scrutinise the standard clauses their lawyer has seen a thousand times while skimming the two-page scope document that decides what actually gets built. Read the boilerplate once for the ownership and termination clauses, then spend your remaining attention on the annex.

## The Email That Tests All Seven at Once

Send this to every vendor you're considering, identically. It takes them fifteen minutes and it tests specificity, exclusions, ownership, team and support in one pass.

> Thanks for the proposal. Before I decide, five quick things:
>
> 1. What are the two things about my specific codebase that make this easier or harder than usual?
> 2. Can you restate the deliverables as outcomes I'd be able to verify myself?
> 3. What's explicitly excluded?
> 4. On day one, whose accounts hold the code, database, hosting and payment provider?
> 5. Who are the named people doing the work, and what's covered in the thirty days after launch?

Grade the replies on speed and specificity. You'll usually have your answer before you've finished reading the third one.

## Real example

### A Founder in Action: The Word That Wasn't in the Document

Bram Sterkenburg, a former veterinary practice manager in Zwolle, built PetChart — a Lovable app for small animal clinics to share treatment histories with owners — and received a well-designed sixteen-page proposal for €14,500 that he found genuinely impressive.

Searching it for the word "clinic" returned nothing. Searching for "Lovable," "Supabase" or the name of his app returned nothing. The document described a generic web application build, recommended starting fresh in a different framework, and quoted 168 hours of development against no verifiable outcomes. When Bram sent the five-question email, the answer to question 4 was that hosting would sit on the agency's managed platform at €190/month.

He asked two other vendors the same five questions. One answered within four hours, named the two engineers, listed six exclusions, and pointed out that his treatment-notes table had no clinic filter on the server — a detail that could only have come from reading the code.

**Result:** PetChart went live in twelve working days for €2,950 fixed, with server-side clinic isolation, verified Mollie payments, and every account registered to Bram's own company.

> *"Sixteen pages and not one of them mentioned veterinary clinics. Once I noticed that, the rest of the document stopped being impressive and started being wallpaper."*
> — **Bram Sterkenburg, Founder, PetChart (Zwolle)**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many of these seven flags should make me walk away?

One flag is a conversation, two is a negotiation, three is usually a no. What matters more than the count is the reaction: a vendor who fixes an omission gladly is different from one who explains why your question is unnecessary.

### Is it reasonable to ask a vendor to rewrite their proposal?

Yes, and it's a useful test in itself. A revision request that takes two days and comes back more specific tells you a lot; one that takes two weeks and comes back reworded tells you more.

### What if the only vendor I can afford has several of these flags?

Then shrink the engagement rather than accepting the risk — buy one small, well-defined piece first and see how it goes. A €600 job that finishes cleanly is a better foundation than a €6,000 job with undefined ownership.

### Should I worry if a proposal has no timeline, only a price?

Yes, but it's usually easy to fix. Ask for a start date, an expected end date, and what would need to happen for the end date to move — those three answers convert a price into a plan.

### Does a signed NDA make any of these concerns go away?

No. An NDA governs confidentiality only; it says nothing about ownership, scope, team continuity or what happens after launch. Founders regularly treat a signed NDA as evidence of overall diligence, and it isn't.
