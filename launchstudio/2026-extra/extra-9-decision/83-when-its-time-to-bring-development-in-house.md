Everyone treats bringing development in-house as the milestone that proves a startup has made it — the moment a founder stops "just" using contractors and finally has a real engineering team. Nobody mentions that the honest math, run properly with fully-loaded Dutch salary costs against the actual, ongoing workload a lot of scale-up SaaS products generate, frequently doesn't support making that move as early as pride suggests it should. This isn't an argument against ever hiring in-house — plenty of companies reach a point where it's clearly the right call. It's an argument against treating "we should have our own developers by now" as a status decision instead of a financial one, because the two paths have genuinely different break-even points, and getting the timing wrong in either direction is expensive.

## The Status Trap: Why Founders Rush This Decision

There's a specific kind of pressure that pushes founders toward in-house hiring earlier than the numbers justify, and it has almost nothing to do with workload. Investors ask about team size as a proxy for seriousness. Other founders in your network talk about "our engineers" in a way that quietly implies contract work is a phase you're supposed to graduate out of. A co-founder or early employee may push for it because managing a contractor relationship feels less prestigious than managing a direct report. None of these pressures are irrational exactly — team size genuinely does correlate with certain kinds of maturity — but none of them are a substitute for actually comparing what continued contract engineering costs against what an in-house hire costs, for your specific, current workload, this year, not the workload you hope to have in eighteen months.

## The Real Cost of an In-House Engineer in the Netherlands

The number founders anchor on — a salary offer of, say, €75,000 — is never the real number. Dutch employer costs add roughly 20–30% on top of gross salary once the mandatory 8% holiday allowance, pension contributions, and employer social security premiums are included, pushing that €75,000 hire to a fully-loaded cost closer to €95,000–€100,000 before a single line of code ships. Add recruiting costs — a specialized technical recruiter typically charges 15–25% of first-year salary, or the equivalent in founder time spent sourcing and screening candidates you may not be equipped to fully evaluate yourself — and realistic onboarding time, during which a new hire is meaningfully less productive than their eventual steady state, and the true first-year cost of one mid-level in-house engineer commonly lands between €110,000 and €130,000. That's before accounting for management overhead: someone now needs to set direction, review work, handle underperformance if it happens, and cover the gap during illness or vacation — overhead that's easy to underweight because it's diffuse rather than a line item, but very real for a founder who's now also, functionally, an engineering manager.

## The Real Cost of Continued Contract or Partner Work

Set against that, continued work with a contract partner has a cost structure that looks different in almost every dimension. There's no recruiting cost, no onboarding ramp — a partner who already hardened your product for launch retains context between engagements rather than starting from zero — and no employment overhead: no holiday allowance, no pension contribution, no severance exposure if the relationship ends, no management burden beyond scoping the next piece of work clearly. The trade-off is that you're paying a rate that reflects specialized, senior expertise on demand rather than a averaged annual salary, and the relationship is inherently less "always available at a moment's notice" than someone sitting in your Slack all day. For a product with real but not yet constant engineering demand — a scale-up shipping a meaningful feature every few weeks, fixing bugs as they surface, occasionally scaling infrastructure — the total annual cost of continued partner engagement frequently comes in at a fraction of the fully-loaded cost of an in-house hire, particularly when you factor in that a partner relationship can flex down in quiet months in a way a salary can't.

## The Crossover Point: When the Math Actually Flips

The math genuinely does flip, and it's worth naming the specific signals rather than treating this as permanently one-sided. The crossover tends to happen when three conditions are true simultaneously: engineering work has become continuous rather than project-shaped — there's always a next thing, not periodic sprints with gaps between them; the work increasingly requires deep, cumulative product context that's expensive to keep re-explaining to an external partner, even a good one, on every engagement; and the volume of work reliably exceeds what a partner relationship can absorb at a rate that's still cheaper than a salary — roughly, once you're consistently paying a contract partner more per quarter than a quarter of a fully-loaded salary would cost, the argument for in-house strengthens fast. Below that threshold, in-house hiring is usually solving a status problem, not a capacity problem, dressed up as a strategic decision.

## A Hybrid Path Most Founders Don't Consider

The in-house-versus-contract framing is falsely binary, and the most financially sound path for a lot of scale-up SaaS founders is neither pure option but a deliberate sequence: continue with a contract partner through the period of variable, project-shaped demand, and bring on a first in-house hire specifically once the workload has become genuinely continuous — using the existing partner relationship as a resource during that new hire's onboarding rather than starting the technical evaluation and knowledge transfer from zero. This is precisely the structure several LaunchStudio clients land on: the same partner team that did the original hardening and holds ongoing context on the codebase becomes the technical second opinion and onboarding resource for a founder's first internal hire, which meaningfully de-risks that hire's first few months without requiring the founder to make an all-or-nothing bet on either path.

## What You Actually Give Up Going In-House Too Early

Beyond the direct cost comparison, there's a flexibility cost to hiring in-house before the workload justifies it that founders consistently underweight. A salaried engineer needs to be kept busy — not maliciously, but structurally, since idle in-house capacity is a sunk cost either way, which quietly creates pressure to invent work, take on scope that wasn't actually necessary, or rebuild things that were working fine, simply because there's now a person whose job is to be building something. A contract relationship has no equivalent pressure: work happens when there's real work to do, and stops cleanly when there isn't, without the awkward dynamic of a founder managing someone through a slow quarter. Dutch employment law also matters here in a way founders sometimes discover too late — ending a permanent contract, even during genuine underperformance or a downturn, is considerably more involved and costly than not renewing a fixed-term contract or simply scoping down a partner engagement, which makes the in-house decision meaningfully less reversible than it feels at the moment of hiring.

## Running Your Own Break-Even Calculation

The comparison doesn't require a finance background, just discipline about which numbers to actually include. Take your trailing twelve months of contract or partner spend on engineering — invoices, not estimates — and compare it directly against the fully-loaded first-year cost of the specific hire you're considering: gross salary, plus 20–30% for Dutch statutory costs, plus realistic recruiting spend, plus a conservative ramp-up discount (treating the new hire as roughly 50% as productive as steady-state for the first two to three months is a reasonable default). If your trailing contract spend is already higher than that fully-loaded number, the in-house case is strong. If it's meaningfully lower — say, you spent €38,000 on contract work against a €100,000 fully-loaded hire — the honest read is that you don't yet have enough continuous work to justify the move, regardless of how the org chart feels. Run this calculation with real invoices in front of you rather than a vague sense of "we spend a lot on developers," because the gap between the felt cost and the actual cost is usually where the premature decision gets made.

## What You Actually Gain Going In-House at the Right Time

None of this is an argument that in-house hiring is a mistake — at the right point, it's clearly correct, and the gains are real. It's worth being honest, too, about the dimension no spreadsheet captures well: an in-house hire who's genuinely invested in the product's success, sitting in on customer calls and feeling the weight of a bad week the way a founder does, brings a form of commitment that a rotating cast of contractors structurally can't replicate, no matter how good the individual work is. That's a real gain, not a soft one — it shows up in fewer things falling through the cracks between engagements and in decisions made with more context than any scoping document can convey. The point isn't that this dimension doesn't matter; it's that it matters most once the financial case has already cleared the bar, not as a justification for jumping the queue ahead of it. An in-house engineer builds compounding product context no external partner can fully replicate, since they're immersed in the product daily rather than re-engaging periodically. They can respond to urgent issues without a scoping conversation first. They become a cultural and technical anchor for the second and third hires that follow, someone who already understands the codebase's history and can onboard the next person faster than any external party could. And past a certain scale, the fully-loaded cost of one senior in-house engineer genuinely does become cheaper than the equivalent volume of contract work, simply because continuous, high-volume work is what salaried employment is economically built for. The point of this article isn't to argue against ever crossing that line — it's to argue for crossing it because the math says so, not because it felt overdue.

## Real example

### A Groningen Founder Runs the Numbers Before Hiring

Bram Oosterhuis had been paying a development partner roughly €4,200 a month, on average, to maintain and extend Vlootbeheer, a fleet-management SaaS for small logistics companies, for just over a year. Every founder he talked to in his accelerator cohort had an in-house engineer, and he'd started to feel behind for not having one, assuming it would obviously be cheaper than what he was already spending.

Before making an offer to a candidate he'd sourced, Bram ran the fully-loaded comparison: the €72,000 gross salary he was prepared to offer came to roughly €96,000 once Dutch employer costs were included, plus an estimated €15,000 in recruiting and onboarding costs in year one — against the roughly €50,000 he'd spent on contract work that same year, for a product whose engineering demand, while steady, still had genuine quiet weeks.

**Result:** Bram held off on the in-house hire for another eight months, during which Vlootbeheer's workload became consistently full — at which point he made the hire, using his existing contract partner to run technical interviews and onboard the new engineer, cutting the typical ramp-up time roughly in half.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is continued contract work ever cheaper even at significant scale?

Generally no, past a certain volume — once engineering demand is continuous and high enough, the averaged cost efficiency of a salaried employee typically overtakes contract rates, which is exactly the crossover point this article describes rather than an argument against ever hiring.

### How do I estimate the fully-loaded cost of a Dutch engineering hire accurately?

Take the gross salary and add roughly 20–30% for mandatory holiday allowance, pension, and employer social contributions, then add realistic recruiting costs (15–25% of first-year salary if using a recruiter) and factor in a genuine ramp-up period of reduced early productivity.

### What's the biggest hidden cost of hiring in-house too early?

Reduced flexibility — a salaried hire needs to be kept busy, which quietly creates pressure to invent or over-scope work during slow periods, and ending the relationship later is considerably more involved under Dutch employment law than scaling down a contract engagement.

### Can I use a contract partner and an in-house engineer at the same time?

Yes, and it's a common and effective hybrid — many founders bring on their first in-house hire specifically while keeping their existing contract partner involved for onboarding and technical continuity, rather than treating it as an either-or switch.

### What's the clearest sign the crossover point has actually arrived?

When you're consistently paying a contract partner more per quarter than a quarter of a fully-loaded in-house salary would cost, and the work has shifted from project-shaped to genuinely continuous with no natural gaps between engagements.
