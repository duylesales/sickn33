**Short answer:** A second opinion on a developer quote checks whether the proposed work matches what your app actually needs, whether important areas are missing, whether a rebuild is really necessary, whether ownership and access are arranged in your favour and whether the price and timeline are realistic. LaunchStudio reviews the quote together with a short look at your app and tells you plainly what we think, including when the quote you received is good and you should accept it.

Founders without technical background face a difficult moment when a quote arrives. It may be one page or twenty, it may propose fixes or a full rebuild, and the price may be €900 or €25,000. Without a technical adviser, it is hard to know whether the scope is right, whether the price is fair and what risks are hidden in the terms. Asking a second party to review the quote is common in construction, legal work and finance. In software it is less common, but just as useful.

## Key Takeaways

- **A second opinion compares the quote with your app,** not just with general expectations.
- **The most common finding is missing scope,** not overpricing.
- **Rebuild proposals deserve particular scrutiny** and a comparison with fixing in place.
- **Ownership and access terms** can matter more than the price.
- **Sometimes the best advice is to accept the quote you have.**

## When a Second Opinion Is Worth It

- The quote is large compared with your budget or revenue
- The quote proposes a rebuild of an app that works
- You received several quotes with very different prices
- The scope is vague, or you do not understand parts of it
- Accounts or hosting would move into the supplier's name
- The supplier is pressing you to sign quickly
- A customer or investor will rely on the result

## Typical Findings

**Missing scope.** The most common issue is not that a quote is too expensive, but that it covers too little. A low quote may fix a few known bugs but leave out database policies, payment webhooks, staging, backups and monitoring. The founder believes the app is production ready afterwards, and the real risks remain.

**Unnecessary rebuilds.** Some proposals recommend rebuilding a working app in a different framework without explaining which problems cannot be fixed in place. A rebuild may cost many times more and take months, while the actual problems could be fixed in days or weeks.

**Ownership risks.** Some quotes include hosting in the supplier's account, sometimes with a monthly fee and markup. That creates dependency and makes switching expensive later.

**Vague deliverables.** "We will make the app secure and scalable" without specific items, tests or documentation makes it impossible to know whether the work was done.

**Unrealistic timelines.** Very short timelines for large scopes, or very long ones for small fixes, both suggest the supplier has not looked closely at the app.

**Good quotes.** Sometimes the quote is clear, complete and fairly priced. Then we say so, and you can proceed with confidence.

## Questions You Can Ask Your Supplier After the Review

The review usually ends with a short list of questions to send to your supplier, such as:

- Will you test that users cannot see each other's data, and how?
- How will payments be confirmed, and what happens with failed renewals and refunds?
- Will staging, backups with a restore test and monitoring be included?
- Will the accounts and domain be in our company's name?
- Which specific problems require a rebuild, and what would fixing them in place cost?
- What will we receive in writing at the end?
- What happens if you find something unexpected?

Good suppliers welcome these questions. Their answers often lead to a better quote, either from them or from someone else.

## Comparing Several Quotes

When you have several quotes, the review includes a comparison: normalising scope, assumptions, exclusions, deliverables and pricing models, then highlighting where they differ and why. Often, the totals end up closer than they looked, or the ranking changes once missing scope is accounted for.

## When to Ask for a Second Opinion

The best moment is before signing. If you have already started a project and have doubts, a review can still help: it can identify missing scope before delivery, suggest questions to ask and help you decide whether to continue, adjust or stop. In that case, we focus first on securing ownership of accounts, because that protects you whatever you decide.

## Reading a Quote Yourself First

Before asking for a second opinion, a quick self-check helps you understand what to look for. Can you list, from the quote, exactly what will be done? Are there items marked as excluded? Is there a sentence about who owns the accounts and code? Does the quote mention testing with different user roles, payment confirmation, backups and monitoring? Is there any support after delivery? Each "no" is a question worth asking the supplier, and a reason why a second opinion may be valuable.

## Second Opinions for Ongoing Work

A second opinion is not only useful for new projects. Founders on monthly retainers or hourly arrangements sometimes want to know whether the work they are paying for is the right work: are hours spent on important risks or on cosmetic changes, is the code getting better or worse, are accounts and documentation in order? A review of the repository history, open issues and recent changes, combined with a short look at the app, can answer these questions and help you steer the relationship.

## Red Flags We Often See in Quotes

Some patterns appear again and again in quotes for AI-built apps. A single paragraph promising "full production readiness" without listing what that means. A recommendation to rebuild in the supplier's favourite framework, with no explanation of which problems cannot be fixed in place. Hosting moved into the supplier's account at a monthly fee. No mention of payments in an app that takes payments. No mention of testing with different user roles. Large upfront payments for long projects. "Unlimited revisions" as a selling point. Each of these is not automatically wrong, but each deserves a question before you sign.

## A Second Opinion on Your Own Plan

Some founders do not have a quote yet, but a plan: "I will ask my AI tool to fix security, then hire a freelancer for payments, then launch next month." A second opinion works for plans too. We look at the app and the plan and tell you which parts are realistic, which risks remain uncovered and where professional help makes the biggest difference. That helps you spend your budget on the areas that matter most.

## Keeping the Relationship With Your Supplier Healthy

Asking for a second opinion does not have to damage your relationship with the supplier. Present it as normal due diligence, share the questions rather than the review itself if you prefer, and give the supplier a chance to clarify or adjust. Most good suppliers respond constructively, and the project often starts on a clearer footing as a result.

## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: The Quote That Was Actually Good

Tessa Vermeulen runs a SaaS for wedding photographers from Den Bosch, built with v0, Cursor and Supabase, where photographers share galleries, contracts and invoices with couples. She received a quote of €4,200 from a local freelancer to make the app production ready and add an organisation model for photography studios. It felt high, so she asked LaunchStudio for a second opinion.

The review compared the quote with a short look at the app. The freelancer's scope was specific: policies per studio and couple with tests, private storage for galleries with signed links, Stripe webhooks with reconciliation, staging and a pipeline, email authentication, backups with a restore test and monitoring. The organisation model was included, accounts would stay in Tessa's name and the freelancer offered two weeks of support. Two items were missing: an export of a couple's gallery and data when they requested deletion, and project instructions for Cursor. The price was plausible for the scope.

LaunchStudio recommended accepting the quote and asking the freelancer to add the two missing items. The freelancer added them for a small extra amount.

**Result:** Tessa signed with the freelancer, and the project went well. A year later, when she needed SSO for a large studio chain, she came back to LaunchStudio for a Launch & Grow project.
