Accessibility means that people with disabilities can use your app: people who navigate with a keyboard instead of a mouse, who use a screen reader, who need higher contrast or larger text, who have limited motor control or who process information differently. The European Commission estimates that around 87 million people in the EU live with some form of disability, and many more benefit from accessible design in specific situations, such as using a phone in bright sunlight or with one hand.

Accessibility is also increasingly a legal and commercial requirement. The European Accessibility Act applies to a range of products and services from 28 June 2025, including many e-commerce services. Public sector organisations in the Netherlands must meet accessibility standards for their websites and apps, and often require the same from suppliers. Larger businesses increasingly ask about accessibility in procurement.

AI-built apps often look polished but are difficult to use without a mouse or with a screen reader. LaunchStudio's accessibility compliance service reviews your app against WCAG 2.2 level AA, fixes the issues in your code and prepares an accessibility statement, at a fixed price.

## Does the Law Apply to You?

Whether accessibility is legally required depends on what you offer and to whom. In broad terms:

- **The European Accessibility Act** covers specific products and services, including e-commerce services, consumer banking, e-books, certain transport services and electronic communications. Microenterprises providing services, generally those with fewer than ten employees and an annual turnover or balance sheet total of no more than €2 million, are exempt from the service requirements. The Act is implemented in national law, and the details matter.
- **Dutch public sector rules** require government bodies to make websites and apps accessible according to the relevant European standard, which refers to WCAG. If you sell to municipalities, provinces, schools or other public bodies, they will often require accessibility from you.
- **Procurement requirements** from larger businesses may include accessibility even when the law does not.

We are not lawyers. If you are unsure whether the Act applies to your product, ask a legal adviser. In practice, many founders choose to meet WCAG 2.2 AA regardless, because it widens their market and simplifies sales to larger customers.

## Typical Issues in AI-Built Apps

| Issue | Who is affected | Typical fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clickable elements built from generic containers | Keyboard and screen reader users | Use real buttons and links, or add correct roles and keyboard handling |
| Icon buttons without labels | Screen reader users | Accessible names for every control |
| Form fields with placeholder text only | Screen reader users, users with memory difficulties | Visible labels connected to fields |
| Error messages shown only in red | Colour-blind and screen reader users | Text errors linked to fields and announced |
| Light grey text on white | Low vision users | Contrast-compliant colours in the design tokens |
| Modals that do not trap or return focus | Keyboard and screen reader users | Proper dialog pattern with focus management |
| Custom dropdowns and date pickers | Keyboard and screen reader users | Accessible component patterns or native controls |
| Missing headings structure | Screen reader users | Logical heading levels on every page |
| Hidden focus outlines | Keyboard users | Visible, consistent focus styles |
| Toast notifications not announced | Screen reader users | Live regions for status messages |

AI builders generate interfaces from visual descriptions, so they optimise for how things look, not how they work for assistive technology. Many of these issues come from a few shared components, which is why fixes often have a broad effect.

## Automated Versus Manual Testing

Automated tools catch a meaningful share of accessibility issues, such as missing alternative text, low contrast and missing labels. They cannot judge whether alternative text is meaningful, whether the tab order makes sense or whether a screen reader user can actually complete a task. Manual testing with a keyboard and screen readers is essential. We use both, and add automated checks to your pipeline so new regressions are caught early.

## Testing With Real Assistive Technology

Our manual tests use the tools people actually rely on: keyboard-only navigation in common browsers, VoiceOver on macOS and iOS, TalkBack on Android and NVDA on Windows, plus browser zoom and operating system text-size settings. Each key journey is completed from start to finish with these tools, because a page that passes automated checks can still be impossible to use when a dialog steals focus or a status message is never announced.

## Keeping Your Design

Founders sometimes worry that accessibility will make their app look worse. In practice, most fixes are invisible or subtle: labels added for screen readers, better focus outlines, slightly darker text colours, proper semantics underneath the same visual design. Where contrast changes are needed, we adjust colours in your design tokens so the brand stays recognisable. Many founders find the app feels clearer for everyone afterwards.

## The Accessibility Statement

An accessibility statement explains how accessible your app is, which standard you aim for, which known limitations exist, what you are doing about them and how users can report problems. Public sector bodies in the Netherlands are required to publish statements in a specific format, and many public sector buyers expect suppliers to provide similar information. For businesses, a clear statement builds trust and shows commitment. We draft it with you based on the actual results of the review, so it is honest and specific.

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: The Municipal Tender That Asked for WCAG

Gerben Huisman runs a SaaS for booking sports facilities from Zwolle, built with v0 and Supabase, used by sports clubs to reserve halls and fields. Three municipalities wanted to use it for public bookings by residents, and their tender required WCAG 2.1 AA conformance with an accessibility statement. Gerben had never tested the app with a keyboard.

The review found that the booking calendar was built from generic containers that could not be reached with the keyboard, time slots were distinguished only by colour, icon buttons had no labels, the booking dialog did not manage focus and error messages appeared only as red borders. Contrast failed on secondary text throughout, and the confirmation PDF had no structure.

LaunchStudio quoted €2,800. The calendar was rebuilt as an accessible grid with keyboard navigation and screen reader announcements, availability was shown with text and patterns as well as colour, every control received an accessible name, dialogs followed the standard pattern and form errors were linked to fields and announced. Contrast was fixed in the design tokens, preserving the brand colours with slightly darker shades. The PDF template gained headings and tags. Automated checks were added to the pipeline, and an accessibility statement listed one remaining limitation with a planned fix date.

**Result:** All three municipalities accepted the statement in the tender, and residents started booking through Gerben's platform. A club secretary who uses a screen reader emailed to say she could make bookings herself for the first time.
