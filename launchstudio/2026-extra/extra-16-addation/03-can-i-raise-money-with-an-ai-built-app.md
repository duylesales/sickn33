**Short answer:** yes. Investors fund businesses, not codebases, and many investors now expect early products to be built with AI tools because it is fast and capital-efficient. What has changed is the questions they ask. Because a demo is cheap to produce, they look harder at traction, defensibility and technical risk: is the product used and paid for, what stops someone from copying it in a weekend, is customer data secure, does the company own its code and accounts, and can the product grow without being rebuilt? Prepare clear answers, and make sure the product behind your pitch is genuinely live and production-ready.

## Key Takeaways

- **Using AI tools is not a disadvantage in itself;** speed and capital efficiency are attractive to investors.
- **Demos have lost their signalling value.** Real usage, retention and revenue matter more than ever.
- **Defensibility must come from more than code:** distribution, data, workflow depth, integrations, domain expertise and customer relationships.
- **Technical due diligence looks for specific risks** common in AI-built products: security gaps, ownership issues, founder dependency and scalability limits.
- **A live, secure product with documented architecture** strengthens any raise and shortens due diligence.

## What Investors Actually Evaluate

At early stages, investors typically weigh:

| Factor | What they look for |
| --- | --- |
| Team | Founder-market fit, ability to execute, ability to attract talent |
| Problem and market | A painful problem, a large or growing market, timing |
| Traction | Users, engagement, retention, revenue, pilots, letters of intent |
| Product | Evidence that the product solves the problem and that customers use it |
| Defensibility | Why this company can win and keep winning |
| Business model | How it makes money and whether unit economics can work |
| Risk | Technical, market, regulatory and execution risks |

The codebase sits mostly under "product" and "risk". For most early-stage investors, it is not the main reason to invest, but serious technical problems can be a reason not to.

## How AI Tools Changed the Conversation

Before AI builders, a working product required either technical founders or significant money spent on developers. A functioning product with users signalled ability and commitment. Today, a convincing prototype can be produced in days. Investors know this, so they ask:

- **"Why can't someone else build this in a weekend?"**
- **"What happens when the big AI platforms add this as a feature?"**
- **"Is the product actually used, or just demonstrated?"**
- **"Who can maintain and extend this when you grow?"**

These are fair questions, and good answers are possible.

## Defensibility Beyond Code

If code is cheap, defensibility must come from elsewhere:

- **Distribution:** a channel to customers that others lack, such as partnerships, an audience, a community or a sales network in a specific sector
- **Domain expertise:** deep understanding of a niche, its rules, workflows and language, often from the founder's own background
- **Workflow depth:** being embedded in customers' daily processes, with integrations and data that make switching costly
- **Data:** proprietary or accumulated data that improves the product over time
- **Trust and compliance:** meeting sector requirements, such as healthcare security standards or public procurement rules, that competitors would take time to meet
- **Customer relationships:** contracts, references and reputation in a market
- **Speed of iteration:** the ability to ship and learn faster, which AI tools amplify

A Dutch founder building for municipalities, healthcare practices or logistics companies often has defensibility in sector knowledge, compliance and relationships that a weekend clone cannot replicate.

## Traction Matters More Than Ever

When demos are cheap, evidence of demand is the strongest signal:

- **Active usage,** not just sign-ups: weekly active users, sessions, key actions
- **Retention:** do users and companies keep using it after weeks and months?
- **Revenue,** even modest: paying customers prove willingness to pay
- **Pilots with recognisable organisations,** ideally converting to paid contracts
- **Qualitative evidence:** customer quotes, case studies, problems solved

A live product on your own domain with real customers is far more persuasive than a polished prototype on a preview URL.

## What Technical Due Diligence Looks For

Investors, especially at seed and later stages, often ask a technical adviser to review the product. In AI-built products, they typically check:

- **Security:** access control between customers, secrets management, authentication, payment handling. Broken access control tops the OWASP Top 10 and is common in AI-built apps.
- **Ownership:** code in the company's repository, accounts for hosting, database, domain and payments in the company's name, contractor work assigned to the company, AI tool terms.
- **Architecture:** a coherent structure that can grow, documented reasonably.
- **Scalability:** whether the product can handle the growth in the plan without a rewrite.
- **Founder dependency:** whether someone other than the founder, or the AI tool, can understand and maintain the system.
- **Dependencies and licences:** no problematic licences, no abandoned or risky packages.

## Answering "Isn't This Easy to Copy?"

A strong answer acknowledges the question and explains what is hard to copy:

> "Anyone could build a similar interface. What took us eighteen months is understanding how dental practices actually manage stock, integrating with the three suppliers they use, meeting the security requirements of practice groups and building relationships with 40 practices that now use us weekly. The code is the easiest part of what we have."

Back it with evidence: integrations, customer numbers, retention, partnerships and compliance work.

## Metrics Investors Ask For

Prepare a simple, honest metrics overview. Investors at early stages commonly look at:

| Metric | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Weekly or monthly active users and accounts | Real usage, not just sign-ups |
| Activation rate | Share of new users reaching the first meaningful outcome |
| Retention by cohort | Whether users keep coming back after weeks and months |
| Revenue and growth rate | Monthly recurring revenue, growth month over month |
| Customer acquisition channels | Where customers come from and what it costs |
| Gross margin | Revenue minus direct costs, including infrastructure and AI usage |
| Pipeline | Pilots, trials and late-stage conversations |

Track these through product analytics and your payment provider, with definitions you can explain. A smaller number with clear definitions is more convincing than impressive figures that fall apart under questions.

## Telling the Build Story Well

How you describe the way the product was built matters. Founders sometimes apologise for using AI tools, or exaggerate the sophistication of what they built. Neither helps. A confident, accurate story works better: "I built the first version with Lovable in six weeks to test demand. When pilots confirmed it, we invested in production readiness: security reviewed, data isolated per customer, monitoring and backups in place. The funding goes to growth and our first engineering hire, not to a rebuild." This tells investors that you move fast, know when to invest in quality and will spend their money well.

## What to Do After the Raise

Investors want to see funds turned into growth. For AI-built products, the first technical investments after raising are often a first engineering hire or a reliable development partner, structured development practices with tests and reviews, and the integrations or compliance work that unlock larger customers. A product that is already production-ready makes these steps far smoother.

## When to Invest in Production Readiness Before Raising

Making the product production-ready before raising is usually worthwhile when:

- You are about to onboard customers whose usage is your main traction argument
- The investor's technical review is likely to find serious security or ownership issues
- Your pitch depends on enterprise or regulated customers who require security answers
- The product currently runs on a preview URL or in a fragile state

A modest, fixed-price production launch before fundraising often costs less than the valuation impact of serious due diligence findings, and it lets you spend the raised money on growth rather than on emergency fixes.

## Bootstrapping as an Alternative

AI tools also make it more realistic to grow without outside capital, funded by customers. If your product reaches paying customers quickly and costs little to run, revenue-funded growth keeps ownership and focus. Many founders raise later, on better terms, once traction is clear.

## Common Mistakes

**Hiding that the product was built with AI.** Reviewers will see it. Be open and explain how quality was ensured.

**Pitching features instead of traction.** Investors have seen many demos.

**Overclaiming scalability or security.** Statements that do not survive review damage credibility.

**Ignoring ownership.** Accounts in personal names or contractors without IP assignment are easy to fix early and awkward to fix late.

**Planning to spend the round on a rebuild.** Investors prefer funding growth. A rebuild plan suggests the current product is not a foundation.

## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: The Pitch That Moved From Demo to Traction

Marco Dekker, a former transport planner, built Rittenbord with Lovable and Supabase: a planning tool for small taxi and patient transport companies that schedules rides, assigns drivers and handles invoicing to healthcare insurers' contracted parties. He pitched to several angel investors using a polished demo, and the feedback was consistent: impressive, but what stops someone else from building it, and does anyone use it?

Marco had six pilot companies, but the app ran on a preview URL, pilots used shared logins, and two pilot companies had stopped using it after data from one company appeared in another's ride list. He also had no clear answer about ownership: the Supabase project was in his personal account and an early freelancer's work had never been formally assigned.

LaunchStudio took Rittenbord to production on its own domain with Row Level Security per transport company, individual driver and planner accounts with MFA for planners, EU hosting with backups and monitoring and verified invoicing exports. Accounts moved to Marco's company, the freelancer signed an IP assignment, and engineers produced an architecture overview, a security overview and a known-issues list. Marco re-engaged the two lost pilots and used product analytics to measure weekly active planners and rides scheduled per company.

**Result:** Three months later, Marco pitched again with eleven companies using Rittenbord weekly, around 9,000 rides scheduled per month and four paying customers. An angel syndicate invested, with a technical review that raised only minor points, and part of the round funded his first developer hire rather than a rebuild.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will investors fund a product built with Lovable or other AI tools?

Many will. Investors fund businesses with strong teams, markets and traction. Building with AI tools signals speed and capital efficiency. What matters is whether the product is used, defensible and free of serious technical risks.

### What do investors check in an AI-built codebase?

Typically security and access control, ownership of code and accounts, architecture and scalability, dependency and licence risks, and whether someone other than the founder can maintain it. Preparing documentation and fixing critical issues beforehand shortens due diligence.

### How do I show defensibility if my product is easy to build?

Show what is hard to copy: domain expertise, distribution, integrations, data, compliance work, customer relationships and evidence of retention. Explain that the code is the easiest part, and back it with metrics and customer evidence.
