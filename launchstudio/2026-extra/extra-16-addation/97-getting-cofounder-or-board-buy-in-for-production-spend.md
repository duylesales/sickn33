**Short answer:** To get a co-founder, partner or board to approve production spend, present it as a business decision rather than a technical one: what could go wrong, what it would cost, what the spend prevents or enables and how its results will be measured. Bring evidence from your own app, such as a short review of current risks, a fixed-price quote with a clear scope and the deals or launches that depend on it. A one-page proposal with these elements usually works better than technical detail.

Many founders who built their app with AI tools are not the only decision-maker. A co-founder handles finances, a business partner co-owns the company, an advisory board meets monthly or an investor expects to be consulted on significant spending. When the founder proposes spending a few thousand euros on making the app production ready, the response is often: "But the app works. Why spend money on something customers cannot see?"

This page helps you answer that question convincingly, without technical jargon.

## Key Takeaways

- **Frame it as risk and opportunity,** not as code quality.
- **Use evidence from your own app,** not general warnings.
- **Quantify what you can:** revenue at risk, deals that depend on it, time saved.
- **A fixed price with a clear scope** makes approval easier than an open estimate.
- **Agree how success will be measured** before the work starts.

## Why "The App Works" Is Not the Whole Story

An AI-built app can work well in daily use while carrying risks that do not show until something goes wrong: customers able to see each other's data, payments that sometimes grant access without confirmation, accounts in a former freelancer's name, backups never restored, no alert when the app goes down. Decision-makers who cannot see these risks naturally underestimate them. Your job is to make them visible in terms that matter to the business.

## Framing the Decision

| Instead of saying | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "The code needs to be cleaned up" | "There are three ways a customer could see another customer's data today" |
| "We need better security" | "Our largest prospect requires a security questionnaire before signing" |
| "We should add monitoring" | "Last month the app was down for four hours before a customer told us" |
| "Payments need refactoring" | "We found 18 members with access but no active payment" |
| "We need staging" | "Two releases broke the app for customers this quarter" |
| "Our accounts are messy" | "Our database is in a former freelancer's personal account" |

Specific, factual statements about your own situation are far more persuasive than general principles.

## Quantifying the Risks

You do not need precise numbers, but rough estimates help:

- **Revenue at risk:** What would a day of downtime cost? What do payment errors cost per month?
- **Deals at stake:** Which customers or contracts depend on security, SSO or documentation?
- **Incident costs:** Time spent on emergency fixes, customer communication and lost trust after a data issue
- **Legal exposure:** Notification obligations and possible scrutiny if personal data is exposed
- **Founder time:** Hours each month spent on technical firefighting instead of selling

Public research, such as the cost of data breach reports published by security firms, shows that incidents are expensive even for small organisations, though figures vary widely and are often based on larger companies. For a board conversation, your own estimates are more relevant than global averages.

## Showing What the Spend Enables

Production spend is not only defensive. It often unlocks growth:

- Closing a deal that requires a security review or questionnaire
- Launching a campaign without fear of the app failing under traffic
- Onboarding larger customers with roles, SSO or audit logs
- Passing investor due diligence faster
- Hiring a first developer who can build on a clean, documented foundation
- Recovering revenue lost through payment errors

When the spend enables a specific deal or launch, the business case often becomes obvious.

## A One-Page Proposal Template

1. **Situation:** what the app does, how many customers, what is planned next
2. **Findings:** three to five specific risks found in a short review, in plain language
3. **Impact:** what each risk could cost or block
4. **Proposal:** the scope of work, fixed price and timeline, for example Launch Ready at a quoted amount
5. **What we get:** fixes, documentation, monitoring, tests and support
6. **What it enables:** deals, launches or growth that depend on it
7. **Measures of success:** how we will know it worked
8. **Alternatives considered:** doing nothing, doing it ourselves, a rebuild, other suppliers
9. **Decision requested:** approval of the amount and timing

Attach the fixed-price quote. Decision-makers appreciate a short document with a clear request.

## Answering Common Objections

**"Can we not do it ourselves with the AI tool?"** Some fixes, yes. But the most serious issues are often in configuration and business rules that AI tools do not see, and fixes need tests to stay fixed. A structured review identifies what the tool can safely do and what needs expertise.

**"Why not wait until we have more customers?"** Risks grow with customers. Fixing data access after a leak costs the same development time, plus notification, apologies and lost trust. Fixing before growth is cheaper in total.

**"Is this not a sign the app was built badly?"** No. Fast building with AI tools was the right choice to reach the market. Production readiness is the normal next step, just as companies that start in spreadsheets eventually move to proper systems.

**"How do we know the supplier is not overselling?"** Ask for a fixed price with specific scope, assumptions and exclusions, compare with another quote if you like and check whether the supplier recommended doing less where appropriate.

**"What about ongoing costs?"** Launch Ready has no monthly fee. Launch & Grow adds €49 per month for monitoring, updates and small fixes, cancellable at any time. Hosting costs are billed by providers as before.

**"Should we hire a developer instead?"** A developer adds ongoing capacity but costs far more per year and needs supervision. Many companies make the app production ready first, then hire on a clean foundation.

## Phasing the Spend

If the budget is tight, propose phases:

1. **Phase one:** the risks that could harm customers or block deals, such as data access, secrets, payments, ownership and backups
2. **Phase two:** operations and reliability, such as staging, monitoring and email
3. **Phase three:** growth enablers, such as enterprise features, integrations or performance

Each phase has its own fixed price and can be approved separately. That reduces the size of each decision and lets results from phase one build confidence.

## Measuring Results

Agree in advance how you will judge the spend:

- Specific risks closed, confirmed by tests
- Deals signed that required security or documentation
- Reduction in incidents or support tickets about errors
- Payment discrepancies resolved and revenue recovered
- Time saved on technical issues
- Uptime and response to alerts

Report on these after the project and again after a few months. Clear results make the next decision easier.

## What Decision-Makers Usually Want to See

- A clear link between the spend and business goals
- Evidence from your own app, not generic warnings
- A fixed price and timeline, with exclusions
- Ownership of code and accounts staying with the company
- Documentation that reduces dependency on any one person, including you
- A plan for ongoing costs

## For Boards and Investors

Boards and investors often think in terms of risk management and valuation. A secure, documented, well-run product reduces risk in due diligence, supports enterprise sales and makes the company less dependent on the founder's personal knowledge. Framing production readiness as protecting and increasing the value of the company's main asset, its product, usually resonates. The written summary and account inventory from the project are also useful board materials.

## Talking to a Co-Founder

Co-founders are often closer to the day-to-day and may have different priorities, such as sales or product features. Involve them early: share the findings of a short review, explain which risks affect their work, such as deals blocked by security questions, and invite them to the intro call. Shared understanding of the risks usually leads to agreement on priorities.

## When the Answer Is No

Sometimes the decision-makers decide not to spend now. In that case, document the risks they accepted and the date to revisit, apply the free measures you can, such as enabling backups, MFA and dependency updates, and set alerts for the most important risks. Decisions recorded with clear risks are easier to revisit when circumstances change, for example when a large customer asks for a questionnaire.

## Getting Evidence Before the Conversation

The strongest proposals are built on findings from your own app. Before the meeting, gather a few facts: run the free platform advisors, try the two-account test to see whether one customer can find another customer's data, check who owns each account, check whether backups have ever been restored and list any customer questions about security you could not answer. If you can, ask a supplier for a short look at the app and a written list of findings. Decision-makers respond to "we tested it and found this" far more than to "experts say apps like ours often have this".

## Presenting to Non-Technical Decision-Makers

Keep the presentation short. Start with the business context and the decision you need, then the three most important findings in one sentence each, then the impact, the proposal and the price. Avoid technical terms; if you must use one, explain it in a few words. Bring the full quote and findings as an appendix for those who want detail. End with the measures of success and the date you will report back. A ten-minute presentation with a clear request is usually more effective than an hour of explanation.

## After Approval

Once the spend is approved, keep decision-makers informed with a brief update at the start, after the findings call and after release. Share the written summary at the end, highlighting which risks were closed and what remains. That transparency builds credibility for future technical decisions.

## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: A Board That Said Yes in One Meeting

Marloes van Dijk is the product-minded co-founder of a home care coordination SaaS in Apeldoorn, built with Lovable and Supabase, used by 15 small home care organisations. Her co-founder, who handled finances, and their two-person advisory board questioned a proposed spend of €4,800 on production work. "The customers are happy. Why now?"

Marloes asked LaunchStudio for a short review to bring evidence. It showed that care workers of one organisation could read client notes of another through a broad policy, the database was hosted outside the EU and the Supabase project was in Marloes's personal account. A regional care group with 300 clients, the company's largest prospect, had sent a security questionnaire that could not be answered honestly in its current state.

Marloes wrote a one-page proposal: the three findings, the potential impact, the deal with the care group worth about three times the project cost per year, the fixed Launch & Grow quote of €4,800 plus €49 per month, what they would receive and how success would be measured. She proposed two phases, with the data separation and EU hosting first.

**Result:** The board approved both phases in one meeting. The care group signed six weeks later after receiving the questionnaire answers, and at the next board meeting Marloes reported the closed risks, the signed deal and zero incidents since release.

### How do I convince my co-founder to spend on production readiness?

Use evidence from your own app, framed in business terms: specific risks, what they could cost and which deals or launches depend on fixing them. A fixed-price quote and a one-page proposal make the decision concrete.
