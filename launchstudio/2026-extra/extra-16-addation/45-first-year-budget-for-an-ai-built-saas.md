**Short answer:** A lean AI-built SaaS can run its first year on roughly €3,000 to €6,000 in product and operations costs, covering AI tool subscriptions, hosting, email, monitoring and a one-time production readiness project. A SaaS with paying business customers more typically spends €6,000 to €15,000, including ongoing care, legal and accounting help and modest marketing. The largest variable is usually marketing and sales, not software. Provider prices change, so check current pricing pages and treat the figures below as a planning framework rather than a quote.

AI tools have made building software dramatically cheaper. They have not made running a business free. Founders who budget only for their Lovable or Cursor subscription are often surprised by the other costs that appear once real customers arrive: hosting plans that need upgrading, email providers, payment fees, an accountant, a privacy policy, a production review and something to fall back on when the app breaks. This page lays out a realistic first-year budget so those costs are planned rather than discovered.

## Key Takeaways

- **Software is the smaller part** of a first-year budget; marketing and your own time are larger.
- **Hosting is cheap at the start** but grows with usage; plan for at least one plan upgrade.
- **Production readiness is a one-time cost** that prevents expensive incidents.
- **Payment fees scale with revenue,** so model them as a percentage.
- **Keep a contingency** of around 15 to 20 percent for the unexpected.

## Costs That Grow With Success

Some costs rise as the product succeeds, which is a good problem but still a budget line. Hosting and database costs grow with users, data and traffic. Payment fees grow with revenue. AI provider costs grow with usage of AI features, sometimes faster than revenue if there are no limits. Email costs grow with users and notifications. Support time grows with customers. The budget should include a simple model of how these scale, for example per 100 customers, so you can see whether pricing covers them.

## Costs Founders Often Forget

- **Plan upgrades** for features such as daily backups, more storage or custom domains
- **A separate staging database,** which may need its own plan
- **Developer account fees** for Apple and Google if you publish mobile apps
- **VAT handling** and accountant time for EU customers
- **Security questionnaires** from business customers, which take time or help
- **Insurance,** such as professional liability, which some customers require
- **Time spent on support,** often the largest hidden cost for solo founders

## Why Budget for Production Readiness

A production readiness project is a one-time cost that often prevents much larger ones: a data leak, a broken payment flow that loses revenue for weeks, a weekend outage during a campaign or an enterprise deal stalled by an unanswered questionnaire. For most AI-built SaaS products, Launch Ready at €800 to €3,500 is a small share of the first-year budget, and it makes the remaining spend more effective, because marketing brings customers to an app that works.

## One-Time Versus Recurring Costs

Separating one-time and recurring costs makes the budget easier to manage. One-time costs include the production readiness project, legal templates, brand and initial design, and setting up accounting. Recurring costs include tools, hosting, email, monitoring, ongoing care, accounting and marketing. Recurring costs determine your monthly burn; one-time costs determine how much cash you need before launch. Founders who know both can decide how much runway they have and when revenue must cover running costs.

## Keeping Costs Predictable

- **Choose fixed-price work** for one-time projects
- **Use free tiers wisely,** but plan the upgrade before you hit limits
- **Set usage limits** on AI features and alerts on provider bills
- **Review subscriptions quarterly** and cancel unused tools
- **Model payment and AI costs** per customer and check pricing covers them
- **Keep a contingency** and do not spend it on new features

## A Budget Template

Use this structure for your own planning:

1. **Tools:** list every subscription and its monthly cost
2. **Infrastructure:** hosting, database, storage, email, monitoring, with expected upgrades
3. **One-time projects:** production readiness, legal setup, brand
4. **Ongoing services:** care plan, accountant, legal retainer if any
5. **Variable costs:** payment fees, AI usage, email volume, as a function of customers
6. **Growth:** marketing, sales, events
7. **Contingency:** 15 to 20 percent

Review it monthly against actual spend. The first three months usually show where your estimates were off.

## A Month-by-Month View

Costs are not spread evenly over the year. In the months before launch, spending is dominated by AI tool subscriptions, the production readiness project, legal templates and setting up accounting. Around launch, marketing spend usually peaks with a campaign, an event or outreach to a first group of customers. In months three to six, hosting and email often move from free tiers to paid plans as usage grows, and ongoing care may start. In the second half of the year, variable costs such as payment fees and AI usage become more visible, along with customer requests that lead to small projects, such as an integration or an export.

Plotting the budget by month helps with cash flow, which matters more than the annual total for most bootstrapped founders. It shows when the largest payments fall and how much runway remains if revenue grows more slowly than hoped.

## Pricing Your Product Against Your Costs

A budget is also a pricing tool. Once you know your fixed monthly costs and your variable cost per customer, you can check whether your prices make sense. For example, if each customer costs a few euros per month in hosting, email, payment fees and AI usage, and your fixed costs are several hundred euros per month, you can calculate how many customers you need to break even. Founders who do this early often adjust their pricing, introduce usage limits on expensive features or choose yearly plans with a discount to improve cash flow.

## Your Own Time Is a Cost Too

Solo founders rarely put their own hours in the budget, yet time spent fixing emails, answering technical support questions or reading provider announcements is time not spent selling. Estimating those hours, even roughly, often changes decisions about ongoing care or outsourcing routine work.

## When to Revisit the Budget

Revisit the budget whenever something significant changes: a first enterprise customer, a decision to add AI features, a funding round, a new market or a large marketing push. Each of these changes both one-time and recurring costs. A short review, with updated provider prices and a new estimate for production work if needed, keeps the plan realistic.

## Funding the First Year

Many AI-built SaaS products are funded by the founder's savings or an existing business. Some founders use the Dutch innovation credit schemes, regional funds or small angel rounds. Investors increasingly ask how an AI-built product is secured and maintained; a production readiness summary and a predictable budget help answer that. Where tax or subsidy questions arise, such as the Dutch WBSO scheme for research and development, ask an adviser who knows the current rules.

## Real example

### An AI-Native Founder in Action: A Budget That Survived Its First Year

Lieke Postma runs a scheduling SaaS for physiotherapy practices from Leeuwarden, built with Lovable and Supabase. Before launching, she made a first-year budget. Her first version had three lines: Lovable, Supabase and a domain, totalling about €600. A conversation with her accountant and a LaunchStudio intro call turned it into a fuller plan.

The final budget included AI tools, hosting with a planned upgrade to a paid Supabase plan for backups, email and monitoring, a Launch Ready project of €2,300, ongoing care at €49 per month from month four, legal templates reviewed by a lawyer, accounting, a modest marketing budget for two regional physiotherapy events and a 20 percent contingency. The total came to around €11,500, plus Mollie fees modelled as a share of revenue.

During the year, the contingency covered two unexpected items: an Apple developer account when a practice asked for a mobile app link, and extra AI usage after she added appointment summaries, which led her to set limits per practice. Hosting was upgraded in month five, as planned.

**Result:** Lieke ended the year with 34 paying practices and spending within about five percent of her budget. Because the technical costs were fixed or capped, she could put the unused marketing reserve into a third event, which brought in eight more practices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to run an AI-built SaaS in the first year?

A lean product can run on roughly €3,000 to €6,000 in product and operations costs. A SaaS with paying business customers typically spends €6,000 to €15,000 including production work, ongoing care, legal, accounting and modest marketing. Payment fees and AI usage come on top and scale with revenue and usage.

### What is the biggest cost in the first year?

Usually marketing and sales, plus your own time. Software and hosting are often a smaller share. Among technical costs, a one-time production readiness project is typically the largest single item, followed by hosting upgrades and AI usage.

### How can I keep my first-year costs predictable?

Use fixed-price projects, plan hosting upgrades before you hit limits, set usage limits and alerts on AI and provider costs, review subscriptions quarterly and keep a contingency of 15 to 20 percent.

### How does Manifera's experience help with budgeting?

### Should I budget for SEO and AI visibility?

Yes. Content, technical SEO and structured information about your product help search engines and AI answer engines find and describe you. A production-ready app provides the technical base: fast pages, a custom domain, HTTPS and stable hosting. Content and promotion are separate budget lines.
