**Short answer:** hire your first developer when the product has real users and the work has shifted from building screens to maintaining, securing and extending a system: recurring bugs, integrations, customer requirements and a growing backlog that AI tools alone are not handling well. Choose between a freelancer, a specialised partner, a fractional CTO or an employee based on how much ongoing work there is and how much technical leadership you need. Evaluate candidates with a paid trial task on your real codebase and an independent technical opinion, and make the codebase production-ready first, so your new developer builds rather than firefights.

## Key Takeaways

- **Hire when the bottleneck is engineering, not ideas:** recurring bugs, security requirements, integrations and maintenance load.
- **You have options:** freelancer, agency or specialised partner, fractional CTO, or employee. Many founders combine them.
- **Look for a product-minded generalist** who is comfortable with your stack and with AI-assisted development, and who takes security seriously.
- **Evaluate with real work:** a paid trial task in your codebase, a review of their code review and references, ideally with an independent adviser.
- **Prepare the codebase:** production-ready foundations, documentation and access make the first months productive.

## When It Is Time

Signals that you need a developer:

- **The same bugs keep returning** after AI-assisted fixes.
- **Customers ask for things AI tools struggle with:** SSO, complex integrations, data migrations, performance at scale.
- **You spend more time debugging than talking to customers.**
- **Security or compliance questions** from customers or investors need someone accountable.
- **The backlog grows faster than you can work through it.**
- **You are afraid to deploy changes** because something might break.

If only one or two apply occasionally, a project-based engagement may be enough. If several apply every week, you need ongoing engineering capacity.

## The Options

| Option | Best for | Strengths | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Freelancer | Defined projects, overflow work | Flexible, specialised skills | Availability, continuity, knowledge leaving with them |
| Agency or specialised partner | Defined scopes, production launches, ongoing support | Team capacity, reviews, continuity | Make sure they keep what works rather than rebuild |
| Fractional CTO | Technical leadership, architecture, hiring, investor conversations | Senior judgement without full-time cost | Limited hands-on capacity |
| Employee | Continuous product development | Commitment, accumulated knowledge, culture | Hiring time, cost, management responsibility |

A common path: a specialised partner makes the product production-ready and supports it, a fractional CTO helps define the technical roadmap and hiring profile, and the first employee joins when there is a steady stream of work. ## The Profile to Look For

Your first developer is not a specialist in one narrow area. Look for:

- **Full-stack ability** in your stack, for example TypeScript, React or Next.js, Postgres and Supabase
- **Product sense:** understands users and business goals, not only technical tasks
- **Comfort with AI-assisted development:** uses AI tools productively and reviews their output critically
- **Security awareness:** understands access control, secrets, authentication and data protection
- **Pragmatism:** improves the existing codebase rather than insisting on a rewrite
- **Communication:** explains trade-offs clearly to a non-technical founder
- **Ownership:** comfortable being the only developer for a while

Be wary of candidates whose first suggestion, before understanding the product, is to rebuild everything in a different framework. Sometimes a rewrite is right, but it should follow analysis, not precede it.

## Evaluating Candidates Without Technical Knowledge

This is the hardest part for non-technical founders. Practical approaches:

**1. A paid trial task in your real codebase.** Give a small, real piece of work, such as fixing a known bug or adding a small feature with tests, paid at a fair rate, with read access to a copy of the repository or a branch. Assess the result, the communication and how they explain what they did.

**2. Ask them to review your code.** Ask candidates to spend a few paid hours reviewing the repository and writing a short summary of strengths, risks and what they would do first. Compare summaries across candidates. Good developers identify the same serious issues; weaker ones focus on style or propose rewrites.

**3. Use an independent adviser.** A fractional CTO, an experienced developer you trust or a technical partner can join interviews and assess trial work.

**4. Check references and previous work.** Ask former clients or employers about reliability, communication and quality.

**5. Watch how they handle uncertainty.** Ask how they would approach something they have not done before. Honest answers about learning are better than confident overstatement.

## Interview Questions That Reveal Judgement

- "Here is our app. How would you check whether one customer can see another customer's data?"
- "What would you do in your first two weeks?"
- "When would you recommend rebuilding instead of improving existing code?"
- "How do you use AI coding tools, and how do you check their output?"
- "Tell me about a production incident you handled. What happened, and what did you change afterwards?"
- "How would you explain a technical risk to me so I can make a business decision?"

## Where to Find Candidates

Good first developers are often found through networks rather than job boards: former colleagues, founders who recently went through the same step, local developer meetups, university alumni networks and communities around your stack, such as Supabase or Next.js groups. Recommendations from other founders tend to produce candidates who already understand startup realities. Job platforms and recruiters work too, but expect more screening. Be clear in your posting about the stack, the AI tools you use, the product stage and the fact that the developer will be first and therefore shape the technical culture.

## What to Offer

Startup developers weigh more than salary: interesting problems, ownership, flexibility, the founder's clarity and, often, equity or profit sharing. Be honest about the state of the codebase and the work ahead. Developers who join knowing there is clean-up to do are far more likely to stay than those who discover it after starting. A clear plan for the first months, including time for foundations, makes the role attractive to experienced candidates.

## Working With Your Developer as a Non-Technical Founder

Your role changes once a developer joins. You set priorities and explain customer needs; they decide how to implement them. Agree on a planning rhythm, such as a weekly session to review progress and choose the next priorities, and ask for demos of working software rather than status reports. When technical trade-offs come up, ask for options with consequences in business terms: cost, time, risk and customer impact. Resist micromanaging implementation details, but do insist on the basics: tests for critical areas, reviewed deployments and documentation that keeps the company independent of any single person.

## Employment Considerations in the Netherlands

Hiring an employee in the Netherlands involves employment contracts, payroll taxes, social security contributions, holiday allowance and obligations such as continued pay during illness. Many startups therefore start with freelancers or partners. Working with self-employed developers also has rules: if a freelancer works in a way that resembles employment, it can create risks under Dutch rules on the qualification of working relationships. Get advice from an accountant or employment lawyer on contracts and arrangements, and make sure intellectual property created by freelancers and employees is assigned to the company in writing.

## Onboarding Into an AI-Built Codebase

A new developer's first weeks determine their productivity for months. Prepare:

- **Access:** repository, hosting, database, payment and monitoring dashboards, with roles appropriate to their work, granted through invitations rather than shared passwords
- **Documentation:** README with setup, testing and deployment steps, architecture overview, list of integrations, known issues and the product brief
- **Environments:** a staging environment and local setup that do not touch production data
- **Context:** customer conversations, product goals, roadmap and the reasons behind past decisions
- **AI instructions:** the project instruction files used with your AI tools, which the developer can refine

## Fix the Foundations Before the First Hire

If your new developer's first months are spent fixing security holes, missing backups, broken deployments and payment bugs, you pay a full salary for emergency work, and the developer may become frustrated. Making the product production-ready before hiring, through a focused, fixed-price engagement, lets the first hire start on features and customer needs, with a codebase they can trust.

## The First 90 Days

| Period | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Weeks 1-2 | Setup, understanding the codebase and product, small fixes |
| Weeks 3-6 | First features, tests for critical areas, improvements to development workflow |
| Weeks 7-12 | Ownership of a product area, technical roadmap input, process for reviews and releases |

Agree on how you will work together: planning rhythm, how priorities are set, how progress is shown and how decisions are documented.

## Keeping Knowledge in the Company

With one developer, knowledge concentrates in one person. Protect the company by keeping documentation current, recording architectural decisions briefly in the repository, ensuring all accounts belong to the company and making sure at least one other party, such as a partner or fractional CTO, understands the system well enough to step in.

## Common Mistakes

**Hiring too early,** before there is enough steady engineering work.

**Hiring too late,** when the codebase has become so fragile that every change is risky.

**Choosing by confidence rather than evidence.**

**No trial work.**

**Accounts and code in the founder's personal name,** complicating access and ownership.

**Expecting one developer to do everything immediately,** including architecture, operations, security and features.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should a founder who built with AI tools hire a developer?

When engineering becomes the bottleneck: recurring bugs, customer requirements such as SSO and integrations, security questions, a growing backlog and fear of deploying changes. If these occur weekly, you need ongoing engineering capacity.

### Should my first developer be a freelancer or an employee?

It depends on the volume and continuity of work. Freelancers and partners suit defined projects and variable workloads; employees suit continuous product development. Many founders start with partners or freelancers and hire an employee when there is a steady stream of work.

### How can I evaluate a developer if I am not technical?

Use paid trial tasks on your real codebase, ask candidates to review your code and summarise risks, check references and involve an independent adviser such as a fractional CTO or trusted developer in the assessment.

### Does having a developer improve my product's visibility?
