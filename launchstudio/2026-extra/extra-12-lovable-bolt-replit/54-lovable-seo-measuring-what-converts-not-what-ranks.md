A founder can tell you their traffic is up 40 percent and not tell you whether it produced a single customer. That is the normal state of measurement in a small product, and it is why content decisions get made on feeling.

The useful question is narrow: which pages bring people who become customers? Everything else — sessions, bounce rate, average time, keyword positions — is context at best and distraction at worst.

Answering it does not require an analytics platform. It requires one column in your database.

## Record the Landing Page on Signup

The mechanism is simple enough to build in an hour and it is the foundation of everything else.

When someone first arrives, store two things in their browser: the page they landed on and where they came from. When they eventually sign up, copy those values onto the account record.

Now every customer carries the page that brought them. You can ask which articles produce signups, which produce trials that convert to paid, and which bring traffic that never becomes anything — and you can ask it months later, because the answer is in your own database rather than in a third-party tool's retention window.

Two refinements worth having: keep the first landing page rather than overwriting it, since the article that introduced someone matters more than the pricing page they saw last; and store the referrer so you can distinguish search from a link someone shared.

## The Three Numbers

**Signups per page.** The one that matters. Sorted descending, it tells you which content to write more of and which to stop writing.

**Conversion rate per page.** Signups divided by visitors. A page with 200 visits and 8 signups is more valuable than one with 3,000 visits and 4, and the second is the one a traffic report celebrates.

**Revenue per page**, once you have enough history. Some pages bring customers who stay; others bring trials that never convert. This is the number that occasionally reverses a decision made on signups alone.

Three numbers, one query, reviewed monthly. That is a complete measurement practice for a product at this scale.

## Use the Search Console for What It Alone Knows

Your database tells you what happened after someone arrived. The search console tells you what happened before.

Three things there are worth a monthly look.

**Pages with impressions but few clicks.** People see you and do not choose you. The title and description are the problem, and rewriting them is ten minutes for an immediate effect.

**Queries you rank for but did not target.** Frequently the most useful information available — people arrive searching for something adjacent that you mentioned in passing, and that is an article waiting to be written.

**Pages that are close.** Ranking just below the point where traffic begins. An hour of updating often moves them, which is the cheapest improvement available.

## Most Measurement Produces No Decisions

The test for any metric is whether a change in it would cause you to do something different. If not, stop collecting it.

Bounce rate for a blog article is the clearest example: someone reads your answer and leaves satisfied, which is success recorded as failure. Average session duration means little when one page is meant to be read and another to be scanned. Keyword position fluctuates constantly and is personalised anyway.

What remains after applying that test is a short list: signups per page, conversion rate per page, revenue per page, impressions and click-through in the search console, and the handful of Core Web Vitals figures. Everything else on a typical dashboard is a number nobody acts on.

## Be Honest About Attribution

The landing page is where someone first arrived, not the whole story. A customer may have read four articles over two months, seen a mention in a forum, and signed up after a colleague recommended you.

Two practices keep the measurement honest without building anything elaborate.

Ask. A single optional question at signup — how did you hear about us, as free text — produces better information than most attribution systems, because people tell you about the podcast, the colleague and the forum thread that no tracking can see.

And accept that some of it is unknowable. The correct response to that is not a more complicated measurement system; it is to weigh the data you have against what customers tell you, and to make decisions with both.

## Give It Time Before Judging

The measurement described above is only useful if you allow enough time for the numbers to mean anything, and impatience is the most common way founders draw the wrong conclusion.

A new article takes weeks to be indexed and months to reach whatever position it will reach. Judging it after three weeks tells you nothing except that it is new. Three to six months is the honest window before a page's performance is informative.

Volume matters too. A page with 40 visits and 1 signup and a page with 40 visits and 3 signups are not meaningfully different; at these numbers the difference is noise. Wait for enough observations, or compare groups of pages rather than individual ones — all the articles about a subject against all the articles about another — which reaches a usable signal much sooner.

Two practical consequences. Review quarterly rather than monthly for content decisions, even if you look at the numbers more often out of interest. And resist rewriting a page because it underperformed in its first month, which is the equivalent of pulling up a plant to check the roots.

The one exception worth acting on immediately: a page with impressions and almost no clicks. That is a title and description problem, it is visible within weeks, and fixing it costs ten minutes — which makes it the only fast feedback loop in this discipline.

## Decide What to Do With the Answer

Measurement earns its keep only when it changes what gets written, and that requires a decision rule agreed with yourself in advance rather than a debate each quarter.

A rule that works for small products: at each quarterly review, sort pages by signups. The top group tells you a subject that works — write two more articles on it, going deeper rather than broader. The middle group is fine and needs nothing. The bottom group, after six months with meaningful traffic and no signups, is a subject that attracts the wrong audience; stop writing about it, and consider whether the existing pages should be updated to speak to buyers or simply left alone.

The harder decision is what to do with a page that brings a lot of traffic and no customers. The instinct is to keep it because the number is large. The honest reading is that it is attracting people who are not buyers, and every further article on that theme compounds the mistake — which is exactly the pattern in the example above.

Keeping such a page costs nothing and it is not a candidate for deletion; the mistake is only in writing more like it. Write what the small converting page suggests instead, and check again in three months.

## Watch for the Page That Was Working

One failure mode is worth a specific check because it is invisible in a report of what is doing well: a page that used to produce customers and has quietly stopped.

Causes are mundane. A competitor published something better. The search results for that query changed shape. Your page's information went out of date and readers notice. A site change broke the link between the article and the signup route it used to feed. Or the page was accidentally deindexed by a robots directive somebody added to a template.

None of these announces itself. The page still exists, still looks fine, and produces nothing.

The check is one line in the quarterly review: compare each page's signups against the same quarter last year, and look at anything that has fallen substantially. Usually the answer is content that aged; occasionally it is a technical fault that has been costing you customers for months.

This is also the argument for keeping the measurement in your own database rather than in a tool with a retention limit. Comparing this quarter with the one two years ago is only possible if the data is still there, and it is the comparison that catches slow decline — which is the kind that nobody notices until the total has moved enough to be alarming.

## Real example

### The Popular Page That Sold Nothing

Aukje Verstraeten built Ritregistratie in Lovable: kilometre and trip registration for company car drivers and sole traders, 340 subscribers at €9 a month.

Her most successful article by traffic — a general piece about the tax rules for private use of a company car — brought 4,200 visits a month, roughly 60 percent of her organic traffic. She had written four follow-ups on the same theme because the numbers were good.

Adding landing page capture at signup changed the picture within two months. The popular article had produced 3 signups in that period. A far smaller article about registering trips for multiple vehicles under one sole trader, with 190 visits a month, had produced 22.

The explanation was obvious in hindsight: the tax article attracted employees researching their own tax position, who were not buyers. The small article attracted people with the specific administrative problem her product solves.

One business day, then two months of measurement: first landing page and referrer captured on arrival and written to the account at signup; a monthly query producing signups, conversion rate and revenue per landing page; the search console reviewed for impressions without clicks, which found six pages with poor titles; an optional free-text question at signup; and metrics that produced no decisions removed from her dashboard.

**Result:** she stopped writing tax explainers and wrote six more articles about specific registration situations — multiple vehicles, mixed private and business use, switching cars mid-year, drivers with two employers. Organic traffic fell by 22 percent over the following six months. Organic signups rose by 210 percent. The free-text question also revealed that 14 percent of signups came from one accountancy forum she had never heard of.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an analytics platform to measure content?

No. Capturing the first landing page and referrer on arrival, and copying them to the account at signup, answers the question that matters and lives in your own database.

### Which metrics actually matter?

Signups per page, conversion rate per page, and revenue per page — plus impressions and click-through in the search console. Anything that would not change a decision is not worth collecting.

### Why is bounce rate misleading for articles?

Because a reader who finds their answer and leaves is satisfied. For a blog page, a high bounce rate frequently indicates success.

### Should I track first touch or last touch?

Keep the first landing page rather than overwriting it. The article that introduced someone tells you more about what to write than the pricing page they saw immediately before signing up.

### How do I account for what tracking cannot see?

Ask an optional free-text question at signup. People mention the colleague, the forum and the podcast that no measurement system can attribute, and it is frequently the most useful data you collect.
