"Experienced with Lovable and Supabase" appears on a great many profiles, and a founder who cannot read code has no way to test it. The usual substitutes — a portfolio, a confident conversation, a reasonable price — are weak signals, and the cost of being wrong is a month and several thousand euros.

There are better checks, and they are available to anyone regardless of technical knowledge. They take about two hours in total.

## Ask Questions With Revealing Answers

Five questions, chosen because a competent answer is recognisable even if you could not have produced it.

**"What are the first three things you would check in an AI-built product?"** A good answer names specifics: where the API keys are, whether endpoints check who is asking, whether storage buckets are public. A vague answer about code quality and best practices is a poor sign.

**"A page is slow for our largest customer and fast for everyone else. What is your first guess?"** The expected answer is a missing index or a query fetching everything, as several articles in this series describe. Someone who proposes an architecture change has not seen this before.

**"How do you handle a customer's data while working on the product?"** Good answers involve anonymised copies and seeded data. An answer that treats production data casually is disqualifying for anyone touching a product with customers.

**"What would you not do?"** A useful question because it asks for judgement. Good answers mention rebuilding what works, or adding infrastructure the product does not need.

**"How would I be able to maintain this after you finish?"** The answer reveals whether they are planning a handover or a dependency.

## Check the Reference That Matters

One reference call, with the right question, is worth more than a portfolio.

Ask to speak to someone whose product they worked on and who is still running it. Then ask three things.

**"What did they leave you with?"** Documentation, tests, a repository you understand, or a working product and nothing else.

**"What happened when something went wrong during the work?"** Every engagement has a moment. How it was handled is the most informative thing you can learn.

**"Would you use them again, and for what?"** The qualification in the answer carries the information.

A supplier who cannot produce a reference for a completed engagement is telling you something. So is one whose references are all people who hired them and never launched.

## The Small Paid Trial

The single most reliable check, and the one most founders skip because it feels like an extra step.

Give them a small, real, bounded piece of work — a day or two, paid at their normal rate. Not a test, not free work: a genuine task you need done.

Choose something with a clear outcome and some judgement in it: fix a specific bug, add a defined feature, or produce an assessment of the product with findings and estimates.

Then judge what comes back on four things. Did they do what was asked, or what they preferred? Did they ask good questions or none? Can they explain what they did in terms you understand? And what did they leave — a change you can read, or a change you have to trust?

Two days tells you more than any interview, and if it goes badly you have spent a day's rate rather than a month's.

## What Does Not Tell You Much

Three signals founders over-weight.

**A portfolio of screenshots.** They show that something was built and nothing about how, by whom, or whether it still works.

**Years of experience.** Someone with fifteen years who has never worked on an AI-built codebase may be less useful here than someone with four who has done it repeatedly — the problems are specific.

**Confidence.** The most confident supplier in a set of three is not more likely to be the best one, and in our experience is slightly less likely.

**Price, in either direction.** The cheapest quote is frequently the most expensive, as the pricing article in this series describes. An expensive quote is not evidence of quality either.

## Where to Look

Vetting assumes you have candidates, and for a founder without a technical network that is the harder half.

**Referrals from other founders in your situation.** The strongest source by a distance, because the person recommending has been through the same work and knows what it involved. Ask specifically for someone who took a product from prototype to launch, not for "a good developer".

**Sector communities.** Dutch founder groups, industry associations and local business networks. A supplier who has worked with three companies in your sector understands your customers' expectations, which is worth as much as technical fit.

**Specialist studios.** Firms that explicitly do this work, which is a small and identifiable category. Their websites describe the same problems this series describes, which is itself a useful filter.

**Freelance platforms**, with considerably more vetting required. Volume is high, quality varies enormously, and the checks in this article matter most here.

**Your existing network, sideways.** The accountant, the lawyer, the designer you already work with have other clients. Asking them is free and the recommendations come with a relationship attached.

What works poorly: cold outreach from suppliers who found you, general job boards for a bounded engagement, and anyone whose pitch begins by telling you that your product should be rebuilt.

## The Warning Signs

Six things that, in our experience of watching these engagements, predict a poor outcome reliably enough to act on.

**A quote before reading the code.** An estimate for work nobody has looked at is a number attached to an assumption.

**A rebuild proposed immediately.** Occasionally correct, usually a preference for starting fresh rather than reading someone else's structure — and it discards the thing your prototype established.

**Dismissiveness about the tools.** A supplier who opens by explaining that AI-generated code is worthless has told you they will not work with what you have.

**No questions about your customers.** Someone who asks only technical questions is building software rather than solving your problem, and the data volume and tenancy questions are the ones that predict competence.

**Unwillingness to do a small paid trial.** For a bounded engagement this is a reasonable request and a supplier confident in their work agrees readily.

**Vagueness about what happens afterwards.** No handover, no documentation, no answer about how you maintain it. This is the signal that most reliably predicts a dependency rather than a delivery.

Any one of these is worth a question. Two together is worth choosing somebody else, and the cost of doing so at the vetting stage is an afternoon rather than a month.

## Assess the Fit, Not Only the Skill

A supplier can be entirely competent and wrong for your situation, and the mismatch causes as many failed engagements as incompetence does.

Four dimensions worth thinking about explicitly.

**Size.** A studio geared to €50,000 projects will find your €5,000 engagement administratively awkward, and you will be their least important client. A solo freelancer running three projects has no capacity for an urgent problem. Match the scale.

**Availability.** Someone excellent who can start in eleven weeks may be worse for you than someone good who can start on Monday, depending on what is at stake. Ask, and believe the answer.

**Continuity.** Will the person you spoke to do the work? In larger firms frequently not, and the difference between the senior person in the meeting and whoever is assigned is worth establishing before signing.

**Interest.** A supplier who finds your product interesting does better work than one taking on filler. It is not a measurable quality and it is visible in the questions they ask.

And one that founders under-weight: whether you can talk to them easily. An engagement involves dozens of small exchanges, and a supplier whose explanations you cannot follow, or who makes you reluctant to ask, will produce a worse outcome than a slightly less skilled one you communicate with comfortably.

## Setting This Up

Before signing anything: five questions whose answers you can evaluate — first checks, the slow page, handling customer data, what they would not do, and how you maintain it afterwards; one reference call with someone still running a product they worked on, asking what was left behind, what happened when something went wrong, and whether they would repeat it; a small paid trial of real bounded work judged on whether they did what was asked, the questions they asked, whether they can explain it, and what they left; and a deliberate discounting of portfolios, years, confidence and price.

## Real example

### A Month Lost to a Confident Answer

Erwin Batelaan built Bezorgplanning in Lovable: delivery route planning for bakeries and regional food suppliers, used by 16 companies.

He needed the product secured and launched to a further 40 companies. Three suppliers responded; he chose the one who was most confident and available immediately, without a trial, a reference or any check beyond a conversation.

The engagement ran for five weeks. The supplier began by rebuilding the frontend in a different framework because he preferred it, which had not been discussed, consumed three weeks, and produced a product that looked the same and worked slightly less well. The security work, which was the actual reason for the engagement, was two days at the end and consisted of moving one API key.

Erwin paid €8,200 and ended with a product he understood less well than before, still holding the authorisation gaps he had been worried about.

Two business days with a different supplier, then the work: a paid assessment of a day, producing a written report with 19 findings and estimates, which established that 23 of 27 endpoints had no authorisation check and that storage was public; five questions asked of both remaining candidates, with the answers differing usefully; one reference call with a founder still running a product they had built, who described what had been left behind; and a fixed-price engagement quoted from the report.

**Result:** the second engagement cost €5,900 and took nine business days, and Erwin can maintain the result because a handover was part of it. His assessment is that the day of assessment and the two hours of checking would have saved him five weeks and €8,200.

> *"I picked the one who sounded most certain and was free that week. He rebuilt my frontend in a framework he preferred, and the security work I was paying for was two days at the end."*
> — **Erwin Batelaan, Founder, Bezorgplanning (Gorinchem)**

**Cost & Timeline:** €6,900 total (€1,000 paid assessment producing a 19-finding report, €5,900 fixed-price remediation covering authorisation across 27 endpoints, storage privatisation, payments and launch with handover) — completed in 9 business days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I assess technical skill without being technical?

By the specificity of their answers, by one reference call with someone still running a product they worked on, and by a small paid trial of real work. All three are available to anyone.

### What question is most revealing?

"What are the first three things you would check in an AI-built product?" A competent answer names specifics — keys in the frontend, missing authorisation, public storage. A vague answer about best practices is a poor sign.

### Should I ask for free work as a test?

No. Pay for a small real task at their normal rate. It respects their time, produces something you need, and tells you more than an unpaid exercise would.

### What should I ask a reference?

What the supplier left behind, what happened when something went wrong during the work, and whether they would use them again and for what. The qualification in the last answer carries the information.

### What signals should I discount?

Portfolios of screenshots, years of general experience, confidence, and price in either direction. The problems in AI-built products are specific, and general seniority is a weak predictor.
