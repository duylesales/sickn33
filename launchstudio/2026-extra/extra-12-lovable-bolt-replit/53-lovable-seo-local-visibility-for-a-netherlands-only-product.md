A product sold only in the Netherlands has an advantage that global SaaS does not: the competition for Dutch terms is a fraction of the competition for English ones, and Dutch buyers actively prefer a Dutch supplier for anything involving their data, their invoices or their obligations.

Realising that advantage requires the site to make the Dutch connection obvious, which AI-built sites frequently fail to do — generated in English, hosted on a `.com`, with no address anywhere, they read as a product from nowhere in particular.

## Make the Dutch Signal Unmistakable

Several small things, each trivial, which together decide whether you read as a Dutch company.

A `.nl` domain if you sell only here. It is a stronger signal than anything else on this list and it costs almost nothing.

The full company details in the footer and on a contact page, in text rather than in an image: legal name, street address, city, KvK number, VAT number, and a Dutch phone number if you have one.

The site's language declared correctly in the HTML, Dutch content written natively rather than translated, and prices in euros including or excluding VAT as your market expects — which for B2B is excluding, stated.

Organisation structured data naming the company, the address and the country, which is the machine-readable version of the same information.

None of this is clever and all of it is missing from a large share of AI-built Dutch products.

## Do You Need a Business Profile?

A Google Business Profile is designed for businesses customers visit. Whether it helps a software product depends on one question: do you serve customers at a place?

**If you have an office people come to**, or you deliver on-site anywhere in the Netherlands, yes. Create it, verify it, keep hours and contact details current, and gather reviews from customers.

**If you are entirely remote with no customer-facing location**, the value is smaller. A profile without a verifiable address risks suspension, and a listing nobody visits does little.

The middle case, which many founders are in: a registered business address you work from. A profile is reasonable, without hours, describing what you do rather than pretending to be a shop.

Where it does apply, reviews are the part that matters. A Dutch B2B buyer comparing two suppliers reads them, and four genuine reviews beat none decisively.

## Regional Pages, Only Where They Are Real

The template trap is covered elsewhere in this series and applies here in its most common form: a page for each of forty Dutch cities, identical but for the name.

Regional pages work when something about the region is genuinely different. If you serve customers on-site and your coverage varies, a page per region with what you actually do there, which customers you have, and travel or response times is real content. If your product is identical everywhere and delivered over the internet, city pages have nothing to say.

The alternative that works for any Dutch product: pages about the things that are specifically Dutch. How your product handles BTW and the quarterly return. Integration with Dutch accounting packages. iDEAL and SEPA direct debit. Compliance with Dutch sector rules. AVG handling with data in the EU.

These bring exactly the buyer you want — someone searching for a product that works here — and they are things an international competitor cannot easily write.

## The Dutch Directories

Being listed where Dutch buyers look is worth more than most link-building, and the useful ones are sector-specific rather than general.

Industry associations and trade bodies for the sector you serve, which frequently have supplier listings and are read by exactly your buyer. Dutch software comparison and review sites. The KvK register, which is not optional and is consulted more than founders expect. Any regional business network you genuinely belong to. And sector publications that list suppliers.

Skip general directories that accept anyone. They add nothing and consume an afternoon.

## Reviews and Mentions Where Dutch Buyers Read Them

For a Dutch B2B product, the evidence buyers look for is other Dutch businesses.

Named case studies with the customer's real company and city, a testimonial with a name and a role, a logo wall of businesses people recognise regionally. This is worth more here than an impressive feature list, because the buyer's question is whether people like them use it.

Ask for reviews on the platforms your sector actually reads — which varies, and is worth finding out from your own customers rather than assuming.

## Write the Way Your Buyers Talk

The subtlest advantage of selling only in the Netherlands is that you can write the way your customers actually speak about their work, and an international competitor translating from English cannot.

Dutch professional vocabulary is specific and not always what a translation produces. Installers say *werkbon*, not "work order form". Bookkeepers say *btw-aangifte*, not "VAT declaration". Care organisations say *zorgplan*. Schools say *leerlingdossier*. These are the words people type into a search box, and they are frequently absent from a site written in English and translated afterwards.

The way to find them is not a keyword tool, which is thin for Dutch long-tail terms. It is your own materials: read the emails your customers send you, the way they describe their own problems on the phone, and the language used in their sector's trade publications and association websites. The terms that recur are the ones to write around.

Two further benefits. Writing in the sector's own vocabulary signals that you understand the work, which is the judgement a professional buyer makes in the first ten seconds. And it produces content that reads as written by someone in the industry rather than about it — which, as the previous article in this series argues, is precisely the thing a model cannot produce on your behalf and the reason your own knowledge is the asset.

## The Competition Is Thinner Than You Think

Founders comparing themselves to global SaaS underestimate how different the Dutch search landscape is, and it changes what is worth attempting.

For a specific Dutch professional term — the software a particular trade uses for a particular task — the competing pages are frequently few, and several of them are poor: a supplier's thin product page, an outdated blog post, a directory listing. A genuinely useful page written by someone who understands the work can reach the first page in months rather than years.

The same term in English is contested by dozens of well-resourced companies publishing continuously.

Two consequences. First, aim narrower than instinct suggests. "Planningssoftware" is contested; "planningssoftware voor fysiotherapiepraktijken" is not, and the person searching it is far closer to buying. The narrower term brings fewer visitors and a much higher proportion of the right ones.

Second, the total available traffic for any Dutch term is modest, and that is fine. A page bringing forty visits a month, of which three become trials, is a better outcome for a product at €50 a month than a page bringing four thousand visitors who are mostly in other countries and cannot buy.

Judge pages by what they produce rather than by volume, which for a Netherlands-only product means measuring signups per page and being entirely unbothered by small numbers.

## Where Dutch Buyers Actually Look

Search is one channel and, for several Dutch sectors, not the first one. Knowing where your buyers actually look changes how much of this work is worth doing.

Professional associations and their member communications carry considerable weight in Dutch trades and professions — being mentioned in a sector association's newsletter reaches more of your market than a month of search visibility.

Sector-specific online communities, which in the Netherlands are often small, long-established and highly trusted. A recommendation in one is worth a great deal and cannot be bought.

Accountants and advisers, who recommend software to their clients and are a sales channel most software founders ignore entirely. For administrative products this is frequently the single largest source of customers.

And regional business networks, which matter more here than in larger markets because the country is small enough that people know each other.

None of this replaces search — the person who searches at the moment they have the problem is the most valuable visitor you get — but it does argue against treating search as the whole of the plan. The practical split for most Dutch B2B products in their first two years: content and search as the compounding long-term channel, and deliberate presence in two or three sector-specific places as the one that produces customers this quarter.

## Real example

### A Dutch Product That Looked American

Wessel Fokkinga built Bouwadmin in Lovable: administration for small construction and renovation firms — quotes, invoices, hours and material costs — used by 58 firms, all Dutch.

The site was in English on a `.com`, with no address anywhere, prices in euros with no indication of VAT treatment, and a home page that could have described a product from anywhere. He ranked for nothing, and two prospects had asked during sales calls whether the company was Dutch and where the data was stored.

Three business days: a `.nl` domain acquired and made primary with permanent redirects from the `.com`; the site rewritten in Dutch as the primary language, with the English version reduced to two pages; full company details added in text to the footer and a contact page, including KvK and VAT numbers and a Dutch telephone number; organisation structured data with the Amersfoort address; prices restated as excluding BTW, which is what his B2B buyers expect; four pages written about specifically Dutch aspects — BTW handling and the quarterly return, integration with two Dutch accounting packages, iDEAL and direct debit for customer payments, and where data is stored under the AVG; a Google Business Profile created for his registered office without opening hours; listings pursued with two construction sector associations and one Dutch software comparison site; and six named case studies published with the firms' real names and towns, with permission, replacing three anonymous ones.

**Result:** organic traffic went from 60 to 890 visits a month over five months, with the BTW page alone bringing 310 of those and converting at four times the site average. The two sector association listings produced eleven enquiries in the first quarter. Wessel's note is that the BTW article took two hours and outperforms everything else he has written.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a .nl domain matter?

For a product sold only in the Netherlands, yes. It is the strongest single signal that you are a Dutch supplier, and Dutch buyers notice it.

### Do I need a Google Business Profile for software?

Only if you serve customers at a place or on-site. A profile without a verifiable location risks suspension and does little. A registered office without hours is a reasonable middle position.

### Do city pages work for a software product?

Only if something about the region genuinely differs — coverage, on-site service, response times. Otherwise write about the specifically Dutch aspects of your product instead, which brings better buyers.

### Which Dutch directories are worth the effort?

Sector associations and trade bodies for the industry you serve, Dutch software comparison sites, the KvK register, and sector publications. Skip general directories that accept anyone.

### What convinces Dutch B2B buyers?

Other Dutch businesses. Named case studies with real company names and towns, reviews on the platforms their sector reads, and clear answers about where data is stored.
