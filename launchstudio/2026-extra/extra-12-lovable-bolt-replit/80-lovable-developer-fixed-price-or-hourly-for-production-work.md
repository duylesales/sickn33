Both arrangements work. Both fail. Which one fails depends almost entirely on how well the work can be described before it starts, and that varies enormously between the different things a founder needs done to an AI-built product.

The useful question is not which is better in general. It is which suits the specific piece of work in front of you, and it frequently turns out that one engagement should be structured as both.

## Fixed Price Suits Work That Can Be Specified

Fixed price means the supplier carries the risk of the estimate being wrong. You know the cost; they absorb the overrun.

That works when the work is knowable in advance: a security review and remediation against a named checklist, a payment integration with a defined set of flows, a deployment and launch, an accessibility pass, a migration with a rehearsed plan. These have boundaries, and a competent supplier has done them before.

What you give up is flexibility. A fixed price is a fixed scope, and anything you think of during the work is a change request — which is not a supplier being difficult but the only way the arrangement can function.

The failure mode is a fixed price agreed from a vague description. The supplier has priced their assumption, you have bought yours, and the difference surfaces in week two.

## Hourly Suits Work That Requires Discovery

Hourly means you carry the risk and buy flexibility.

It suits work whose shape is genuinely unknown: diagnosing a problem nobody understands, an open-ended improvement pass on an unfamiliar codebase, ongoing development where priorities change weekly, and anything where you want to redirect as you learn.

The failure mode is no ceiling and no visibility — hours accumulate, progress is unclear, and the conversation at the end is about the invoice rather than the outcome.

Both are fixed by structure: a cap you agree in advance, a short written update at each interval saying what was done and what is next, and a checkpoint where either side can stop.

## The Arrangement That Usually Works

For an AI-built product needing production work, the shape that fits most situations is a small paid assessment followed by a fixed price.

**An assessment**, a day or two, paid, producing a written report: what is wrong, what it takes to fix, what it will cost, in what order. You own the report regardless of what you do next.

**Then a fixed price** for the work, quoted from the report rather than from a conversation. Because the supplier has read the code, the estimate is realistic and the scope is genuine.

This resolves the central difficulty, which is that nobody can accurately price work on a codebase they have not read. The alternatives are a padded fixed price, a hourly arrangement with no ceiling, or a cheap quote that becomes expensive through change requests.

A small assessment fee is the cheapest thing in this article and it is the one founders resist most, because it feels like paying for a quote. It is paying for a plan, and you keep it.

## What Fixed Price Must Include

If you agree a fixed price, four things belong in writing.

**What is included**, itemised. Not "production readiness" but the list: authorisation across all endpoints, credentials moved and rotated, server-side validation, payment integration with failure paths, and so on.

**What is explicitly not included.** The absence of this line is the source of most disputes.

**What happens if something unexpected is found.** It will be. The reasonable arrangement: the supplier tells you immediately with an estimate, and you decide whether to add it, defer it, or accept it as a known issue.

**Payment terms tied to milestones** rather than a single payment at either end. A third at the start, a third at an agreed midpoint, a third on acceptance, is normal and protects both sides.

## What Hourly Must Include

And if hourly: an agreed cap that cannot be exceeded without your approval, a written update at a regular interval, a stated rate with any differences for different work, and a clear definition of what is billable — is a status call billable, is thinking time, is the time spent getting your environment running.

That last question is worth asking explicitly. The answer is reasonable either way; discovering the answer from an invoice is not.

## Dutch Rates, Honestly

Founders benchmarking these engagements need a sense of what the market actually charges, and the ranges in the Netherlands are reasonably stable.

**Freelance developers:** roughly €65 to €110 an hour, or €500 to €850 a day. The upper end buys seniority and specific experience; the lower end is frequently someone earlier in their career or working from a lower-cost location.

**Small studios and agencies:** €85 to €150 an hour, reflecting overheads, continuity and the fact that somebody else manages the work.

**Larger agencies:** €120 to €200 an hour, appropriate for substantial projects and rarely the right fit for taking a prototype to production.

**Offshore arrangements:** considerably lower per hour, with the difference in time zone, communication and supervision costing back some of the saving. It works well for defined work with clear specifications and poorly for discovery.

Two notes on interpreting these. A lower rate is not a lower cost if the work takes three times as long, and rate is the least useful number for comparing suppliers — what matters is the total for the outcome, which is why itemised fixed prices are easier to compare than hourly rates.

And for the work this series describes — securing and launching an AI-built product — the total for a small product is typically €800 to €7,500 whoever does it, because the work is broadly the same list.

## What Happens When Something Goes Wrong

Every engagement of any length has a difficult moment, and how it is handled depends almost entirely on what was agreed beforehand rather than on goodwill.

Four provisions worth having in any arrangement, fixed or hourly.

**A checkpoint where either side can stop.** After the first milestone, or after two weeks. Both parties having an exit makes the work better, because neither is trapped.

**A definition of done.** What has to be true for the work to be accepted. Vague acceptance criteria produce a final week of adjustments that neither side thinks they agreed to.

**Ownership from the start.** The code is yours as it is written, in your repository, not handed over at the end. This is the provision that matters most if the relationship ends badly.

**A warranty period.** Thirty days in which defects in the delivered work are fixed without further charge. Reasonable, standard, and it distinguishes a supplier confident in what they built.

And one thing that is not a provision but decides more than any of them: pay on time. A supplier waiting on an invoice deprioritises you, and for a small studio a late payment is a real problem. The founders who get the best work are frequently the ones who pay within the week.

## Judge the Work, Not the Hours

A founder who cannot read code is uncertain how to evaluate what they are paying for, and the answer is not to learn to read code. It is to judge the things you can observe.

**Does it do what was agreed?** Work through the itemised list yourself. Each item should be demonstrable: show me that a user from another organisation cannot see this record; show me what happens when a payment fails; show me the backup being restored.

**Did the estimate hold?** Not perfectly — estimates are estimates — but roughly, and with early warning when it did not. A supplier who tells you in week one that something is larger than expected is behaving well; one who tells you at the end is not.

**Can you understand the explanation?** A good engineer can explain what they did to a non-technical founder. If every answer is impenetrable, that is sometimes genuine complexity and frequently a signal.

**What did they leave behind?** Documentation, tests, a conventions file, a handover conversation. The difference between a supplier who solved your problem and one who made themselves necessary is entirely in what you can do afterwards without them.

That last measure is the one to weight most heavily, and it is worth stating as a requirement before the work rather than hoping for it afterwards.

## Real example

### A Fixed Price for Unknown Work

Lieselotte Bijkerk built Taxatierapport in Lovable: valuation reporting for independent property appraisers, used by 23 appraisers producing around 180 reports a month.

She needed the product secured, payments added and a launch to a further 60 appraisers. She took the cheapest of three quotes: €3,900 fixed, agreed from an email exchange, with no assessment and no itemisation.

In week two the supplier found that reports were stored as files on the application's filesystem and had been disappearing on redeploys, that no endpoint checked which appraiser was asking, and that the valuation calculation existed in three places with two variants. None of it was in the quoted scope, because nobody had read the code.

The engagement became a sequence of change requests. Final cost €9,400, eleven weeks instead of three, and a relationship that ended badly on both sides — the supplier believing they had been asked to fix an undisclosed mess, Lieselotte believing she had been quoted for a job the supplier had not understood.

Two business days with a different supplier, then the work: a paid assessment of €1,200 producing a nine-page report naming every finding with an estimate and a priority order, which she owns; a fixed price of €6,100 quoted from that report, itemised across eleven items with four explicit exclusions; a stated procedure for anything further found, which was invoked twice and handled in a day each; and milestone payments in thirds.

**Result:** the second engagement completed in eleven business days against an estimate of ten, at €6,100 plus the €1,200 assessment. Lieselotte's assessment is that the cheap quote cost her €9,400, eight extra weeks and a supplier relationship, and that the assessment fee she initially resisted was the cheapest part of the whole exercise.

> *"I refused to pay twelve hundred euros for someone to tell me what was wrong. Then I paid nine thousand four hundred to find out during the work, over eleven weeks, one argument at a time."*
> — **Lieselotte Bijkerk, Founder, Taxatierapport (Hilversum)**

**Cost & Timeline:** €7,300 total (€1,200 assessment producing a written report, €6,100 fixed-price remediation across eleven itemised items including storage migration, authorisation, calculation consolidation, payments and launch) — completed in 11 business days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which is better, fixed price or hourly?

Neither in general. Fixed price suits work that can be specified — security, payments, deployment, migration. Hourly suits discovery and changing priorities. Most engagements should use both, in that order.

### Why should I pay for an assessment?

Because nobody can price work on code they have not read. The alternatives are a padded quote, an uncapped hourly arrangement, or a cheap quote that grows through change requests.

### What must a fixed price include?

An itemised scope, explicit exclusions, a stated procedure for unexpected findings, and milestone payments rather than a single payment at one end.

### What must an hourly arrangement include?

A cap that cannot be exceeded without approval, regular written updates, a stated rate, and a clear definition of what is billable — including whether calls and environment setup are.

### Is the cheapest quote usually the most expensive?

Frequently, when it was produced without reading the code. The price you agree is for the work somebody imagined; the price you pay is for the work that exists.
