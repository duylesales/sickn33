A founder in Utrecht and a developer in Ho Chi Minh City have about two hours of overlapping working day. Handled badly, that means a question asked at four in the afternoon is answered the following morning and the day is lost. Handled well, it means work happens while you sleep and you wake to a result.

The difference is not the distance. It is whether the work was described well enough to proceed without you.

That skill is worth developing regardless, because it is the same skill that makes an AI agent productive — a precise description, explicit boundaries, and a way to verify — and a founder who has learned it for one gets the other free.

## Describe Work So It Can Proceed Without You

A task that requires a clarifying question is a task that costs a day. A task that contains its own answers costs nothing.

Four elements, which will look familiar from the agent article in this series.

**The outcome**, concretely. What should be true when this is finished.

**The context.** Why it matters, who it is for, what the customer actually asked. This is what lets someone make a sensible decision when they hit an ambiguity rather than stopping.

**The boundaries.** What not to change. What must keep working.

**The answers to the obvious questions.** Think through what you would be asked and answer it in advance. Five minutes of writing saves a day of waiting.

The founders who find distant collaboration frustrating are almost always writing one-line tasks and receiving questions.

## Use the Overlap for What Only It Can Do

Two hours of overlap is enough for the things that genuinely need to be live, and wasteful for anything else.

**Use it for:** decisions that require discussion, demonstrating something and reacting together, difficult conversations, and the weekly conversation about priorities.

**Do not use it for:** status updates, anything that could be written, or reviewing work you could have read beforehand.

Protect it. A daily fifteen-minute call at the start of their day and the middle of yours is worth more than an hour scheduled irregularly, because it is the reliable point at which anything blocked gets unblocked.

## Write Things Down

Everything that matters should exist in writing, because the person who needs it is asleep when they need it.

Decisions, in a place both can find. Specifications, as described above. Progress, as a short daily written update saying what was done, what is next and what is blocked. Questions, asked in writing with enough context to be answerable without a conversation.

This is not bureaucracy. It is the substitute for the ambient awareness that colocated people have, and without it the collaboration depends on two people reconstructing context in a two-hour window every day.

One artefact repays the effort more than any other: the conventions file this series keeps recommending. A distant developer with a clear description of how the codebase works produces code that fits; one without it produces code in their own idiom, and you discover the divergence at review.

## Review in Your Morning, Not Theirs

The rhythm that works: they finish a piece of work and submit it before their evening. You review it in your morning, which is their night. Your comments are waiting when they start.

That gives one full cycle per day, reliably, which is enough for steady progress. What breaks it is a review that waits — a change submitted Monday evening and reviewed Wednesday costs two days for nothing.

So make review a morning habit rather than a task that competes with everything else. Twenty minutes, first thing, before the day fills.

## Unblock Aggressively

The cost of being blocked is asymmetric across time zones. A question that would take thirty seconds to answer in an office costs a working day.

Two practices reduce it substantially. Ask the developer to proceed on their best judgement when blocked, documenting the assumption, rather than stopping — a wrong assumption costs an hour to correct, while waiting costs a day. And keep a short list of what is next, so a blocked task is set aside for something else rather than becoming idle time.

And answer questions when they arrive rather than at a convenient moment. A question answered at ten at night is a day recovered.

## The Things That Are Genuinely Harder

It is worth being honest about what distance costs, because the practices above reduce the friction without removing it.

**Exploratory work.** Diagnosing something nobody understands benefits from two people looking at the same screen and thinking aloud. Across a two-hour overlap it is possible and slower, and it is the category where a local person or a longer scheduled session earns its cost.

**Anything requiring shared judgement about the product.** Whether this interface is confusing, whether this message reads correctly in Dutch, whether a customer will accept this compromise. These are conversations rather than tasks, and they compress badly into written form.

**Urgency.** An incident at four in the afternoon Dutch time is midnight for a developer in Asia. If your product needs someone available during your business hours, that must be arranged explicitly and paid for, rather than assumed.

**Cultural and language nuance in customer-facing text.** Anything a Dutch customer reads should be written or checked by someone who writes Dutch natively. This is not a comment on capability; it is the same reason the SEO articles in this series argue against machine-translated marketing pages.

The practical response is to allocate deliberately: defined implementation work across the distance, exploratory and customer-facing work closer to you, and an explicit arrangement for incidents. That allocation, agreed at the start, is what makes the arrangement sustainable rather than a source of recurring friction.

## Trust Is Built by Structure, Not by Monitoring

Founders new to distant collaboration frequently reach for oversight: screen recording, hourly time tracking, frequent check-ins. It is understandable and it produces the worst version of the arrangement — a developer who feels watched, a founder who spends their day supervising, and no improvement in output.

What produces confidence is visible progress rather than visible activity.

**Small, frequent, reviewable work.** A change every day or two that you can see and use. This tells you more than any time report, because it is the actual product moving.

**A written daily update.** Three lines: done, next, blocked. It takes the developer two minutes and it removes the question that oversight was trying to answer.

**A short trial engagement.** Two weeks of real work before a longer commitment. Both sides learn whether it fits, and a poor match ends without anybody having invested much.

**Payment against delivered work** rather than against reported hours, where the work can be defined that way.

The founders who do well with distant developers describe something closer to a colleague than to a resource, and the arrangement that produces it is the same as for anyone: clear expectations, regular visible progress, prompt payment and the assumption of competence until contradicted.

## The Same Skill, Twice Over

The argument worth ending on: everything in this article is also how to get good results from an AI agent, and a founder who develops it gets both.

A precise outcome. The context that lets a sensible decision be made at an ambiguity. Explicit boundaries about what must not change. A way to verify. Written conventions the work should follow. Small, reviewable pieces rather than large ones. A review habit that closes the loop within a cycle.

That list applies without alteration to a developer eight time zones away and to an agent running on your own machine. It is the skill of specifying work for someone who is not in the room — and it is, increasingly, the central skill of building software this way at all.

Which suggests a practical order for a founder who is not technical: learn it on the AI tools first, where the cycle is minutes rather than a day and the cost of a poorly described task is a wasted prompt. Then apply it to people, where the same discipline is worth considerably more.

The founders in this series who describe distant collaboration as effortless are almost always the ones who had already learned to write a good specification for a machine.

## Setting This Up

For a founder working with a distant developer: tasks described with outcome, context, boundaries and pre-answered questions; the overlap reserved for decisions and demonstrations with a short daily call; everything else written, including a daily update of done, next and blocked; a conventions file so distant work fits the codebase; review as a morning habit within one cycle; a standing instruction to proceed on documented assumptions rather than waiting; and a visible list of what is next so blocked time is never idle.

## Real example

### A Day Lost to Every Question

Sylvia Grimberg built Evenementplanner in Lovable: event and venue coordination for conference organisers, used by 19 organisers running around 300 events a year.

She engaged a developer in Vietnam through a colleague's recommendation. The rate was good and the first three weeks were frustrating.

Her tasks were one line each: "add the attendee export", "fix the venue capacity bug". Each produced questions, each question waited until the following day, and roughly half of her working days ended with a question in her inbox and no progress. She reviewed work when she got to it, which was sometimes two days later. In three weeks, four small features had been completed.

Two business days of changing how the work was described, and the same developer: tasks rewritten to include the outcome, the context of which customer asked and why, explicit boundaries about what not to change, and answers to the questions she could anticipate; a fifteen-minute call at the start of his day, every day; a written daily update of done, next and blocked; a standing instruction to proceed on a documented assumption when blocked rather than stopping; a visible list of the next five tasks so a block never produced idle time; a conventions file written from the existing code; and review moved to twenty minutes first thing in her morning.

**Result:** in the following three weeks, eleven features were completed with the same developer at the same rate. Sylvia's assessment is that nothing about him changed — her descriptions did. She also notes that the same discipline made her own use of AI tools noticeably more effective, because it turned out to be the same skill.

> *"I was writing one-line tasks and losing a day to every question. Three weeks, four features. Then I started writing a paragraph instead of a line, and the next three weeks produced eleven."*
> — **Sylvia Grimberg, Founder, Evenementplanner (Utrecht)**

**Cost & Timeline:** €1,800 (task description framework, conventions file from existing code, daily written update and call structure, assumption-documentation practice, review rhythm) — completed in 2 business days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is working across time zones slower?

Only if work cannot proceed without you. A well-described task produces a result overnight; a one-line task produces a question and costs a day.

### What should the overlapping hours be used for?

Decisions, demonstrations, difficult conversations and the weekly priority discussion. Never status updates or anything that could have been written.

### How do I stop losing a day to every question?

Answer the obvious questions in the task itself, and instruct the developer to proceed on a documented assumption when blocked rather than waiting. A wrong assumption costs an hour; waiting costs a day.

### When should I review their work?

In your morning, within one cycle. A change submitted Monday evening and reviewed Wednesday has cost two days for no reason.

### What single document helps most?

A conventions file describing how the codebase works. Distant work fits the existing code when it exists and diverges in the author's own idiom when it does not.
