Of everything that can be done to improve how a small site ranks, internal linking has the best ratio of effort to result — and it is almost entirely absent from sites built with AI tools.

The reason it is absent is structural. Each page is generated in its own session, in response to its own request, with no knowledge of the pages that came before. So nothing links to anything except through the navigation, and a blog with thirty articles has thirty isolated pages sharing only a header.

Fixing this is an afternoon and it requires no new content.

## What Internal Links Do

Three things, and the third is the one founders underrate.

They distribute authority. Your home page has more than your newest article; a link passes some of it along, and pages linked from several relevant places rank better than isolated ones.

They tell search engines what a page is about, through the text of the link. A link reading "voorraadbeheer voor webshops" says more about the destination than one reading "click here".

And they keep readers on your site. Someone who reads an article and finds a relevant next one is someone still with you rather than back in search results — which matters commercially and is, separately, a signal about whether your content satisfied the visit.

## Find the Orphans First

An orphan page is one nothing links to. Crawlers may find it through a sitemap and it will rank poorly, because nothing on your site indicates it matters.

Finding them is mechanical: list every page, list every internal link, and subtract. In an AI-built site with any history, expect a third of the content to be orphaned — typically the older articles, since only the most recent appear on the blog index.

Every one of them needs at least two internal links from relevant pages. Not from a footer list, which is weak and looks like what it is, but from within the text of related content.

## Anchor Text Should Describe the Destination

The words in the link matter, and generated sites use "here", "this article" and "read more" almost exclusively.

Use the phrase the destination page is about. Vary it naturally across links — not the identical phrase every time, which reads as manipulation — and keep it descriptive: "how we handle iDEAL payments" rather than "payments" and certainly rather than "click here".

One caution: do not use the same anchor text pointing at different pages, which tells search engines two of your pages compete for the same thing. That is the cannibalisation problem, and it is usually solved by consolidating rather than by adjusting links.

## Build Hubs Rather Than a Flat Pile

The structure that works for a content-producing site is a hub with spokes.

A hub page covers a subject broadly and links to every detailed article about it. Each article links back to the hub and to two or three siblings. The hub is linked from your main navigation.

This does three things: it gives search engines a clear picture of which pages belong together, it concentrates authority on the hub which is usually your most commercially valuable page, and it gives readers an obvious route through your content.

For a product with thirty articles, three or four hubs is right. Each should be a genuinely useful page in its own right rather than a list of links — an overview of the subject, with the detailed articles linked from within the relevant sections.

## Link From the Old to the New, Not Only Forwards

The habit almost everyone gets wrong: when publishing a new article, they link from it to existing pages and stop there.

The valuable direction is the other one. Your older pages have more authority and more traffic, so a link from an established article to a new one does considerably more for the new page than the reverse.

So publishing should include a step: find two or three existing articles where the new one is genuinely relevant, and add a link within the text. Ten minutes, and it is the single most effective thing in this article.

## Automate What Can Be Automated, Write the Rest

Related-article blocks generated from shared tags are worth having: they catch what you forget and they keep old pages connected as new ones appear.

They are not a substitute for links within the text. A contextual link in a sentence, where a reader is already thinking about the subject, is both more useful to the reader and a stronger signal than a block of thumbnails at the bottom.

Use both. Automate the block, write the contextual links.

## Link to Your Commercial Pages, Not Only Between Articles

There is a pattern in small sites where all the internal linking happens between blog posts, and the pages that actually sell the product are linked only from the navigation.

That is backwards. Your feature pages, your pricing page and your use-case pages are where a reader becomes a customer, and they are usually the pages with the least internal support.

Every article should link to the commercial page it is closest to, from within the text, at the point where it becomes relevant — not as a call to action bolted to the end, which readers skip, but as the natural next step in the sentence where the reader has just understood why the problem matters.

Two effects. The commercial page accumulates authority from every article that mentions it, which is what makes it rank for the terms you actually want to win. And a reader who has just read a useful explanation is at the moment of maximum interest, which is a better place to offer the product than the bottom of a page they may not reach.

The discipline is to keep it honest. A link that genuinely helps the reader do the thing the article is about works; one shoehorned into every third paragraph is noticed immediately and costs more than it earns.

## Broken Links and Redirect Chains

Two mechanical problems accumulate quietly and both waste the work described above.

Internal links to pages that no longer exist. An article renamed, a page removed, a URL structure changed — every link pointing at the old address now returns an error, and in a generated site where links are typed by hand rather than derived, this happens more than people expect. Crawl your own site quarterly with any link checker and fix what it finds.

Redirect chains, where a link points at a URL that redirects to another that redirects again. Each hop is a delay for the reader and a dilution of the signal. After any URL change, update the internal links to point at the final destination rather than relying on the redirect to carry them — the redirect exists for external links and old bookmarks, not as a permanent part of your own navigation.

Both checks take ten minutes with a tool and both are worth attaching to the same quarterly review that looks for orphans. The three together — orphans, broken links, redirect chains — are the complete maintenance of a site's internal structure, and they are the sort of work that never feels urgent and quietly determines whether anything else you do has an effect.

## Depth From the Home Page Matters

One structural measure is worth checking because it is easy to get wrong and expensive to leave wrong: how many clicks from your home page it takes to reach any given page.

Three is a reasonable maximum for a small site. Pages buried five levels deep — reachable only through a category, then a subcategory, then a paginated list — receive very little of whatever authority the site has, and readers do not find them either.

Two common causes in AI-built sites. Pagination, where the blog index shows ten articles per page and article number forty-one is four clicks away through pages nobody visits. And category structures with only a handful of items each, which add a level without adding usefulness.

Both are fixed the same way: flatter structure, fewer levels, hub pages linking directly to everything beneath them. An index page listing all thirty articles grouped by subject is better for readers and for crawling than a paginated list showing ten at a time, and for a site of this size there is no reason to paginate at all.

The check takes five minutes: pick your three oldest articles and count the clicks from the home page. If any is more than three, the structure is burying your own work.

## Real example

### Thirty-One Articles, Nine Connected

Elise Vlasman built Planbaas Agenda in Lovable: appointment scheduling for beauty salons, physiotherapists and small clinics, 96 customers.

She had written 31 articles over two years and was frustrated that only four brought any traffic. Her assumption was that the other 27 were not good enough.

The audit found something else. Nine of the 31 were linked from anywhere at all — the blog index showed the ten most recent, and 22 older articles were reachable only through the sitemap. Of the links that did exist, almost all read "lees meer" or "hier". There was no hub structure, and her commercially important pages — the ones about appointment reminders and no-show reduction, where her product's actual value lay — had no internal links pointing at them at all.

One business day: every page mapped against every internal link, identifying 22 orphans; each orphan given two to four contextual links from relevant articles, written into the text rather than appended; anchor text rewritten across 140 links from generic phrases to descriptive ones; four hubs created — appointment scheduling, no-shows and reminders, salon administration, and practice management — each a genuinely useful overview page linking to its detailed articles, with every article linking back; automatic related-article blocks generated from tags to catch future gaps; the blog index changed to list all articles by hub rather than the ten most recent; and a publishing step added requiring two links from existing articles before a new one goes live.

**Result:** organic traffic rose from 420 to 1,900 visits a month over four months with no new articles written. Eleven of the previously orphaned articles now bring traffic, and the no-shows hub — which did not exist before — became her highest-converting page, producing more trial signups than her home page.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an orphan page?

One that no other page links to. Crawlers may find it via the sitemap but nothing on your site signals that it matters, so it ranks poorly. AI-built sites typically have many.

### How many internal links should a page have?

At least two pointing at it from relevant content, and a handful going out to genuinely related pages. Quality and relevance matter far more than quantity.

### What should link text say?

The subject of the destination page, varied naturally. Avoid "here" and "read more", and avoid using identical anchor text for different pages, which makes them compete.

### Should I link from new articles to old, or old to new?

Both, but old to new is the valuable direction — established pages have more authority to pass. Make it a step in your publishing routine.

### Are automatic related-article blocks enough?

They help and they are not sufficient. A contextual link inside a sentence is both more useful to a reader and a stronger signal than a block of thumbnails.
