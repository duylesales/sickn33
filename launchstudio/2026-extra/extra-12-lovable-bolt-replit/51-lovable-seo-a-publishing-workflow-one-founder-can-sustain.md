Look at the blog of almost any small SaaS and you will find the same shape: four articles published in one month, then nothing for a year. The dates are the whole story.

It is rarely a failure of intention. It is a failure of mechanism — publishing an article involves enough steps that it becomes a task requiring a free afternoon, and free afternoons do not arrive.

Search results reward consistency over a long period, which means the workflow matters more than any individual article. This is about making publishing cheap enough that it keeps happening.

## Where the Content Should Live

Three options, and the wrong one is the reason many founders stop.

**Markdown files in your repository**, built into pages at deploy time. Fast, versioned, no database, no admin interface to maintain. The right choice when you are the only author and you are comfortable with a repository.

**A headless CMS.** A hosted editor that your site reads content from. Right when someone non-technical will write, or when you want to write from a phone. Costs a subscription and a dependency.

**A blog built into your application**, with an editor you wrote. This is what an AI session will produce if asked, and it is almost always the wrong answer: you have built and must now maintain a piece of software that is not your product, to solve a problem that two better solutions already solve.

For a solo technical founder, markdown files. For everyone else, a CMS.

## The Cadence That Survives

Consistency beats volume, and the honest question is what you can sustain in a bad month.

One article a fortnight is a realistic target for a founder with a product to run. One a week is achievable with help. One a month is slow but genuinely compounds — twelve articles a year, every year, is more than most competitors manage.

What does not work is four in a week and then nothing. Search engines respond to a site that is regularly updated, and more importantly, a rhythm you can keep is a rhythm that produces fifty articles over three years while an ambitious plan produces five.

Two practices make the cadence real. Write in batches — a morning producing two or three drafts is far more efficient than three separate starts — and schedule them to publish over the following weeks. And keep a running list of ideas, so the writing session begins with a topic rather than with the question of what to write.

## What to Write

The best source is sitting in your inbox.

Every question a customer asked before buying is an article, and it is already phrased the way people search. Every support question that recurs is an article. Every objection you answer on sales calls is an article. Every time you explain your product's approach to something, that explanation is an article.

Three formats do most of the work for a small product. The direct answer to a question your buyers ask, which is the highest-converting content you will produce. The comparison against alternatives they are also considering, written honestly including where the alternative is better — dishonest comparisons are transparent and damage you. And the specific how-to for the thing your product helps with, which brings people who have the problem your product solves.

What to avoid: general industry commentary, news, and anything where you have no particular authority. It competes with publications that do it better and brings readers who will never buy.

## Make Publishing Cheap

Count the steps between a finished draft and a live page. If it is more than three, that is your problem.

The target: write the file, commit or press publish, done. Everything else — the URL, the title and description fields, the structured data, the sitemap entry, the internal linking, the social preview image — should be handled automatically or derived from the content.

Where automation is not possible, use a checklist rather than memory, and keep it short enough to follow. Five items that are always done beat fifteen that are done for the first three articles.

## Update Rather Than Only Adding

The instinct is that content work means new articles. For a site more than a year old, updating is frequently the better investment.

An article that ranks on page two and is two years old often reaches page one with an hour of work: current figures, a section covering what has changed, removed advice that no longer applies, and a fresher date.

Review quarterly. Find the pages with impressions but few clicks — the title and description are the problem — and the pages ranking just below where they would get traffic. Those are the cheapest wins available, and they take a fraction of the time a new article does.

## Using AI to Write, Sensibly

The question sits behind every discussion of content volume, and the honest answer is that it helps with some parts and harms others.

Where it works: turning a messy voice note into a structured draft, producing an outline from a topic, rewriting a paragraph you cannot get right, generating titles and descriptions to choose between, and translating your own writing into another language for you to correct.

Where it fails: writing the article. Not because the output is incompetent — it is fluent — but because it contains no information. An article about quotation software written by a model contains what every article about quotation software contains, and the thing that makes yours worth reading is the four years you have spent watching installers use one.

Search engines' stated position is that they reward helpful content regardless of how it was produced, and penalise content produced at scale to manipulate rankings. The practical reading: the fluency is not the problem, the emptiness is.

The workflow that produces good work quickly: talk through what you know about the topic, have the model organise it, then rewrite it in your own words with the specifics only you have — the numbers, the customer example, the thing that surprised you. That is thirty minutes for an article that could not have been written by anyone else, and it is a completely different activity from asking for a thousand words on a subject.

## Distribution Is Half the Work

An article published and left alone reaches whoever finds it in search, eventually, if it ranks. That is a slow and uncertain return on a morning's writing.

The same article deliberately distributed does considerably more, and the distribution costs fifteen minutes.

Send it to the people who asked the question. If the article came from a support conversation, reply to that thread with the link — the person asked, so they want it, and it is the most useful reply you can give. Do the same for the next person who asks, which is what turns an article into a support asset.

Post it where your buyers already are. For Dutch B2B products that is usually LinkedIn and one or two industry groups. One post, in your own words, with the link — not a promotional announcement.

Include it in whatever you send customers. A monthly email with the two articles you published is both useful and a reason to write them.

And link to it from your existing pages, which is covered in its own article in this series and is the one with the most durable effect.

None of this is marketing in any elaborate sense. It is making sure the thing you wrote reaches the people it was written for, which the passive version of publishing quietly assumes will happen by itself.

The compounding effect is worth naming: an article sent to three customers who each found it useful is an article that gets mentioned, and a mention from someone in your industry is worth more than a month of patient waiting for a ranking that may not arrive.

One further reason it pays: a fortnightly rhythm means the person who finds your site in six months finds twenty-five useful pages rather than four, which affects whether they believe you know the subject — a judgement people make in seconds and rarely revisit.

## Real example

### Six Articles in Eighteen Months

Timo Hoogland built Offertegenerator in Lovable: quotation software for installers and construction firms, 74 customers, acquired through advertising at a cost he was unhappy with.

He had published six articles in eighteen months and described himself as bad at content. The actual obstacle was mechanical. His blog was a custom editor built into his application, which required logging into the admin area, pasting content into a rich-text field that mangled formatting, uploading images separately, writing the URL slug by hand, filling four metadata fields, and manually adding the page to a sitemap file. Publishing took ninety minutes, so he did it when he had ninety minutes, which was rarely.

Two business days: the custom editor removed and content moved to markdown files in the repository, built into pages at deploy; URLs derived from filenames; titles, descriptions and structured data generated from the file's front matter; the sitemap generated from the files present; related articles linked automatically by shared tags; social preview images generated from the title; the existing six articles migrated; a five-item pre-publish checklist; and a seeded idea list built by going through eighteen months of support email and sales notes, which produced 34 topics in an afternoon.

**Result:** publishing went from ninety minutes to about five. Timo has since published 41 articles in fourteen months, mostly in batches of three on Friday mornings, scheduled across the following fortnight. Organic traffic went from 180 to 3,400 visits a month, and organic signups now exceed paid ones — which allowed him to cut his advertising spend by 60 percent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where should a small product's blog live?

Markdown files in your repository if you are the only author and comfortable with a repository; a headless CMS if someone non-technical will write. Building a custom editor into your application is almost always the wrong answer.

### How often should I publish?

At a rhythm you can keep in a bad month — a fortnight is realistic for most founders, a month still compounds. Four articles in a week followed by silence achieves very little.

### What should I write about?

The questions customers asked before buying, the support questions that recur, and the objections you answer on calls. They are already phrased the way people search.

### Should I write new articles or update old ones?

For a site over a year old, update first. An article ranking on page two often reaches page one with an hour of refreshing, which is far cheaper than a new piece.

### How do I keep it going when I am busy?

Reduce publishing to three steps or fewer, write in batches and schedule ahead, and keep a running idea list so a writing session never begins with a blank page.
