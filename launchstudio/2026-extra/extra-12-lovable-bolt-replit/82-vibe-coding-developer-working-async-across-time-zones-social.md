📝 The same task, written two ways (illustrative wording; both formats are what was used)

THE ONE-LINER
"Fix the venue capacity bug."

THE PARAGRAPH
Outcome: what should be true when it's done.
Context: which customer asked, and why it matters to them.
Boundaries: what not to change while you're in there.
Pre-answered: the questions you'll probably ask, already answered.
If blocked: proceed on a written assumption, don't stop.

Sylvia Grimberg built Evenementplanner in Lovable: event and venue coordination for 19 conference organisers running around 300 events a year. She hired a developer in Vietnam on a colleague's recommendation. The rate was good, and the first three weeks were frustrating. 🗓️

Every one-liner produced questions. Each question waited until the next day. About half her working days ended with a question in her inbox and no progress, and she sometimes reviewed work two days late. Three weeks: four small features.

Same developer, different instructions (LaunchStudio, 2 business days, €1,800):
✓ Tasks rewritten in the paragraph format above
✓ A fifteen-minute call at the start of his day, every day
✓ A written daily update: done, next, blocked
✓ A visible list of the next five tasks, so a block never means idle time
✓ A conventions file written from the existing code
✓ Her review moved to twenty minutes first thing in her morning

The next three weeks: eleven features, same developer, same rate. Nothing about him changed, Sylvia says. Her descriptions did.

And a side effect: the same discipline made her own AI tools noticeably more effective. It turned out to be the same skill.
