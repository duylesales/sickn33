There's a particular moment in an enterprise sales conversation where a founder's pitch and an IT security team's expectations stop being about the same product. It usually happens right after the phrase "AI in IT security" gets used — by the founder, describing a genuinely useful monitoring or anomaly-detection feature, and heard by the security team as a claim about a much bigger, more formal apparatus. Understanding what each side actually means by that phrase is the difference between a deal that stalls in due diligence and one that clears it.

## What an enterprise IT security team hears

To a buyer's security team, "AI in IT security" implies a specific bar: structured audit logging that records who accessed what and when, in a format that can be reviewed and exported; controls aligned with a recognized framework like SOC 2, even if formal certification isn't in place yet; documented incident response procedures; and some evidence that security posture is monitored continuously, not just built once and left alone. These aren't unreasonable expectations — they're standard procurement questions for any vendor handling business-sensitive data, and hearing "AI" attached to "security" in a pitch tends to raise the bar further, not lower it, because it implies sophistication.

## What most AI-generated SaaS products actually have

Most AI-native SaaS products at this stage have a basic activity log — a record of major events like logins or record changes, sufficient for a founder debugging their own app, but not structured, exportable, or comprehensive enough to satisfy a formal audit requirement. There's usually no documented incident response process because nothing has gone wrong yet requiring one. This isn't a failure specific to any one founder — it's simply where most products sit at this stage of maturity, and it's a completely different thing from what "AI in IT security" implies to a buyer evaluating vendors against a checklist.

## How to close the gap before a deal depends on it

- Get specific before the conversation happens: ask what the buyer's security team actually needs to see, rather than assuming your existing activity log will satisfy an unspecified bar.
- Separate what you have from what you're marketing. If your pitch mentions security in AI-adjacent language, make sure what's behind it matches what a technical reviewer will actually find.
- Prioritize structured, exportable audit logging over broader initiatives — it's usually the single item enterprise security teams ask about first, and the one most AI-generated apps lack by default.
- Have a written incident response plan, even a short one, rather than none at all — a documented process, however lean, answers a question that "we haven't needed one yet" doesn't.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does "AI in IT security" typically mean to an enterprise buyer?

It usually implies structured, exportable audit logging, controls resembling a framework like SOC 2, and documented incident response — a higher bar than most AI-generated SaaS products have by default.

### Is a basic activity log the same as audit logging?

No. An activity log records major events for internal debugging; audit logging is structured, comprehensive, and exportable in a format a formal reviewer or auditor can work with directly.

Not necessarily — just make sure what's behind the language matches what a technical reviewer will find, rather than letting the phrase imply more than your app currently delivers.
