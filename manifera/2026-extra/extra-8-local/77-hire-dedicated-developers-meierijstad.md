## The Decision Framework: What "Dedicated" Actually Needs to Mean

"Hire dedicated developers" gets used loosely enough in vendor marketing that a CTO evaluating options needs a working definition before comparing anyone. A dedicated developer, done properly, is not a shared contractor split across five client accounts and reachable on a best-effort basis — it's an engineer who works exclusively on your codebase, during hours that overlap meaningfully with your team, integrated into your existing sprint cadence, code review process, and deployment pipeline as if they were a direct hire.

That definition matters because it's the difference that determines whether the arrangement actually solves the Meierijstad CTO's problem or just adds another vendor relationship to manage. The architectural requirement is full access parity: the same repository permissions, the same CI/CD pipeline visibility, the same ticketing system access, and the same standup cadence as an in-house engineer. Anything less — a walled-off contractor working from specs thrown over a wall — reproduces the coordination tax of outsourcing without the throughput benefit of augmentation.

The second requirement is codebase-specific onboarding that goes deeper than a README. A dedicated developer joining mid-sprint needs the same context an internal hire would get in their first two weeks: architecture decision records, a walkthrough of the domain model, and a mentor relationship with an existing team member for the first month. Skipping this step is the single most common reason a "dedicated developer" engagement underperforms — not because the engineer lacks skill, but because nobody invested the onboarding time that makes any new team member productive, in-house or not.

The third requirement is a genuinely reversible commitment structure. A CTO hiring dedicated developers through an intermediary should be able to scale the engagement up or down on a defined notice period without severance obligations or contractual lock-in — this is one of the structural advantages a well-run dedicated-developer model has over a direct hire, and it's worth insisting on explicitly in any vendor contract rather than assuming it.

Fred Brooks's observation from *The Mythical Man-Month* is directly relevant here: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." The lesson isn't that hiring is pointless — it's that hiring without the onboarding and integration discipline described above adds headcount without adding throughput, which is functionally the same failure mode Brooks documented in 1975 and one that a rushed dedicated-developer engagement can reproduce just as easily as a rushed direct hire.

2. **Week 1-2 — Candidate matching and technical screening:** Manifera proposes matched engineers against the defined scope; the CTO's team runs its own technical interview, exactly as it would for a direct hire.
3. **Week 2 — Onboarding sprint:** The dedicated developer is granted full repository, CI/CD, and ticketing access, and paired with an internal mentor for architecture and domain context.
4. **Week 3-4 — Ramp to independent ownership:** The developer takes on standalone tickets with review, moving toward fully independent ownership of their scope.
5. **Ongoing — Standing sprint integration:** The developer participates in the same standups, retros, and planning sessions as the in-house team, with the engagement scalable up or down on a defined notice period.

### (Scenario: CTO whose local senior-developer search has stalled for a full quarter) At what point does it make sense to stop the local search and hire dedicated developers instead?

When the search has run past 8-10 weeks without a signed offer, or when accepted offers are being lost to counteroffers from larger regional employers — both are reliable signals that the local pool is structurally too thin for the timeline the roadmap needs.

### (Scenario: CTO worried a "dedicated developer" is really a shared contractor) How do I know a dedicated developer is actually dedicated and not split across other client accounts?

Insist on full access parity — the same repository, CI/CD, and ticketing access as an in-house hire, plus participation in your actual sprint cadence. A vendor unwilling to commit to that in writing is not offering a genuinely dedicated arrangement.
