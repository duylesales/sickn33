The build-versus-outsource software staffing decision is almost always presented to finance as a comparison of salary against day rate, and that comparison is wrong often enough, in large enough ways, that it deserves to be thrown out and rebuilt from scratch before any number reaches a board slide.

**The Pain:** A CFO at a dairy-technology company based in the Krimpenerwaard — the rural stretch of Zuid-Holland between Gouda and Rotterdam long associated with Gouda and Krimpener cheese production, now home to a growing cluster of agri-tech firms digitizing that same dairy supply chain — needs to decide whether to hire three in-house software engineers to build a new farm-to-processor traceability platform, or commit budget to an offshore software development team instead, and the recruiting agency's quoted salary figures make in-house hiring look deceptively affordable on the spreadsheet.

**The Agitation:** The company's last in-house engineering hire took five months from approved headcount to productive output once recruiting time, notice periods, and ramp-up were accounted for, and two of the four engineers hired in the previous eighteen months left within their first year, each departure triggering another full recruiting cycle and another few months of reduced team output — a pattern the CFO now has to explain to the board isn't simply bad luck, but a structural cost the salary-only comparison never captured in the first place.

## The Total Cost of Ownership Mandate

A CFO evaluating build versus outsource needs a cost model that goes meaningfully beyond gross salary versus day rate, because the salary-only comparison systematically understates in-house cost and overstates offshore cost in ways that consistently bias the decision toward the wrong answer.

First, the fully loaded cost of an in-house engineer includes far more than base salary: employer social security contributions, pension contributions, holiday allowance, benefits, equipment, office overhead allocated per seat, and management overhead for the time a CTO or VP of Engineering spends managing that person rather than doing other work. In the Netherlands, fully loaded cost typically runs meaningfully above gross salary once all of this is included, and a model that omits it is comparing an incomplete number against a complete one.

Second, ramp time and recruiting cost need to be amortized into the real cost of a hire, not treated as a one-time sunk cost outside the model. A recruiting cycle running three to five months before a new engineer is fully productive, plus the recruiting agency fee typically running a meaningful percentage of first-year salary, represents real cash and real calendar time that a dedicated offshore team — sourced and onboarded in weeks, not months — simply does not incur in the same way.

Third, attrition risk has to be priced, not ignored. Every in-house departure resets the recruiting and ramp clock and creates a knowledge-transfer gap, and a company with a demonstrated pattern of early departures should model attrition probability explicitly into the comparison rather than assuming the next hire will be the one who stays.

Fourth, workforce elasticity has real financial value that a static headcount comparison misses entirely. A traceability platform build that needs six engineers for four months and two engineers afterward is expensive to staff in-house, where headcount reductions carry real severance and morale cost, but is a straightforward scaling conversation with a dedicated offshore team structured around project phases.

Fifth, the comparison has to account for management overhead correctly on both sides. In-house hiring concentrates management burden on internal leadership continuously; a well-governed offshore engagement shifts a meaningful share of day-to-day delivery management to the vendor's own leadership structure, freeing internal leadership time for strategic work — a real cost difference that rarely appears on a spreadsheet but shows up clearly in how a CTO or VP of Engineering actually spends their week.

Sixth, currency and rate stability matter for multi-year budget planning. A dedicated offshore team engagement, properly contracted, can offer more budget predictability over a multi-year horizon than in-house hiring in a competitive local labor market where salary inflation for scarce technical skills has been running well ahead of general wage growth.

## By the Numbers

- Fully loaded in-house engineering cost in the Netherlands typically runs meaningfully higher than gross salary once employer contributions, benefits, and overhead are included — a gap finance models frequently underestimate.
- Recruiting-to-productive-output timelines for a mid-level to senior engineer typically span three to five months once search, interviews, notice periods, and onboarding ramp are all accounted for.
- Companies with recent attrition patterns similar to two departures in eighteen months typically see recruiting and ramp costs recur at a rate that meaningfully erodes any apparent savings from in-house hiring.
- Organizations that model workforce elasticity explicitly — the ability to scale a team up or down with project phases — consistently find outsourced or hybrid staffing options financially favorable for projects with a defined, non-permanent peak staffing need.

## Common Pitfalls for Krimpenerwaard-Based Finance and Engineering Teams

- **Comparing gross salary directly against day rate:** This omits employer contributions, benefits, overhead, recruiting cost, and ramp time on the in-house side, systematically favoring in-house hiring in a way the real numbers don't support.
- **Ignoring the calendar-time cost of recruiting:** A five-month gap between approved headcount and productive output is a real delay to the roadmap, not a footnote to the budget conversation.
- **Failing to price attrition risk into the model:** A company with a recent pattern of early departures should treat the next hire's retention as uncertain, not as a best-case assumption.
- **Assuming project-based offshore work only makes sense for short engagements:** Dedicated offshore teams work well for both short projects and long-term platform ownership; the workforce-elasticity advantage applies at any duration where staffing needs actually fluctuate.
- **Treating management overhead as a sunk cost on the in-house side only:** In-house hiring concentrates delivery management burden on internal leadership continuously; failing to price that time against leadership's other priorities understates the true comparison.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO comparing a recruiting agency's salary quote against an offshore day rate) Why does a salary-versus-day-rate comparison give a misleading answer?

Gross salary omits employer contributions, benefits, overhead, recruiting fees, and ramp time, all of which are real costs on the in-house side. A fully loaded total-cost-of-ownership comparison consistently shows a smaller gap, and often a reversed one, compared to the salary-only version.

### (Scenario: CFO whose company has had recent engineering attrition) How should recent attrition history factor into a build-versus-outsource decision?

Model it explicitly as a probability of recurrence, not as bad luck. A company with a recent pattern of early departures should assume some chance the next hire follows the same pattern, and price that risk into the in-house side of the comparison.

### (Scenario: CFO evaluating a project with a temporary staffing peak) Does outsourcing make sense for a project with a temporary staffing peak, or only for permanent needs?

It's often a better fit specifically for temporary peaks. A dedicated offshore team can scale up during a build phase and down during maintenance without the severance and morale cost that comes with reducing in-house headcount.

### (Scenario: CFO worried about losing management visibility with an offshore team) Does outsourcing reduce our visibility and control over the engineering work?

Not with proper governance structure. A well-run engagement includes regular reporting and an accountable delivery lead, and often frees internal leadership time that was previously spent on day-to-day management of in-house hires.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding how long it takes to see savings from switching models) How quickly does switching from in-house hiring to a dedicated offshore team show up in the budget?

Most companies see the eliminated recruiting cost alone recover the transition cost within the first quarter, with the workforce-elasticity savings compounding over the life of the project beyond that.
