## The Architectural Mandate: Five Things Edam-Volendam CFOs Get Wrong About Software Cost

**1. Treating "custom software" as a single price category.** A CFO who asks "what does custom software cost" is asking an underspecified question — the honest answer depends entirely on integration complexity, data volume, and whether the build needs to talk to existing systems. A standalone internal tool and a customer-facing platform integrated with three external systems are different cost categories entirely, not points on the same scale.

**2. Budgeting for development only, not the full lifecycle.** Development labor is typically 55-65% of total cost. The remaining 35-45% — architecture, QA, DevOps, and post-launch support — gets left out of early estimates constantly, which is exactly why so many "custom software" budgets run short mid-project without anyone having done anything wrong on the build itself.

**3. Assuming cost scales linearly with team size.** Doubling the engineering headcount on a project doesn't halve the timeline — coordination overhead grows with team size, and a four-person pod with clear domain ownership frequently outpaces an eight-person team without it. Budgeting for a bigger team as a shortcut to a faster timeline is often the more expensive path to the same delivery date.

**4. Ignoring the cost of not building it.** CFOs evaluate custom software cost in isolation far more often than they evaluate it against the cost of the manual process it replaces. A production-scheduling system that costs €90,000 to build but eliminates 15 hours a week of manual coordination across two staff members pays for itself within roughly eighteen months — a comparison that rarely makes it into the initial cost conversation.

**5. Underpricing post-launch maintenance.** A custom application that ships without a maintenance budget attached is a liability waiting to surface as an emergency invoice. Ongoing maintenance — security patching, dependency updates, minor enhancements — typically runs 15-20% of the original build cost annually, and a CFO who doesn't budget for it in year one is budgeting for a surprise in year two.

Getting the number right requires decomposing the build into its actual cost drivers — architecture and discovery, core development by role, QA, DevOps and infrastructure, and ongoing maintenance — rather than applying an industry rule of thumb that was never calibrated to the specific project's integration complexity or data volume.

## The Economics

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO building a business case with conflicting online cost estimates) Why do online estimates for custom software cost vary so much?

Because "custom software" spans wildly different complexity levels, and most published rules of thumb don't specify integration complexity or data volume. A defensible estimate has to be built from your specific project's cost drivers, not an industry-wide average.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding project team size to hit a deadline) Will a bigger team get us to launch faster for the same total cost?

Not necessarily — coordination overhead grows with team size, and a smaller team with clear domain ownership often outpaces a larger, less coordinated one on both speed and cost. Team sizing should follow the architecture's natural ownership boundaries, not the deadline alone.

### (Scenario: CFO preparing a board presentation for a software investment) How do I make the case for software spend against the cost of doing nothing?

Quantify the manual process it replaces in fully loaded labor hours and error cost, then compare that recurring annual cost against the one-time build investment plus ongoing maintenance — this comparison is usually the most persuasive part of a board presentation and is often missing from the first draft.

### (Scenario: CFO budgeting for the year after launch) How much should we budget for maintenance after the software ships?

Plan for roughly 15-20% of the original build cost annually for security patching, dependency updates, and minor enhancements. Omitting this from the first-year budget is one of the most common reasons software projects need an unplanned follow-up budget request.

### (Scenario: CFO in a public-sector or procurement-governed organization) Can an itemized cost model survive a formal procurement or audit review?

Yes, when it's built from the five underlying cost categories with documented assumptions for each, an itemized model is specifically designed to withstand the kind of line-by-line scrutiny a procurement board or internal audit process applies.
