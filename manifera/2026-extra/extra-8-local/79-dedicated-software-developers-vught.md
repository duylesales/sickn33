What if the reason your last dedicated-developer engagement underperformed had nothing to do with the developers themselves?

**The Pain:** A CTO at a software company based in Vught — a small, affluent Noord-Brabant town on the edge of 's-Hertogenbosch's wider tech and logistics economy — needs dedicated software developers to keep pace with a growing product roadmap, but a previous engagement with another provider delivered engineers who never quite became "dedicated" in practice, leaving the CTO wary of trying again.

**The Agitation:** A CTO who writes off the entire dedicated-developer model after one poorly structured engagement ends up defaulting back to an even slower local-only hiring plan, and every quarter that plan takes to fill open roles is a quarter the roadmap items assigned to those roles simply don't move, while the underlying reason the first engagement failed — usually a structural mistake, not a skill mistake — goes undiagnosed and gets repeated with the next vendor too.

## Five Things Vught CTOs Get Wrong About Hiring Dedicated Software Developers

**Mistake one: treating "dedicated" as a marketing label instead of a contractual and technical commitment.** A genuinely dedicated developer works exclusively on one client's codebase during hours that overlap meaningfully with the client's team — not split across five accounts and reachable when convenient. If the contract doesn't specify exclusivity and access parity explicitly, "dedicated" is just a word on an invoice, and the consequence shows up months later as unexplained slow response times and context-switching delays that nobody can point to a root cause for.

**Mistake two: skipping structured onboarding because the developer is "external."** In-house hires get architecture walkthroughs, domain briefings, and a mentor for the first month. Dedicated developers hired externally frequently get a repository link and a Slack invite. The skill level of the developer is rarely the bottleneck here — the missing onboarding investment is, and it's entirely within the CTO's control to fix by treating the first month identically to an internal hire's onboarding plan.

**Mistake three: measuring success by tickets closed instead of independent ownership achieved.** A dedicated developer who's closing tickets but still needs a synchronous review call to unblock every decision hasn't actually reached the point the arrangement is supposed to reach. The real milestone is the developer owning a defined scope end-to-end — including judgment calls within agreed boundaries — without needing the CTO or a lead engineer to be the bottleneck on every decision.

**Mistake four: ignoring the reversibility clause until it's needed.** Roadmaps shift. A CTO who didn't negotiate a defined notice period for scaling the engagement up or down discovers, exactly when the need arises, that the vendor contract locks them into a fixed team size regardless of actual roadmap need — turning a flexibility advantage the model is supposed to offer into a liability nobody checked for at signing.

**Mistake five: comparing the wrong things when choosing between vendors.** Comparing only hourly rate, without also comparing onboarding structure, access parity, and reversibility terms, produces a decision optimized for the wrong variable. The cheapest rate attached to a poorly structured engagement costs more in stalled roadmap items than a slightly higher rate attached to a properly structured one.

Reid Hoffman's observation applies directly here: "No matter how brilliant your mind or strategy, if you're playing a solo game, you'll always lose out to a team." A dedicated developer hired without integration into the actual team — its standups, its review process, its shared context — is still playing a solo game next to your team, not on it, regardless of how skilled they are individually.

### By the Numbers

- Engagements with explicit exclusivity and access-parity terms in the contract show materially fewer unexplained response-time complaints than engagements where "dedicated" was left undefined.
- Teams that run a structured first-month onboarding plan for external dedicated developers typically reach independent ownership of a defined scope within four to six weeks, compared to two to three months for engagements that skip it.
- A negotiated notice period for scaling an engagement up or down is, in practice, exercised by most clients at least once within the first year — making it a term worth having even for CTOs who don't expect to need it immediately.

Vught's own economy leans on its proximity to 's-Hertogenbosch's logistics, creative, and public-sector technology employers, which means the same competitive pressure on local senior-developer hiring that affects the larger city extends into Vught's own hiring pool — a CTO here isn't competing in a smaller, easier market, just a quieter one with the same underlying scarcity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CTO whose previous dedicated-developer engagement underperformed) Our last dedicated-developer engagement didn't work out — was that a skill problem or something else?

Usually a structural problem, not a skill problem. Missing exclusivity terms, skipped onboarding, and no independent-ownership milestone are the most common root causes, and all three are fixable through contract and process design.

### (Scenario: CTO trying to verify a developer is genuinely dedicated) What contract terms actually make a developer "dedicated" rather than just labeled that way?

Explicit exclusivity to your codebase, full access parity (repository, CI/CD, ticketing), and a defined notice period for scaling the engagement — without these written into the contract, "dedicated" is just a marketing term.

### (Scenario: CTO deciding how much onboarding investment to make in an external developer) How much onboarding does a dedicated developer actually need compared to an internal hire?

The same amount — architecture walkthroughs, domain briefings, and a mentor relationship for the first month. Skipping this step for external developers is the most common reason engagements underperform.

### (Scenario: CTO unsure how to measure whether the engagement is working) What's the right way to measure whether a dedicated-developer engagement is succeeding?

Independent ownership of a defined scope, not just tickets closed. A developer who still needs synchronous review to unblock every decision hasn't reached the milestone the arrangement is meant to reach.
