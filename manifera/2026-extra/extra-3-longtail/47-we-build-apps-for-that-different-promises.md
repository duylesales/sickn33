Every vendor a founder talks to says some version of "yes, we can build that" — it's the answer sales conversations are structurally designed to produce. The confidence behind that sentence varies enormously between "we've built exactly this five times" and "we've never built this specific thing, but we're confident we could figure it out" — and from the outside, in a sales call, the two sound identical.

## Why the Same Words Hide Wildly Different Realities

"We build apps for that" can mean a team has deep, repeated experience with your specific type of project — the integrations, the regulatory considerations, the scale patterns — or it can mean a generalist team is confident in their general engineering ability to figure out something genuinely new to them. Both are legitimate positions to be in, but they carry very different risk profiles, and a founder deserves to know which one they're actually getting.

## Questions That Reveal Which Kind of "Yes" You're Getting

- **"How many projects like this have you actually built?"** — a specific number, not "several" or "quite a few," reveals genuine repeated experience versus a first-time attempt framed confidently.
- **"What's the hardest technical problem you hit on a similar past project, and how did you solve it?"** — a team with real experience has a specific, textured answer. A team without it tends to answer in generalities or pivot to a different question.
- **"Which of your current team members have actually worked on something like this?"** — distinguishing whether the relevant experience sits with people who would work on your project, versus experience the company has in the abstract, possibly from people no longer there.
- **"What would you do differently on this project compared to a typical one?"** — a team with genuine specific experience can usually name the ways your project's particular requirements would change their normal approach. A generalist confident answer tends to sound the same regardless of what's actually being asked.

## Why This Matters Even When the Generalist Answer Is Honest

A generalist "yes, we can figure this out" isn't necessarily a red flag — strong engineering teams can genuinely deliver on unfamiliar problem types. What matters is that the founder knows which situation they're in, so they can adjust their expectations around discovery time, potential unknowns, and risk buffer accordingly, rather than assuming deep specific experience that isn't actually there.

## The Economics of Words That Cost Nothing to Say

Economists Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel formalized a distinction, in a widely cited 1982 paper, between what game theorists call cheap talk and costly signaling. Cheap talk is communication that costs the sender nothing to produce regardless of whether it's true, and is not directly verifiable by the receiver at the moment it's said — a vendor saying "we can build that" costs precisely the same breath whether the team has done it five times or never once. Costly signaling, by contrast, is communication or action that would be genuinely expensive or difficult to produce falsely — a specific, named past project with technical details a receiver could actually check imposes a real cost on the sender if it turns out to be fabricated, in a way a generic affirmation never does.

Crawford and Sobel's formal result was that cheap talk can still convey some real information in equilibrium, but only in proportion to how well the sender's and receiver's interests are aligned — the less aligned the incentives, the less informative cheap talk becomes, collapsing toward pure noise as incentive misalignment grows. A sales conversation is close to the least-aligned case the framework describes: the vendor's immediate incentive is to close the deal, which favors saying yes regardless of the sender's true private information about whether "yes" is actually accurate. This is precisely why "we can build that" alone carries close to zero real information content, and why the four questions in this article work: each one demands a costly signal — a specific number, a named problem, an identifiable person, a tailored answer — that would be measurably more expensive to fabricate convincingly than the cheap talk it replaces.

## Case Study: A Katowice Founder's Probing Conversation

A founder at Silesia-based logistics startup Węglar asked four vendors the "how many projects like this have you built" question directly during discovery calls for a multi-carrier tracking platform. Three vendors gave vague answers ("we've done similar work") that, on further probing, revealed no genuinely comparable past project. The fourth described two specific past projects with named technical challenges and how they were solved.

The founder chose the fourth vendor specifically because the specificity of the answer matched what a direct reference call later confirmed — a genuine, checkable track record rather than confident generality.

> *"Every single vendor said yes to my requirements. Only one of them could actually back it up with a real story when I pushed."*
> — **Founder, Węglar**

The founder now describes the four probing questions internally as "the cost test" — a shorthand for asking, of any vendor claim, whether the specific claim being made would actually cost something real to fabricate, or whether it's free to say regardless of whether it's true.

## Why the Costly Signal Still Requires Verification

A costly signal is more informative than cheap talk, but Crawford and Sobel's framework doesn't claim it's automatically true — it claims only that it's more expensive to fake, which raises the bar without eliminating the possibility of deception entirely. A named past project and a specific technical problem could, in principle, still be embellished or partially fabricated by a sufficiently motivated vendor. This is exactly why the reference call remains the final step even after a vendor clears the specificity bar: it converts the costly signal from merely expensive-to-fabricate into independently verifiable, closing the small remaining gap a specific answer alone doesn't fully close on its own.

## Applying the Cost Test Beyond the Discovery Call

The same logic extends usefully past the initial sales conversation, into how a founder reads proposals, contracts, and even mid-project status updates. A proposal that describes deliverables in specific, checkable terms — named modules, concrete milestones, testable acceptance criteria — is a costlier, more informative signal than one written in confident but vague language about "robust, scalable solutions," precisely because the specific version is falsifiable in a way the vague version structurally isn't. A status update that says "on track" costs nothing to produce whether or not it's accurate; one that says "the payment integration is passing its test suite, and here's the failing edge case we're still working through" is a costly signal a team without real progress would find much harder to convincingly fabricate.

Treating this as a general filter, not a one-time discovery-call trick, gives a founder a durable habit for the entire life of a vendor relationship: whenever a claim feels reassuring but vague, ask what it would cost the other party to make that exact claim if it weren't true. If the honest answer is "nothing," the claim is cheap talk, informative mainly about the sender's incentive to reassure rather than about the underlying reality — and worth treating as a prompt for a more specific follow-up question rather than as settled information on its own.

## Confident Generality vs. Specific Experience

| Question | Confident Generality Answer | Specific Experience Answer |
|---|---|---|
| "How many projects like this?" | "Several" / "Quite a few" | A specific number with named examples |
| "Hardest problem you hit?" | Vague, deflects to a different topic | Textured, specific technical detail |
| "Who on your team has done this?" | Unclear or company-level claim only | Named individuals, verifiable |
| "What would you do differently here?" | Generic answer regardless of specifics | Tailored to your project's actual requirements |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder hearing confident "yes" answers from every vendor) Why does every vendor say yes when I ask if they can build my project?

Because sales conversations are structurally biased toward confident affirmative answers — the useful information isn't whether they say yes, it's how specifically they can back that yes up when probed further.

### (Scenario: founder worried a generalist "yes" is automatically risky) Is it always risky to work with a team that hasn't built something exactly like my project before?

Not always — strong generalist engineering teams can deliver well on unfamiliar problem types. The risk comes from not knowing which situation you're in, which is why probing for specificity matters more than the yes/no answer itself.

### (Scenario: founder trying to prepare for a vendor discovery call) What's the single best question to distinguish genuine experience from confident generality?

Asking for a specific number of comparable past projects, along with a specific hard problem from one of them and how it was solved, is the fastest way to separate genuine repeated experience from general confidence.

### (Scenario: founder unsure how to weigh a first-time answer) If a vendor honestly admits they haven't built exactly this before, should I rule them out?

Not necessarily — an honest admission paired with a thoughtful plan for managing the unknowns is often a better sign than false confidence, since it suggests the vendor will scope risk and discovery appropriately rather than overpromise.

### (Scenario: founder trying to verify a vendor's claimed specific experience) How do I verify that a vendor's specific project examples are actually real?

Ask for a reference call specifically tied to the example they described — a vendor with a genuine track record will connect you readily; hesitation here is itself informative.
