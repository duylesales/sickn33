A founder comparing developer profiles often defaults to counting listed technologies — more frameworks, more languages, more tools mentioned on a resume reads as more capable. A specific design concept from product development explains why that count is a weaker signal than it appears, and what a founder should actually be looking for instead.

## Why Breadth Alone Doesn't Predict Project Success

A developer who's touched ten frameworks across a career has, by definition, spent less depth-building time with any single one of them than a developer who's spent the same career years going deep on two or three. Breadth and depth trade off against the same finite quantity — time and attention — which means a long technology list on a resume isn't simply "more capability," it's a specific trade-off that founders rarely evaluate consciously, often assuming more listed technologies straightforwardly means a stronger developer, when it may actually mean a developer who's never gone deep enough with any single technology to develop the kind of pattern recognition that only comes from sustained, concentrated experience.

## The Design Concept That Names the Right Balance

The concept of "T-shaped skills," popularized within design and product development circles including by design firm IDEO's former CEO Tim Brown in writing published in the 2000s, describes a specific, deliberately balanced skill profile: the vertical stroke of the T represents deep expertise in one or a small number of specific domains, while the horizontal stroke represents a broader, shallower familiarity across many adjacent domains, enough to communicate effectively and collaborate productively with specialists in those areas without needing deep expertise in all of them personally. The concept was developed specifically as an alternative to two less useful extremes: the narrow "I-shaped" specialist with deep expertise but poor collaborative range, and the flat generalist with broad exposure but no genuine depth anywhere.

Applied to evaluating a developer or a development team, the T-shaped framework offers a more precise diagnostic than either "how many technologies do they know" or "how deep is their single specialization" alone: a genuinely strong developer for most real projects has real, demonstrated depth in the specific technology stack a project actually needs — the vertical stroke — combined with enough broader exposure to collaborate well across adjacent concerns like infrastructure, design, or a different part of the stack — the horizontal stroke — without that breadth substituting for the depth a project's core technical challenges actually require.

## Why a Long Technology List Can Signal the Wrong Shape

A resume or team profile emphasizing sheer technology count, without clear evidence of sustained depth in any of them, may be signaling a flat generalist profile — broad horizontal exposure with an underdeveloped vertical stroke — which is a meaningfully weaker fit for a project with genuine technical complexity in a specific area than a developer with a shorter but deeper, more focused list would be. This isn't a universal rule against breadth — some roles, particularly at a very early-stage startup building simple, low-complexity systems, genuinely benefit from a flatter generalist profile that can move fluidly across many small tasks. But for a project with real technical depth requirements in a specific domain, a founder optimizing purely for the longest technology list risks selecting for exactly the wrong shape of expertise.

## What to Actually Look for Instead of Counting Technologies

- **Evidence of sustained depth in the specific stack your project actually needs**, not just familiarity — ask about a specific hard problem solved within that stack, not just whether it appears on a list.
- **Genuine collaborative range beyond the core specialization**, evaluated by asking how a developer has worked with adjacent disciplines — infrastructure, design, product — rather than assuming breadth of listed technologies substitutes for this.
- **The shape of a career's technology history, not just its total count**, distinguishing a developer who's gone deep in a few areas over time from one who's moved broadly and shallowly across many.
- **Team-level T-shape, not just individual**, since a well-composed team can combine several individually T-shaped specialists whose vertical strokes cover a project's different depth requirements collectively, which matters more for team hiring than any single individual's personal breadth.

## Why the Horizontal Stroke Still Matters, Not Just the Vertical One

It's worth being explicit that the T-shaped framework isn't simply an argument for depth over breadth — the horizontal stroke is a genuinely necessary part of the shape, not a nice-to-have. A developer with real depth but zero horizontal range struggles specifically at the points where a project's core technical work touches other disciplines: a backend specialist with no functional understanding of how frontend or infrastructure concerns interact with their own work tends to design in ways that create friction for the rest of the team, even when the core depth itself is genuinely excellent. This is precisely why the T-shaped concept was developed as an alternative to the narrow "I-shaped" specialist profile, not just as an alternative to the flat generalist — both extremes are incomplete in their own specific way.

The practical implication for evaluating a developer or team is that the useful question isn't "depth or breadth," treated as an either-or choice, but "does this profile have both strokes in the right proportion for what my project actually needs." A project with a single, isolated, highly specialized technical challenge might genuinely be well served by something closer to an I-shape. Most real projects, which involve a core technical challenge that has to integrate with a broader system, are better served by genuine T-shaped profiles — real depth where it counts, combined with enough breadth to collaborate well across everything the depth has to connect to.

## Case Study: A Novi Sad Founder's Reconsidered Hire

A founder at Novi Sad-based startup Petrovaradin Tech had initially favored a freelance developer whose profile listed exposure to over fifteen different frameworks and languages, reading the breadth as evidence of strong, versatile capability for a project requiring deep, specific expertise in a particular real-time data processing stack.

A technical advisor pointed out, during a review of the freelancer's actual project history, that none of the fifteen-plus technologies had more than a few months of documented use, and none matched the specific depth the project's core technical challenge actually required. The founder instead chose a team with demonstrated, multi-year depth in exactly the required stack, combined with reasonable collaborative range across adjacent concerns — a considerably shorter technology list, but the right shape for the project's actual needs.

> *"The long list looked more impressive on paper. What it actually meant was a few months each with fifteen things, when what I needed was years with the one thing that actually mattered for my project."*
> — **Founder, Petrovaradin Tech**

Petrovaradin Tech's founder now asks specifically about depth and duration of experience with core relevant technologies during any hiring or vendor evaluation, treating a long, breadth-heavy list as a prompt for further questions rather than a positive signal on its own.

## Reading Technical Profiles by Shape, Not Length

| Profile Shape | Description | Best Fit |
|---|---|---|
| I-shaped (narrow specialist) | Deep in one area, limited collaborative range | Highly specialized, isolated technical work |
| Flat generalist | Broad exposure, limited depth anywhere | Simple, low-complexity, varied early-stage tasks |
| T-shaped | Real depth in core area, functional breadth elsewhere | Most real projects with genuine technical complexity |
| Team-level T-shape | Multiple T-shaped specialists covering different depths | Complex projects needing multiple deep specializations |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder comparing developer profiles by technology count) Should I favor a developer or team with the longest list of technologies they've worked with?

Not automatically — a long list can signal broad but shallow exposure rather than genuine depth, which is a weaker fit for a project with real technical complexity in a specific area than focused, sustained expertise would be.

### (Scenario: founder trying to evaluate genuine depth) How can I tell if a developer's experience with a technology is genuinely deep or just surface familiarity?

Ask about a specific hard problem they solved within that technology, and how long they sustained real, ongoing work with it — genuine depth produces a specific, detailed answer that surface familiarity generally can't.

### (Scenario: founder wondering if breadth is ever the right priority) Is a broad generalist profile ever the right choice for a project?

Yes — for a very early-stage, low-complexity project needing flexible movement across many small tasks, a flatter generalist profile can be a reasonable fit, though this is a narrower use case than founders often assume.

### (Scenario: founder trying to evaluate a whole team rather than one hire) Does the T-shaped concept apply to evaluating a team, not just an individual developer?

Yes — a well-composed team can combine several individually T-shaped specialists whose depth areas collectively cover a project's different technical requirements, which is often more relevant than any single individual's personal breadth.

### (Scenario: founder trying to write a better job posting or RFP) How should I describe what I actually need if not by listing required technologies broadly?

Describe the specific technical depth your project's core challenges require, and separately note the collaborative range needed across adjacent concerns — this produces a more accurate match than a broad, unprioritized technology wishlist.
