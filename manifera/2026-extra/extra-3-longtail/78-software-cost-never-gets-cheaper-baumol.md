A founder who watched the price of a laptop fall steadily for two decades while its capability rose reasonably expects software development to follow that same curve — better tools, more automation, cheaper output over time. Custom software development costs have, if anything, risen steadily in real terms across that same period, and the reason isn't inefficiency or a failure to adopt better tools. It's a specific, well-documented economic pattern that applies to labor-intensive services generally, not a problem unique to the software industry.

## Why Manufacturing and Software Follow Different Cost Curves

A manufactured good's cost falls over time primarily because production can be automated, parallelized, and mechanized — a factory producing laptops benefits directly from robotics, process optimization, and economies of scale in a way that reduces the human labor required per unit produced. Custom software development, despite genuine tooling improvements, still fundamentally requires a skilled person to understand a specific business problem, make design judgments, and produce a working, tested solution — and that core act of skilled judgment hasn't been mechanized away the way physical assembly has been, even as the tools surrounding that judgment have genuinely improved.

## The Economic Concept That Explains This Directly

Economists William Baumol and William Bowen, in a 1966 study originally examining the performing arts, documented what has since become known as Baumol's cost disease: the observation that labor-intensive services requiring a fixed amount of skilled human time — their original example was a string quartet, which takes exactly as many musician-hours to perform in 2026 as it did in 1826 — see their relative cost rise over time even without any decline in productivity, specifically because wages across the broader economy rise (driven by productivity gains in sectors that do automate well), and a service that hasn't automated has to raise its own prices to keep attracting skilled workers who could otherwise earn the rising wages available in sectors that have.

Baumol and Bowen's insight applies to custom software development with unusual precision. A skilled engineer's time, like a musician's, is the core input a custom build actually requires, and while tooling has made that time somewhat more productive, it hasn't eliminated the need for it the way automation eliminated much of the manual labor in manufacturing. Meanwhile, the wages skilled engineers could earn elsewhere in the economy — including in sectors that have automated and become more productive — keep rising, and custom software development has to keep pace with that rising opportunity cost to continue attracting the same caliber of talent, producing exactly the pattern Baumol's cost disease predicts: real cost increases over time, not because the service has become less efficient, but because the skilled labor it depends on has an ever-rising price elsewhere in the economy.

## Why AI-Assisted Development Complicates, but Doesn't Eliminate, the Pattern

AI-assisted coding tools genuinely increase a skilled engineer's output per hour for certain kinds of work, which looks, on the surface, like exactly the kind of productivity gain that should push software costs down the way automation did for manufacturing. The complication is that AI assistance speeds up the more mechanical, pattern-matching parts of development considerably more than it speeds up the judgment-heavy parts — architecture decisions, understanding a specific ambiguous business requirement, evaluating trade-offs — which remain, for now, a fundamentally human, skilled-labor bottleneck in Baumol's original sense. This is precisely why the earlier expectation that AI would make custom software dramatically cheaper hasn't fully materialized: the tool speeds up part of the process, but the part that hasn't sped up is still the part setting the effective price floor.

## What This Means for How a Founder Should Budget Over Time

- **Expect nominal and real software development costs to trend upward over multi-year horizons**, not downward, absent a genuine structural change in how much skilled human judgment a build requires — this is the normal pattern for a Baumol-affected service, not a sign of an inefficient market.
- **Don't benchmark against manufacturing cost trends when planning a multi-year software budget**, since the two follow structurally different cost curves for well-understood economic reasons, not because one industry is simply better run than the other.
- **Value efficiency gains from tooling and AI assistance as genuinely real, but partial**, reducing cost growth rather than reversing it, since the judgment-heavy core of custom development remains a Baumol-affected bottleneck even as the mechanical parts speed up.
- **Treat a vendor's efficiency and track record as more important over time, not less**, since a Baumol-affected market rewards genuine productivity advantages more, not less, as the overall cost baseline keeps rising around everyone.

## Why This Isn't a Reason for Pessimism About the Industry

It's worth being explicit that Baumol's cost disease describing a pattern isn't the same as the pattern being a sign of dysfunction — Baumol and Bowen's own framing was specifically that cost disease afflicts sectors doing nothing wrong, simply competing for the same skilled labor pool as sectors that have automated more successfully. A founder encountering rising software costs over time isn't necessarily working with a vendor that's becoming less efficient or less honest — they're operating in a market where the underlying economics apply to every vendor simultaneously, which is precisely why the framework is useful: it explains an industry-wide pattern a founder would otherwise be tempted to read as evidence of something wrong with their specific vendor relationship.

This distinction matters practically because a founder who misattributes Baumol-driven cost growth to vendor inefficiency might switch vendors expecting a better outcome, only to encounter the same underlying economic pattern with the new vendor a year or two later. The useful response isn't vendor-switching in search of an exemption from cost disease that doesn't exist — it's selecting a vendor whose efficiency, relative to the market baseline, is genuinely strong, and planning budgets around realistic Baumol-adjusted expectations rather than an assumption that better vendor selection alone can escape an economy-wide pattern entirely.

### (Scenario: founder confused why software costs rose despite better tools) Why did our software development costs rise even though we're using better tools and AI assistance now?

### (Scenario: CFO building a multi-year software budget) How should I budget for software development costs over a multi-year roadmap?

Assume real cost growth over time rather than decline, grounded in Baumol's cost disease, and treat any efficiency gains from tooling or AI as a partial offset to that growth rather than a reversal of it.
