A software vendor pitching a modern inventory or production management system to a manufacturing company frequently encounters a specific, recognizable resistance pattern: genuine interest in the capability, real acknowledgment that the current system has real limitations, and still, a strong pull toward sticking with the existing, familiar tools a little longer. This isn't unique to any one company or any one vendor's specific pitch — it's a well-documented pattern in how new technology genuinely spreads through an industry over time, and manufacturing sits in a specific, quite predictable place within that pattern.

## Why Manufacturing Consistently Lags Software's General Adoption Curve

Manufacturing and other industrial sectors have historically adopted new software technology later than sectors like finance, media, or consumer tech, and this lag isn't well explained by manufacturing companies being less capable or less interested in improving their operations — it's better explained by structural factors specific to the sector: high switching costs when software controls physical processes with real safety and continuity stakes, a workforce with different technology comfort baselines than a software-native industry, and a genuine, reasonable caution about disrupting a working physical process for a digital improvement whose benefit, while real, is less immediately tangible than the risk of a production line going down.

## The Framework That Predicts and Explains This Pattern

Sociologist Everett Rogers, in his influential 1962 book *Diffusion of Innovations*, developed a model describing how new technologies and ideas spread through a population over time, dividing adopters into categories along a predictable curve: innovators and early adopters who embrace a new technology quickly, an early and late majority who adopt only once the technology's value is well established and de-risked, and laggards who adopt last, often only once the older alternative becomes genuinely unavailable or clearly untenable. Rogers's research identified specific factors that predict where an industry or organization sits on this curve, including the perceived risk of adoption relative to the perceived benefit, and critically, how directly and visibly failure would be felt if the new technology didn't work as expected.

Manufacturing's position toward the later end of Rogers's curve for most software categories follows directly from these factors: a failed software adoption in a media company might mean an inconvenient bug; a failed software adoption controlling a physical production process can mean a stopped line, a safety incident, or a costly physical remediation — a fundamentally higher-stakes failure mode that rationally shifts an entire industry's risk tolerance toward the later-majority and laggard end of Rogers's curve, not because manufacturing leaders are less sophisticated, but because the actual cost-benefit calculation genuinely is different when software failure has physical, not just digital, consequences.

## Why This Matters for How Manufacturing Software Should Actually Be Built and Sold

A vendor treating a manufacturing client's caution as simple resistance to overcome through better sales technique is misreading the situation — Rogers's framework suggests the caution is a rational response to a genuinely different risk profile, not an attitude problem to be persuaded away. The more effective approach treats the adoption curve position as a design and rollout constraint to work with directly: emphasizing incremental, low-risk adoption paths, extensive de-risking through piloting and parallel-running before full cutover, and proof points from genuinely comparable manufacturing peers rather than case studies from lower-stakes industries that don't address the specific risk profile a manufacturing buyer is actually weighing.

## What Building for a Late-Majority Industry Actually Requires

- **Design for incremental, reversible adoption paths**, since a manufacturing buyer's risk tolerance genuinely favors piloting a new system alongside an existing one before a full, irreversible cutover, not a rapid full replacement.
- **Prioritize proof points from genuinely comparable industrial contexts**, since a case study from a lower-stakes software category doesn't address the specific physical-process risk a manufacturing buyer is actually weighing.
- **Build in extensive safety margins and fallback mechanisms**, since the cost of a software failure affecting a physical process is categorically different from a failure in a purely digital context, and the system should be architected with that asymmetry in mind from the start.
- **Respect the caution as rational, not a sales objection to overcome**, since treating a manufacturing buyer's diffusion-curve position as an attitude problem rather than a genuine risk calculation tends to produce exactly the kind of pushback that confirms the buyer's original caution was warranted.

## Why "Laggard" Is a Misleading Label for Rational Risk Management

Rogers's own terminology is worth addressing directly, because "laggard" carries an unfortunate connotation of backwardness or resistance to progress that doesn't actually match what his research found was driving late adoption in high-stakes contexts. Rogers's later work and subsequent research applying his framework specifically distinguished between laggard behavior driven by genuine resource constraints or resistance to change, and late adoption driven by a rational, well-founded risk calculation given a specific context's actual stakes — the latter isn't a failure to keep pace with progress, it's an appropriately calibrated response to a genuinely different cost-benefit equation than the one facing an early-adopting industry.

A manufacturing operations director who insists on a parallel-running pilot before trusting new software with a physical production line isn't behaving like Rogers's stereotypical laggard, resistant to change on principle — they're behaving exactly as a rational actor should when the downside of a failed adoption includes a stopped production line or a safety incident, not just an inconvenient bug. Understanding this distinction is precisely what separates a vendor who works productively with a manufacturing client's caution, as Orobie Meccanica's engagement did, from one who reads the same caution as an obstacle to be overcome through more aggressive persuasion, and predictably encounters more resistance as a direct result of that misreading.

## Case Study: A Bergamo Manufacturer's Incremental Adoption

Orobie Meccanica, a Bergamo-based industrial manufacturer, had been approached by several software vendors over the years proposing a full replacement of its aging production tracking system, each pitch emphasizing the new system's superior capability while offering little concrete de-risking beyond general reassurance — proposals the company had consistently declined despite acknowledging the existing system's real limitations.

Manifera's Amsterdam team proposed a structurally different rollout: a parallel-running pilot on a single, lower-risk production line for three months, with the legacy system remaining the system of record throughout, before any broader cutover was even discussed. The pilot's concrete, comparable results — from Orobie's own production line, not an external case study — gave the company's leadership genuine, situation-specific evidence to evaluate, rather than a general sales pitch asking them to accept the vendor's risk assessment on faith.

> *"Every previous pitch asked us to trust that it would work. This one let us actually watch it work, on our own floor, with the old system still running underneath it as a safety net, before asking us to commit to anything bigger."*
> — **Operations Director, Orobie Meccanica**

Orobie Meccanica has since expanded the system to two additional production lines, each following the same incremental, parallel-running pattern the initial pilot established, rather than a full-scale cutover following the first line's success — a pace the operations director explicitly describes as rational risk management, not reluctance to modernize.

## Where Different Industries Sit on the Adoption Curve

| Industry Type | Typical Adoption Position | Primary Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Software-native (media, fintech) | Early adopter / early majority | Lower failure cost, digital-only stakes |
| Professional services | Early to late majority | Moderate risk, workflow disruption cost |
| Manufacturing / industrial | Late majority / laggard | High failure cost, physical process stakes |
| Highly regulated industrial (aerospace, pharma) | Laggard | Regulatory and safety-critical stakes |
