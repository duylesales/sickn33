A software company with two hundred five-star reviews looks safer than one with twelve — until you notice that review volume mostly measures how systematically a company asks for reviews, not how well its projects actually went. Reading reviews critically means looking past the star rating entirely and reading what the text actually says, or doesn't.

## Why Star Rating and Review Count Are Weak Signals Alone

Many agencies build review solicitation into their process as a matter of course, asking every client for a review regardless of how the project actually went, often right after a positive kickoff moment rather than after delivery reality has had time to surface. This produces high review counts that reflect follow-up discipline more than delivered outcomes — a company that's genuinely mediocre but systematic about review requests can out-rate a genuinely excellent company that simply doesn't ask as consistently.

## What to Actually Look For in Review Text

- **Specificity.** A review mentioning a specific project type, timeline, and concrete outcome is more informative than "great to work with, highly recommend" — vague praise is easy to write about a project you barely remember the details of.
- **Reviews that mention problems and how they were handled.** A review acknowledging a mid-project challenge and how the company responded to it is more credible and more useful than a uniformly glowing account, since real projects rarely go perfectly.
- **Recency and consistency over time.** A cluster of five-star reviews from three years ago followed by silence, or a recent shift toward more mixed reviews, can indicate the company's quality or team has changed since the reviews that built its reputation.
- **Reviewer profile plausibility.** Reviews from verifiable, findable people or companies (LinkedIn profiles, real company names) carry more weight than anonymous or unverifiable reviewer identities.

## What Reviews Structurally Can't Tell You

Reviews are self-selected — clients with strong opinions in either direction are more likely to leave one than clients with a merely adequate experience, skewing the visible sample toward extremes. They also can't tell you whether a reviewer's project matched your specific complexity, industry, or requirements, which matters more to predicting your own outcome than the aggregate rating does.

## Why Volume Persuades Even When It Shouldn't

Psychologist Robert Cialdini's research on influence, most widely known through his 1984 book *Influence*, identified social proof as one of the core principles by which people decide how to act under uncertainty: when we're unsure of the right course of action, we look at what other people, especially people similar to us, are doing, and we treat that collective behavior as evidence about what's correct. Cialdini's experiments demonstrated this mechanism across a striking range of contexts — from hotel towel-reuse signage that shifted behavior most effectively when it cited what other guests in that specific room had actually done, to laugh tracks measurably increasing how funny audiences rated a sitcom, even when viewers consciously knew the laughter was artificially added rather than a genuine audience reaction.

The persuasive power of two hundred five-star reviews operates through exactly this mechanism, and understanding the mechanism is what lets a founder resist it deliberately. A large review count doesn't just claim quality directly — it activates the same "if this many other people approved, it must be right" heuristic Cialdini documented, largely independent of whether the underlying reviews are individually meaningful, specific, or even genuine. This is precisely why the heuristic misfires so easily in this specific context: review count is trivially gameable through systematic solicitation, in a way that a genuinely difficult-to-fake signal, like a candid reference call, structurally is not. Social proof evolved as a reasonable shortcut for situations where independently verifying an answer is expensive; a founder evaluating a vendor almost always can verify independently, through reference calls and reading review text closely, which is exactly why leaning on the shortcut here is a mistake worth consciously correcting for.

## Case Study: A Wiesbaden Founder's Review Deep-Dive

A founder at Wiesbaden-based startup Klarpuls initially favored a vendor with over 150 five-star reviews over one with 20, until reading the larger set closely revealed a pattern: nearly all were short, generic, and dated within the same three-month window two years earlier, with almost no reviews since — a pattern suggesting an aggressive early review campaign rather than sustained quality.

The smaller review set, by contrast, included several detailed accounts spanning different years, including one describing a mid-project scope challenge and how it was resolved. The founder chose the second vendor after a reference call confirmed the detail in those reviews, and the resulting engagement matched what the reviews had described.

> *"The bigger number felt safer at first glance. Reading the actual text instead of just the star count told a very different story."*
> — **Founder, Klarpuls**

Klarpuls now applies the same read-the-actual-text approach to evaluating its own suppliers more broadly, treating review count as a secondary signal behind specificity and recency across every vendor category, not just software development.

## Deliberately Overriding the Social Proof Shortcut

The founder describes the correction as consciously procedural rather than purely instinctive: before weighting any vendor's review profile, the team now explicitly asks whether the number in front of them is doing the actual persuading, separate from anything the review text itself demonstrates. Naming the heuristic out loud, in Klarpuls's experience, made it noticeably easier to resist in the moment a large number would otherwise have quietly done the deciding on its own.

## A Practical Test for Whether a Number Is Doing the Persuading

A useful check a founder can run on any impressive review count: cover the number itself and read only ten reviews chosen at random, ignoring the ones featured most prominently at the top. If those ten, read cold without the anchoring effect of the total count, would independently convince a skeptical reader of genuine quality, the volume behind them is probably real signal. If the ten read as generic, interchangeable, or oddly clustered in time once the total is hidden, the large number was very likely doing most of the persuasive work on its own, exactly the pattern Cialdini's research would predict, and exactly the pattern a specific, verifiable reference call is structurally immune to in a way an aggregated review count never can be.

This test generalizes well beyond software vendor selection, which is part of why naming the mechanism explicitly is useful for a founder beyond this one decision: any purchase or hire evaluated partly by "how many other people chose this" benefits from briefly separating the count from the content and asking which one is actually doing the convincing. A founder who makes this separation a habit, not just a one-time exercise for a single vendor decision, ends up systematically harder to influence by volume alone across every domain where volume is cheap to manufacture and substance is not — which, as review-solicitation tooling becomes more automated and more common industry-wide, is an increasingly large share of the vendor decisions a growing company has to make.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder comparing vendors with very different review counts) Should I always prefer a vendor with more reviews?

Not automatically — review count often reflects how systematically a company solicits reviews rather than how well projects go. Read the actual content of reviews rather than weighting the count heavily on its own.

### (Scenario: founder trying to spot a low-quality but high-review-count vendor) What's a warning sign that review volume doesn't reflect genuine quality?

A large cluster of reviews concentrated in a narrow time window, followed by long silence, along with generic, non-specific praise across most reviews, both suggest a solicitation campaign rather than organically sustained satisfaction.

### (Scenario: founder unsure how much weight to give a single negative review) Should one negative review disqualify an otherwise well-reviewed vendor?

Not automatically — read it for specificity and how the company responded, if a response is visible. A single detailed, specific negative review sometimes reveals more useful information than dismissing it outright would.

### (Scenario: founder trying to verify review authenticity) How can I tell if reviews are genuine versus possibly fabricated?

Look for reviewer profiles that are verifiable — real names, findable LinkedIn profiles or company affiliations — and be more skeptical of anonymous or unverifiable reviewer identities, especially in unusually large clusters.

### (Scenario: founder trying to combine reviews with other due diligence) How should reviews fit into a broader vendor evaluation process?

Use them as one input alongside portfolio review, direct reference calls, and technical Q&A — reviews alone, regardless of star rating or count, shouldn't be the deciding factor for a significant engagement.
