A proposal listing "a team of five developers" could genuinely mean five people working full-time and exclusively on your project alone, or it could just as easily mean five people spread thinly across your project and three others simultaneously, with your actual daily attention closer to a single person's worth of real capacity. Both descriptions remain technically accurate uses of the phrase "a team of developers." Only one of them actually delivers what a founder usually and reasonably assumes when first reading that phrase in a proposal.

## Why "Team of Developers" Is a Phrase That Hides More Than It Reveals

The phrase describes only a raw headcount claim, without specifying allocation, seniority mix, or actual exclusivity — three genuinely distinct factors that ultimately determine whether that team actually delivers the real capacity a founder is budgeting for. A team of five junior developers each spending 20% of their time on your project delivers roughly one full-time-equivalent of junior-level output, a meaningfully different reality than five senior developers working exclusively and full-time, even though both could be described identically as "a team of five developers" in a sales conversation.

## The Specific Questions That Reveal What "Team" Actually Means

- **"What percentage of each person's time is specifically allocated to my project?"** — directly revealing whether the team is genuinely dedicated or actually shared across multiple concurrent client engagements simultaneously.
- **"What's the seniority mix of this team?"** — a team of five that's mostly junior with one senior lead delivers different output than a team of five that's mostly senior, even at identical headcount.
- **"Will this exact same team stay consistent all the way through the project, or does composition change over time?"** — team churn mid-project reliably reintroduces real onboarding cost that a genuinely stable team avoids entirely from the outset.
- **"What's the effective full-time-equivalent capacity I'm actually getting?"** — converting allocation percentages into a real FTE number gives a founder a directly comparable figure across different proposals, rather than comparing headcounts that mean different things.

## Why This Matters More Than the Headline Headcount Number

Two proposals both claiming "a team of five" can represent genuinely wildly different actual capacity and real cost-efficiency once allocation and seniority are properly accounted for — a founder comparing headline headcount numbers alone, without probing these specifics directly, is effectively comparing numbers that aren't actually comparable at all, which can easily lead to choosing a nominally larger but practically weaker team over a nominally smaller but genuinely far more capable one.

## The Economic Term for What's Actually Happening Here

Economists describe this exact dynamic — where one party's actions are difficult for the other party to observe or verify after an agreement is made — as moral hazard, a concept with roots in insurance economics and developed extensively within the broader information economics literature that also produced principal-agent theory. Moral hazard describes situations where an agreement changes one party's incentives in ways the other party can't fully monitor: once a contract is signed, the vendor has private information about actual time allocation that the client structurally cannot observe directly, and that information asymmetry creates room for the vendor's stated commitment and its actual delivered reality to quietly diverge without the client having an easy way to detect it in the moment.

This isn't necessarily deliberate deception — a vendor genuinely committed to "a team of five" at signing may face internal pressure later to stretch that same team across a newly won client, and the client paying for the original commitment has no direct visibility into that reallocation happening. Moral hazard theory's standard remedy is exactly what this article's four questions accomplish: converting an unobservable, unenforceable commitment (general team availability) into specific, contractually explicit terms (exact allocation percentages, named individuals, stability guarantees) that are actually verifiable after the fact, closing the information gap that made the original vague commitment risky to rely on in the first place.

## Case Study: A Toulouse Founder's Proposal Comparison

A founder at Toulouse-based startup Occivol compared two proposals, both describing "a dedicated team of four developers" at similar headline pricing. Direct questioning revealed one vendor's "team of four" meant four developers each allocated 50% to the project (effectively two FTEs), while the other's meant four developers fully dedicated (four FTEs) at a comparable total price.

The founder chose the second vendor after this clarification, since the effective cost-per-FTE was significantly better once the actual allocation was accounted for — information the headline "team of four" language from both proposals hadn't revealed on its own.

> *"Both proposals said the exact same words. Only one of them meant what I'd assumed those words meant."*
> — **Founder, Occivol**

The founder now writes exact allocation percentages directly into every vendor contract as a specific, checkable term, rather than trusting the proposal language alone, a direct application of closing the moral hazard gap through explicit, monitorable commitments instead of general assurances.

## Monitoring Doesn't End at Signing

Converting a vague commitment into a specific contractual term addresses moral hazard at the point of agreement, but the underlying incentive problem doesn't fully disappear once the ink is dry — a vendor under pressure from a newly won client can still, in principle, quietly under-deliver against a specific allocation commitment just as easily as against a vague one, unless the client has some ongoing way to verify actual delivered capacity over time. Regular, specific progress reporting tied to the committed allocation — not just general status updates — gives a client an ongoing check against the same information asymmetry the initial contract term was designed to close, rather than treating the signed commitment as self-enforcing on its own.

## Why Reputation Alone Doesn't Fully Solve the Problem

A natural objection to worrying about moral hazard here is that a vendor's reputation should discourage this kind of quiet under-delivery — a company caught stretching a committed team too thin risks losing future business and damaging references that matter to its pipeline. Reputation genuinely does discipline behavior, but it's an imperfect solution precisely because the same information asymmetry that creates the moral hazard problem also delays when reputational consequences arrive: a client typically only discovers under-allocation well into a project, once delays or quality issues have already surfaced, by which point real cost has already been incurred regardless of whatever reputational consequence eventually follows for the vendor.

This is why specific contractual terms and reputation work best as complements rather than substitutes for one another — a written allocation commitment gives a client recourse and an early, concrete basis for raising a concern the moment delivered capacity doesn't match what was promised, rather than waiting for project outcomes to eventually reveal a problem that a vague commitment made much harder to catch, name, and act on early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder comparing two proposals with identical headcount claims) How do I compare two proposals that both claim "a team of X developers" at similar pricing?

Ask both vendors for exact allocation percentages, seniority mix, and team stability commitments, then convert to an effective FTE figure for a true comparison, rather than comparing the headline headcount numbers directly.

### (Scenario: founder trying to understand allocation percentages) What does it mean if a developer is "50% allocated" to my project?

It means that person splits their working time between your project and at least one other client engagement, delivering roughly half the throughput of a fully dedicated team member, while still being described as part of "your team."

### (Scenario: founder worried about team consistency) Why does team stability matter if the headcount and skill level stay the same?

Because a new team member, even at equivalent skill level, needs onboarding time to understand your project's specific architecture and decisions — team churn reintroduces that onboarding cost repeatedly, reducing effective productivity even if raw headcount is unchanged.

### (Scenario: CTO trying to negotiate better proposal terms) Should I ask for a lower price if a team is only partially allocated to my project?

That's a reasonable negotiating position — pricing that assumes full dedication when the actual allocation is partial should be adjusted, or you should request full dedication if the project genuinely requires it.

### (Scenario: founder trying to build a standard question into future vendor conversations) What's the single best question to ask about any proposed "team of developers"?

"What's the effective full-time-equivalent capacity I'm actually getting, once allocation percentage and seniority mix are accounted for?" — this single question surfaces most of the information hidden behind a simple headcount claim.
