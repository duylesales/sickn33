Two competing proposals arrive: one at $35 an hour, one at $65. A founder comparing purely on that single number picks the cheaper rate, does the quick arithmetic on estimated hours, and reasonably expects to save real money on the total project. What that simple comparison leaves out entirely is that hourly rate and total project cost aren't actually the same thing at all, and the real relationship between them is considerably less predictable than the initial back-of-envelope math ever suggests.

## Why Hourly Rate Is an Incomplete Number

Total project cost is hourly rate multiplied by actual hours required — and the second variable is exactly where a lower rate can quietly stop being a bargain. A less experienced or less disciplined developer at a lower rate can easily take meaningfully more hours to complete the same scope than a more experienced developer at a higher rate, either through slower raw output, more rework from mistakes caught late, or simply less efficient problem-solving on unfamiliar territory. When hours scale up enough to offset the rate difference, the "cheaper" option costs the same or more in total — and that's before accounting for the cost of bugs, rework, and delays that a rushed or under-skilled team is statistically more likely to produce.

## The Economic Theory That Explains Why Paying More Sometimes Costs Less

Economists George Akerlof and Janet Yellen, building on earlier work in labor economics through the 1980s, formalized efficiency wage theory, which argues that employers sometimes rationally pay above the minimum market-clearing wage because higher pay increases worker productivity, effort, and retention enough to more than offset the higher direct cost — through reduced turnover, stronger effort under limited direct supervision, and the ability to attract and retain more capable workers who have better outside options and wouldn't accept the lower rate at all. The theory's core insight is that wage and productivity aren't independent variables to be optimized separately — paying more can causally increase output per hour, not just attract people who were already going to be more productive regardless.

Applied to vendor selection, efficiency wage theory offers a precise explanation for why a higher hourly rate sometimes produces a lower total project cost: a more experienced, well-compensated developer typically works with meaningfully fewer hours of rework, catches more problems before they compound, and requires less oversight to produce a working result — all effects the theory predicts should scale with compensation, up to a point. A founder comparing two proposals purely on hourly rate is implicitly assuming hours-to-completion is constant across both options, when efficiency wage theory, along with substantial real-world project experience, suggests that assumption is frequently false, and false in a specific, cost-relevant direction.

## Why This Doesn't Mean "Always Choose the Most Expensive Option"

Efficiency wage theory doesn't argue that higher pay always produces proportionally higher output — beyond a certain point, additional compensation stops buying meaningfully more productivity, and an inflated rate with no corresponding skill or efficiency difference is simply a worse deal, not a better one. The useful takeaway isn't "pay more automatically" — it's that hourly rate alone is an incomplete basis for comparison, and a founder needs additional information — the vendor's actual track record, typical project timelines relative to scope, and evidence of efficient delivery — to judge whether a given rate difference reflects a genuine productivity difference or just a pricing difference with no corresponding capability behind it.

## What a More Complete Comparison Actually Requires

- **Ask for typical hours-to-completion for comparably scoped past projects**, not just the hourly rate, since this is the actual number that determines total cost, and it varies meaningfully between vendors even at similar rates.
- **Weigh rework and defect rate as a real cost factor**, since a cheaper hourly rate that produces more post-launch bugs shifts cost forward into maintenance and lost user trust rather than eliminating it.
- **Evaluate the specific track record behind a given rate**, since a lower rate backed by genuine efficiency and specific relevant experience is a different proposition than a lower rate with no evidence behind it at all.
- **Calculate a realistic total cost estimate for each proposal**, not just the headline hourly figure, using the vendor's own track record data as the basis for the hours estimate rather than accepting either party's optimistic assumption uncritically.

## Why the Effect Is Hardest to See From the Buyer's Side

A specific reason efficiency wage dynamics are so easy for a founder to underestimate when comparing vendors: the productivity difference the theory predicts is largely invisible at the point of comparison, before any work has actually started. A proposal document looks equally confident regardless of the actual hours-to-completion behind it, and a founder has no direct way to observe the efficiency difference the theory describes until the project is already underway and the hours are already accumulating. This is structurally different from a straightforward price comparison for a physical good, where quality differences are often at least partially visible or testable before purchase — a software vendor's efficiency is almost entirely invisible until real work reveals it, which is exactly why asking for concrete, verifiable past-project data matters more here than it would for a more directly comparable purchase.

This invisibility problem is also why reference calls specifically focused on delivery efficiency — not just general satisfaction — are unusually valuable in this specific context. A past client can speak directly to whether a vendor's actual hours matched their estimates, which is precisely the information efficiency wage theory identifies as the real determinant of total cost, and precisely the information a proposal document, however professionally formatted, cannot reliably convey on its own.
