A sales call, left to run its own course, is designed to answer the questions a vendor wants to be asked. The questions that actually predict how a project will go are the ones a founder has to bring themselves, asked the same specific way of every vendor in the running — because no pitch deck volunteers "here's how we've disappointed past clients" without being asked directly and specifically.

## 1. Who Specifically Will Be Assigned to My Project?

Ask for names and seniority levels, not job titles. "A senior engineer" could mean two years of experience at some firms and ten at others — get the actual person's background before assuming.

## 2. Is This Team Dedicated to Me, or Split Across Other Clients?

A shared team means your urgent issue competes with someone else's urgent issue. A dedicated team means your priorities are the only priorities in that engineer's day.

## 3. What Does Your Discovery Process Actually Look Like?

If the answer is "we'll get started right away," that's a red flag disguised as enthusiasm. Real discovery — stakeholder interviews, requirements documentation, integration scoping — takes time, and skipping it is how estimates go wrong.

## 4. How Do You Handle a Change in Requirements Mid-Project?

Get the change-order pricing and process in writing before you need it, not after a change request is already on the table and both sides have an incentive to argue their preferred interpretation. A vague answer here becomes a contentious negotiation later, at the worst possible moment.

## 5. Can I Speak Directly With the Engineers, Not Just the Account Manager?

A firm confident in its technical bench will make this easy, without deflecting the request or pushing it to "later in the process." Resistance to a technical Q&A before signing is a preview of how accessible the actual delivery team will be after signing.

## 6. What Happens If a Key Engineer Leaves Mid-Project?

Ask specifically about redundancy and knowledge transfer practices. A firm with no answer here is one bad resignation away from your project stalling.

## 7. Can You Share a Reference From a Project Similar in Complexity to Mine?

Not just any happy client — one who dealt with a similar integration count, compliance profile, or technical challenge. A general reference tells you less than a complexity-matched one.

## 8. What's Your Typical Communication Cadence?

Daily standups, weekly demos, async written updates — get the specifics. "We communicate regularly" means something different to every firm.

## 9. How Do You Approach QA and Testing?

Ask what's included by default versus billed separately. QA is one of the most commonly under-scoped line items in software development quotes.

## 10. What Happens After Launch?

Get clarity on the post-launch stabilization window, ongoing maintenance terms, and what's covered versus billed as new work. Many disputes happen in the ambiguous weeks right after go-live.

## Why a Structured List Beats an Open-Ended Conversation

The idea that a fixed, structured set of questions produces better decisions than an open-ended conversation isn't specific to vendor selection — it's one of the more robust findings in personnel selection research. Frank Schmidt and John Hunter's meta-analytic work on employment selection methods, among the most cited research in industrial-organizational psychology, found that structured interviews — a consistent set of questions asked the same way of every candidate — predict real-world performance substantially better than unstructured, free-flowing conversations, even when the unstructured conversation feels more natural and informative to the person conducting it. The reason is a well-documented one: unstructured conversations let a decision-maker's existing impressions — how likeable someone seems, how confident they sound — dominate the judgment, while a structured format forces comparable information out of every candidate regardless of how the conversation happens to flow.

A vendor discovery call has the exact same structural vulnerability. A skilled salesperson can guide an open-ended conversation toward their strengths and away from their weaknesses without ever technically lying, simply by controlling which topics get airtime — which is precisely why a founder walking in with a fixed list, asked in the same form regardless of how charming or reassuring the conversation feels, produces meaningfully more comparable and more predictive information across multiple vendors than letting each call run wherever the vendor steers it.

This is also why the specific wording of these ten questions matters more than it might seem to. A vague version of question 6 — "what if someone leaves?" — invites a vague, reassuring non-answer. The more specific version — asking directly about redundancy and knowledge-transfer practices — closes off the easy, comfortable non-answer and forces something checkable. Structured selection research consistently finds that specificity in the question is what produces specificity in the answer; a loosely worded question, however well-intentioned, tends to get a loosely worded response back.

## Scoring Answers Instead of Just Collecting Them

Structured selection research offers one more useful practice beyond the fixed question list itself: scoring each answer against a defined rubric immediately after the call, rather than relying on overall impression once the conversation has ended. A general sense of "that call went well," formed after the fact rather than during the call itself, is exactly the kind of unstructured, halo-effect-prone judgment structured interviewing is designed to correct for — a charismatic account manager can leave a founder with a warm overall impression even when three of the ten specific answers were genuinely weak, simply because the strong answers are what gets remembered afterward.

A simple three-point scale per question — vague, adequate, specific — applied consistently across every vendor call turns a set of separate, hard-to-compare conversations into a genuinely comparable dataset. This is a small amount of extra discipline for a large amount of extra clarity, particularly once a founder is three or four vendor calls deep and the specifics of an early conversation have started to blur against the impression left by a more recent, more polished one.

## What a Strong vs. Weak Answer Sounds Like

| Question | Weak Answer | Strong Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Who's assigned? | "Our best people" | Named engineers, specific seniority |
| Dedicated or shared? | "We'll prioritize you" | Explicitly dedicated pod |
| Discovery process? | "We'll jump right in" | Documented multi-week process |
| Engineer departure? | "That won't happen" | Named redundancy and documentation practice |
| Post-launch? | "We'll figure it out" | Defined stabilization window in writing |

A firm worth working with expects this level of scrutiny for a significant engagement. Discomfort with direct questions before signing is itself useful information about how the relationship will go afterward.

### (Scenario: founder with limited time for vendor calls) Which of these ten questions matters most if I can only ask a few?

Who's specifically assigned, whether the team is dedicated, and how change requests are handled are the three that predict the most common sources of project trouble.

### (Scenario: founder unsure how to verify an answer's accuracy) How do I know if a vendor's answers to these questions are actually true?

Cross-check against the reference call from question 7 — ask that reference specifically whether the vendor's stated process, communication cadence, and team stability matched what was promised.

### (Scenario: founder deciding whether a vague answer is disqualifying) Is one vague answer among the ten enough to walk away?

Not automatically, but a pattern of two or more vague answers, especially on questions 3, 4, and 6, is a meaningful risk signal worth weighing heavily against the rest of the proposal.

### (Scenario: founder preparing for their first vendor call) Should I send these questions in advance or ask them live?

Asking live, without advance notice, often produces more honest answers — a vendor's ability to respond confidently and specifically without a prepared script is itself part of what you're evaluating.
