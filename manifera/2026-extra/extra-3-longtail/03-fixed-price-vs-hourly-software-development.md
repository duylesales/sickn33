A fixed-price contract feels safer to a founder who has never run a software project, and a time-and-materials contract feels safer to one who has. Both instincts are correct — they're just answering different questions about who eats the cost when requirements change, which they always do, and neither contract structure makes that underlying uncertainty disappear on its own.

## The Question Neither Contract Actually Answers

Founders comparing fixed price to time and materials usually ask "which one is cheaper," which is the wrong question, because neither structure changes the underlying engineering cost of the work — it only changes who absorbs the cost of scope changes and estimation error. Fixed price shifts that risk to the vendor, who prices in a buffer to cover it. Time and materials shifts it to the client, who pays exactly for what happens, uncertainty included.

**Fixed price** means one number for a defined scope. The vendor eats the cost if the estimate was wrong, which is exactly why every fixed-price quote includes a risk buffer — typically 15-30% above the vendor's honest internal estimate — priced in specifically because scope discussions after signing are expensive to litigate.

**Time and materials (T&M)** means paying for actual hours worked against an evolving scope. No buffer is priced in because the client absorbs estimation risk directly, which means T&M projects can come in below a fixed-price equivalent if requirements stay stable, or meaningfully above it if they don't.

## When Each Structure Actually Fits

Fixed price fits genuinely well-defined projects: a known feature set, minimal external dependencies, and a client who won't need to pivot mid-build. An MVP with a locked spec, a website redesign, a defined integration — these are fixed-price-friendly because the vendor can estimate confidently and the buffer stays small.

T&M fits projects where the requirements will legitimately evolve: a product still finding its market, a platform being built alongside ongoing user research, or work where "build the right thing" matters more than "build the pre-specified thing." Forcing an evolving project into a fixed-price contract doesn't remove the uncertainty — it just relocates it into change-order negotiations, which are slower and more adversarial than simply paying for the hours the pivot actually takes.

## The Hybrid That Most Serious Vendors Actually Run

Fixed-price-only and pure T&M are both edge cases. Most well-run projects use a hybrid: fixed price for a clearly scoped discovery and design phase, then T&M or milestone-based fixed price for the build phase once requirements are concrete enough to estimate with real confidence. This structure prices the genuinely uncertain part of the project (early discovery) the way uncertain work should be priced, and the genuinely predictable part (a scoped build) the way predictable work should be priced.

## The Economic Theory Behind Why Buffers Exist at All

Fixed-price buffers aren't an arbitrary markup — they're a predictable consequence of what economists call the principal-agent problem, a concept with a long history in contract theory going back to foundational work by economists like Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz on information asymmetry, later recognized with a Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. The core insight applies directly to software vendor relationships: the party taking on risk (the vendor, under fixed price) will price that risk into the contract precisely because they can't fully observe or control the client's future behavior — whether requirements will actually stay stable, whether stakeholders will agree internally, whether "one small change" will really stay small.

This is also why a vendor's fixed-price buffer isn't something a client can simply negotiate away without changing the underlying risk allocation. Asking a vendor to cut their buffer while keeping full fixed-price risk transfer is asking them to accept a worse-than-fair price for the same risk — a request that either gets refused, or gets accepted and then recouped later through aggressive change-order pricing, which produces exactly the adversarial dynamic fixed-price contracts are supposed to prevent. The honest lever isn't the buffer size; it's how much genuine uncertainty exists in the contract to begin with, which is precisely what a discovery phase is designed to reduce before the fixed-price number is set.

Time and materials contracts sidestep this dynamic entirely by not transferring the risk in the first place — there's no buffer to negotiate because the vendor isn't pricing in uncertainty they don't control. The trade-off, as in most principal-agent structures, is that the party bearing the risk (now the client) needs enough visibility into the work to trust that hours billed reflect hours genuinely needed, which is why well-run T&M engagements pair the billing model with transparent sprint reporting rather than an opaque monthly invoice.

## Fixed Price vs. Time and Materials

| Factor | Fixed Price | Time and Materials |
|---|---|---|
| Who absorbs estimation risk | Vendor (priced into a buffer) | Client (paid as it happens) |
| Best fit | Locked, well-defined scope | Evolving product, active discovery |
| Cost if requirements stay stable | Slightly higher (buffer included) | Slightly lower |
| Cost if requirements change significantly | Change-order negotiation, often contentious | Naturally absorbed, no renegotiation |
| Budget predictability | High, until scope changes | Requires active budget monitoring |

## Structuring Milestones So They Actually Protect Both Sides

A milestone badly defined does almost nothing to solve the problem fixed price is supposed to solve, regardless of how carefully the rest of the contract was negotiated. "Milestone 1: Backend complete" is a recipe for exactly the dispute milestone pricing exists to prevent, because "complete" is a matter of interpretation the client and vendor can reasonably disagree about after the fact. A milestone worth paying against needs an explicit, checkable definition of done — specific functionality that either works against a written acceptance test or doesn't, not a general sense that a phase of work has wrapped up.

This matters more than it sounds like it should, because the entire value of milestone-based fixed pricing collapses back into T&M-style ambiguity the moment a milestone's definition is vague enough to argue about. The discipline required to write genuinely checkable milestones is, not coincidentally, close to the same discipline required to run good discovery in the first place — which is why the two tend to travel together in vendors who do either one well, and why a vendor unwilling to write specific milestone acceptance criteria is often also the vendor who skipped real discovery to quote quickly.

## Choosing the Right Structure for Your Project

If your scope is genuinely locked, fixed price protects your budget. If your product is still evolving, T&M — or better, a discovery phase followed by milestone-based fixed pricing — will save you from paying a large uncertainty buffer for certainty you don't actually have yet. The contract structure question and the discovery-quality question are really the same question asked from two different angles, and getting the second one right tends to resolve the first almost automatically. ## The Break-Even Math: When T&M Actually Beats a Fixed-Price Buffer

The buffer size in a fixed-price quote isn't fixed at all — it scales directly with how much discovery was done before the number was set. A software development company quoting off a one-page feature list typically prices a 25-35% buffer into a bespoke software development services contract, because that's the only way to price the risk of a spec that's still going to move. The same vendor quoting after two weeks of real discovery — wireframes, data model, integration list agreed — can usually drop that buffer to 8-15%, because most of the uncertainty a buffer exists to cover has already been resolved.

That gap is the number that actually decides fixed price vs. T&M for your project. On a €120,000 build, a 30% pre-discovery buffer is €36,000 of pure risk premium; a 12% post-discovery buffer is €14,400 for the identical scope. If your realistic scope-change rate is below the buffer percentage a vendor is quoting, you're overpaying under fixed price. If it's above, fixed price is protecting you from a cost you'd otherwise absorb directly under T&M.

Blended hourly rates matter here too: a Western-European-only vendor typically bills €75-120/hour, while a Netherlands-governed, Vietnam-executed pod blends to roughly €35-55/hour for the same seniority mix — which compresses the absolute euro size of any buffer, fixed-price or T&M, regardless of the percentage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder unsure which contract type to request) Which is generally cheaper, fixed price or time and materials?

Neither is inherently cheaper — it depends on how much your requirements change. T&M tends to cost less if scope stays stable, but fixed price protects you from cost overruns if scope shifts significantly.

### (Scenario: founder worried about a vendor's fixed-price buffer) Why do fixed-price quotes always seem to include extra margin?

Because the vendor is pricing in the risk of your requirements changing or the estimate being wrong. That buffer is the cost of the vendor absorbing uncertainty instead of you.

### (Scenario: founder deciding how to structure a new project) Can I switch from time and materials to fixed price partway through a project?

Yes, and it's often the smartest sequence: run early discovery as T&M while requirements are still forming, then convert to fixed-price milestones once the scope is concrete enough to estimate confidently.

### (Scenario: founder trying to avoid change-order disputes) How do I avoid change-order fights on a fixed-price contract?

Make sure the fixed price is set after a real discovery phase, not before one. Most change-order disputes happen because the original scope was estimated from a feature list rather than from real requirements analysis.

### (Scenario: founder evaluating a milestone-based proposal) What does milestone-based fixed pricing actually mean in practice?

The project is broken into discrete deliverables, each with its own fixed price agreed after that phase's requirements are scoped — giving you fixed-price budget certainty without pricing the entire uncertain project as one large buffer upfront.

### (Scenario: founder comparing quotes from multiple software development companies) Why do two fixed-price quotes for the exact same spec come in 30% apart?

The gap almost always reflects how much discovery each vendor actually did before pricing, not a difference in honesty. A vendor quoting off your feature list alone is pricing a 25-35% buffer for unresolved uncertainty; a vendor who ran real discovery first can quote a much smaller buffer because there's less unknown left to price.

### (Scenario: founder negotiating a bespoke software development services contract) Should I ask a vendor to break out their buffer percentage separately in the quote?

Yes — a vendor confident in their scoping will tell you the buffer number directly, and it's a fast way to compare quotes on equal terms rather than guessing why one custom software development company's total is higher than another's for the same spec.

### (Scenario: founder worried about hidden costs in T&M billing) How do I audit whether T&M hours billed actually match hours worked?

Insist on sprint-level reporting tied to specific deliverables, not a single monthly total. A vendor billing transparently can show you which ticket or feature each week's hours went to; one who can't is the reason T&M gets a reputation for opacity it doesn't have to earn.

### (Scenario: startup founder budgeting runway against a fixed-price quote) How much contingency should I hold in reserve on top of a fixed-price quote?

Even with fixed price, hold 10-15% of the contract value in reserve for scope additions you'll inevitably want once you see the product taking shape — the contract protects you from the vendor's estimation risk, not from your own mind changing.
