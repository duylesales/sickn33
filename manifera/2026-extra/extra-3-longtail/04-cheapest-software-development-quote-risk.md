**Myth:** the cheapest software development company quote is the most cost-efficient choice, and a competitive multi-vendor process naturally surfaces it.

**Fact:** the cheapest quote is usually the least scoped one, and the gap between what it quotes and what it eventually costs gets paid anyway — just later, with less leverage, and after the relationship has already soured.

## Myth #1: A Lower Number Means the Vendor Is More Efficient ❌

**Fact ✅:** A lower number more often means the vendor scoped less, not that they'll deliver the same scope for less money, and the format of a competitive multi-vendor process makes this outcome more likely, not less. Two vendors bidding on the same brief aren't necessarily disagreeing about efficiency — they're frequently just disagreeing about what's actually included, and the founder comparing "final numbers" has no way to see that from the proposal alone.

The tell is specificity. A quote that names design hours, QA hours, and a post-launch stabilization window separately is harder to lowball, because every line item is a promise that can be checked against delivery. A quote that gives one number for "full app development" is easy to make small, because nothing in it can be individually verified as missing until the project is already underway, at which point the missing pieces surface as change orders rather than as a conversation that could have happened before signing.

## Myth #2: If the Cheap Vendor Underdelivers, You Can Just Switch ❌

**Fact ✅:** Switching vendors mid-project is dramatically more expensive than paying more upfront, because the new team has to spend weeks reverse-engineering undocumented decisions the previous team made before they can write a single new line of code productively. Projects that change vendors mid-build routinely lose 20-40% of the already-spent budget to this re-onboarding cost, on top of whatever the second vendor charges to finish the work.

The cheapest quote's real cost isn't visible in the contract — it's visible in the second contract, the one signed six months later with a different company, to finish what the first one couldn't.

## Myth #3: Price Competition Between Vendors Protects You ❌

**Fact ✅:** Price competition genuinely protects you from overpaying for a defined, well-understood scope. It does nothing to protect you from an underdefined scope, because vendors competing on price have every incentive to quote the smallest defensible number, not the most accurate one. The founder isn't choosing between five accurate estimates of the same project — they're choosing between five different guesses about how much can be left out of the proposal without losing the deal.

## What Actually Predicts a Reliable Quote

- **Line-item specificity** — design, build, QA, and post-launch broken out separately, not bundled into one figure.
- **A discovery phase before the number** — a vendor that quotes without asking questions first is guessing, regardless of how confident the number sounds.
- **References that match your project's complexity** — not just any past client, but one who built something with a similar integration count and compliance profile.
- **A written change-order policy** — vendors who plan to handle scope changes well disclose the process before you need it, not after.

## The Auction Theory Problem Hiding Inside Every Multi-Vendor Bid

Running a multi-vendor pitch and picking the lowest number is, structurally, an auction — and auction theory has a well-known name for exactly the outcome that tends to follow: the winner's curse. The term originated in petroleum industry research in the early 1970s, describing how the winning bidder in a competitive auction for uncertain-value assets — oil leases, in the original studies — tends to be the bidder who most overestimated the asset's value, not the one who valued it most accurately. The same mechanism applies directly to a fixed-price software RFP with an uncertain scope: the winning quote is disproportionately likely to be the one that most underestimated the actual work, not the one that priced it most realistically.

This isn't a claim that every low bidder is acting in bad faith. Among five vendors independently estimating an ambiguous project, normal variation in judgment alone will produce a spread of numbers, and the auction structure mechanically selects the low end of that spread as the "winner" — regardless of which estimate was actually closest to the truth. A founder running a five-vendor pitch and picking the cheapest number isn't picking the most efficient vendor; they're picking whichever estimate happened to sit furthest from reality in the founder's favor, a selection effect built into the format of the competition itself, independent of any individual vendor's honesty or skill.

Sophisticated buyers in industries that have dealt with this problem for decades — procurement teams running competitive tenders for construction or large infrastructure projects — routinely correct for it by discounting unusually low bids rather than rewarding them, treating an outlier-low number as a signal to investigate rather than a signal to celebrate. The same correction applies directly to software vendor selection: an unusually low quote in a competitive process is evidence to interrogate, not a bargain to accept at face value.

## Correcting for the Winner's Curse in Your Own Vendor Selection

The standard corrections auction theory suggests translate reasonably directly into a software procurement process. Discount bids more aggressively the more uncertain the underlying scope is, since ambiguous requirements widen the spread of possible estimates and make the low outlier more likely to be a significant underestimate rather than genuine efficiency. Require every bidder to itemize their estimate by component — discovery, design, build, QA, post-launch — rather than submitting one number, since itemization makes it possible to see which specific line the low bidder cut rather than accepting an unexplained gap. And treat an unusually low bid as a prompt for a direct conversation about what's included, not as the natural conclusion of the comparison.

None of this requires distrust of any individual vendor's intentions, and it isn't a case against competitive bidding as a practice. It requires recognizing that a competitive multi-vendor process has a structural bias built into how it selects a "winner," the same way a well-run auction house corrects for the winner's curse not because bidders are dishonest, but because the format itself systematically rewards optimism over accuracy unless someone actively corrects for it.

Kranzhof, a Vienna-based specialty retailer, signed with the lowest of four bids for an inventory-management rebuild — 35% below the next lowest quote. Four months in, the vendor had delivered roughly 60% of the agreed scope and requested a 45% budget increase to finish, citing "unforeseen complexity" that had, in fact, been visible in the original brief.

## Reading a Quote Like a Buyer, Not Like a Bargain Hunter

The lowest number in a set of proposals is a signal to investigate, not a reason to sign — the same discipline auction theory has taught procurement teams in far higher-stakes bidding contexts for decades. Ask what's itemized, what discovery work produced the number, and what the vendor's last three projects of similar scope actually cost by the time they shipped, since the final delivered cost is a far more honest data point than the quoted starting one. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder deciding between multiple wildly different quotes) Should I always pick the middle quote instead of the cheapest?

Not automatically — but treat any quote significantly below the others as a reason to ask what's missing, not a reason to celebrate. The middle-priced, most itemized quote is usually the safer signal than the cheapest one.

### (Scenario: founder who already signed with a low-cost vendor) What should I do if my current vendor is underdelivering against a cheap quote?

Get an independent assessment of what's actually been built before deciding whether to continue or switch. Switching too early wastes sunk cost; continuing too long compounds it — an outside audit gives you the real picture either way.

### (Scenario: founder trying to evaluate a proposal before signing) What's the fastest way to tell if a quote is underscoped?

Ask the vendor to itemize design, build, QA, and post-launch stabilization as separate line items. A vendor who can't or won't break this down likely hasn't scoped it themselves.

### (Scenario: founder worried about switching vendors mid-project) How much does it typically cost to switch software vendors mid-project?

Expect to lose 20-40% of the already-spent budget to re-onboarding and reverse-engineering undocumented decisions, on top of the new vendor's fee to complete the remaining work.

### (Scenario: founder wanting to negotiate price without sacrificing scope) Is it possible to negotiate price without losing scope?

Yes — negotiate on rate or timeline flexibility rather than asking a vendor to quietly cut design, QA, or post-launch work to hit a lower number, which is the change most likely to resurface as a problem later.
