A CTO comparing an €85,000 senior engineer salary to a €65,000 dedicated-team engagement sees a €20,000 saving and moves on, treating the two line items as directly comparable when they measure fundamentally different things. The number that actually decides the comparison — recruitment cost, ramp time, and the eighteen months it takes a new in-house hire to reach full productivity — never makes it into that spreadsheet.

## The Salary Number Is Never the Real Number

Fully loaded cost of an in-house engineer, once every real component is accounted for, runs 25-40% above base salary once benefits, employer taxes, equipment, office overhead, and recruitment amortization are included — a €85,000 salary is closer to €110,000-€120,000 in true annual cost. That's before counting the 3-6 months a typical technical hiring process takes in a competitive European market, during which the seat sits empty and the roadmap slips.

A dedicated software development team engagement quotes an all-in monthly rate that already includes these costs, which is why the headline numbers look closer than the real comparison actually is — the in-house number is usually understated by omission, not by dishonesty.

## The Ramp-Time Cost Nobody Puts in the Hiring Plan

A new in-house engineer, even a strong senior hire, typically needs three to six months to reach full productivity on a codebase they didn't build — learning the architecture, the undocumented decisions, the team's specific conventions. During that ramp period, the company is paying full salary for partial output, a cost that rarely appears explicitly anywhere but is very real.

A dedicated team engaged through an established provider brings engineers who are already onboarded onto the provider's delivery processes, code review standards, and communication cadence — the ramp is shorter because the team-level infrastructure already exists, even though the specific project context still takes time to absorb either way.

## Where In-House Genuinely Wins

This isn't a case for always choosing outside teams. In-house hiring wins decisively when the work requires deep, long-term product ownership by someone embedded in daily company strategy discussions, or when the company's competitive advantage is specifically its proprietary engineering culture. A dedicated team is a poor fit for a five-person founding engineering team defining a company's technical identity from scratch — that identity benefits from people who are there permanently, in the room, invested as owners.

## Why Hiring Takes as Long as It Does, Economically Speaking

The three to six months a technical hire takes to close isn't primarily a reflection of how hard software engineering is to evaluate — it's a specific, well-studied instance of what labor economists call search and matching friction, a field of research recognized by the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences awarded to Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen, and Christopher Pissarides for formal models of how buyers and sellers in a market with imperfect information find each other. Their core insight, developed originally to explain persistent unemployment alongside unfilled job vacancies, applies directly to technical hiring: even when qualified candidates and open roles both exist in a market, matching a specific candidate to a specific role takes real time and resources, because neither side can costlessly verify fit in advance.

This friction is worse, not better, in competitive, specialized technical labor markets — exactly the market a CTO hiring a senior engineer in a major European tech hub is operating in. A larger pool of comparably qualified candidates doesn't shorten the search; it lengthens it, because a hiring manager now has more plausible options to screen and compare, and strong candidates simultaneously have more competing offers pulling them elsewhere mid-process, which is precisely why counteroffers and declined offers are such a common feature of technical recruitment in tight markets. Search and matching theory predicts this pattern formally; most CTOs learn it empirically, one lost candidate at a time.

A dedicated team model doesn't eliminate this friction — it relocates it. The provider absorbs the search-and-match cost once, at the point of building and maintaining a bench of engineers, and amortizes it across every client engagement rather than making each client run the full search independently. This is the real economic mechanism behind why a dedicated team can be engaged in weeks rather than months: not because the provider has found a way to skip matching friction entirely, but because they've already paid that cost upstream, at a scale an individual hiring company can't easily replicate for a single open role.

## What This Means for How You Sequence Hiring Decisions

Search and matching friction also explains why hiring plans that assume linear, predictable timelines so often slip in practice. A plan to fill three roles in a quarter isn't three independent search processes running at the expected pace — it's three processes each subject to the same market-wide friction, competing for attention from the same pool of engineers who are themselves fielding multiple offers simultaneously. The theory predicts, and CTOs repeatedly rediscover, that hiring plans slip disproportionately more than linearly as headcount targets grow, not because recruiting gets worse at scale but because the underlying matching problem compounds.

This has a direct, practical implication for sequencing: a dedicated team engagement isn't necessarily a permanent alternative to in-house hiring, it can be the tool that absorbs immediate capacity needs while a slower, more selective in-house search runs in parallel without the pressure of an empty roadmap forcing a rushed hire. Treating the two as sequential rather than mutually exclusive options resolves a false choice many CTOs feel they're facing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CTO building a hiring plan for the next two quarters) Is a dedicated team always cheaper than hiring in-house?

Not always on a pure monthly-rate basis, but frequently cheaper once fully loaded cost, recruitment time, and ramp-up productivity loss are included — the comparison depends heavily on how fast you need output and how competitive your local hiring market is.

### (Scenario: CTO worried about losing engineering culture by outsourcing) Does using a dedicated team mean losing control over engineering culture?

Not if the team is integrated into your existing sprint process and code standards, which a well-run dedicated-team engagement does by design. It's a bigger risk with unstructured, low-integration outsourcing arrangements.

### (Scenario: CTO trying to combine both models) Can I use a dedicated team alongside in-house hires?

Yes — many scaling companies keep a small in-house core for product ownership and strategy while using a dedicated team for delivery capacity, which is often the most cost-effective structure of all.

### (Scenario: CTO estimating true in-house hiring costs) What's typically missing from an in-house hiring cost estimate?

Recruitment fees or time, benefits and employer taxes, equipment and office overhead, and the productivity loss during a new hire's ramp-up period are the most commonly omitted costs.

### (Scenario: CTO deciding how fast they need to scale) When does a dedicated team make more sense than in-house hiring?

When you need to scale delivery capacity quickly, when local hiring is slow or expensive, or when the work is well-defined enough that deep, permanent product ownership isn't the primary requirement.
