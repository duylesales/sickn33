A founder says "let's build an MVP" and a vendor nods in agreement, and both walk away from that sentence with meaningfully different mental pictures of what's about to get built — one imagining the smallest possible thing that tests a specific hypothesis, the other imagining a scaled-down but still fairly complete version of the eventual full product. Neither is using the term incorrectly by the standards they're each working from. The term itself has always carried more than one legitimate meaning, and the gap between them causes real, avoidable scoping friction.

## Why "Minimum Viable Product" Was Never as Precise as It Sounds

The phrase sounds like it should have one clear, technical definition — minimum, viable, product, three fairly ordinary words combined into what feels like a specific engineering concept. In practice, "minimum" gets interpreted against wildly different baselines: minimum to test a hypothesis with real users, minimum to be sellable to a first paying customer, minimum to demo convincingly to an investor, or minimum relative to the founder's full eventual vision — four genuinely different scopes that can all reasonably be called an MVP, depending on which baseline a specific speaker has in mind.

## Tracing the Term Back to Where the Ambiguity Started

Product strategist Frank Robinson is generally credited with coining the term "minimum viable product" in the late 1990s, originally describing a product with just enough features to be adopted and useful to early customers, primarily as a mechanism for gathering real market feedback efficiently. Steve Blank's customer development methodology and Eric Ries's subsequent popularization of the term through Lean Startup in 2011 both used "MVP" in a broadly similar spirit — as a learning tool, not a scaled-down version of a full product — but the term's rapid, widespread adoption across the startup world happened faster than any single, precise definition could be consistently enforced across every context it got used in afterward.

The practical result, visible today in almost any founder-vendor scoping conversation, is that "MVP" has drifted into genuinely multiple, coexisting usages, none of which is simply wrong, but which produce very different actual scopes when two parties assume they're talking about the same thing without checking. A founder who read Ries's original framing closely tends to mean "the smallest thing that tests our core hypothesis, even if it's rough." A founder who's absorbed the term more informally through general startup culture often means something closer to "version one of the product, with fewer features than the eventual full vision" — a meaningfully larger, more polished scope than the first definition implies, even though both founders would describe what they want using the identical word.

## Why This Ambiguity Causes Real, Costly Scoping Friction

A vendor scoping to the "smallest hypothesis test" definition and a founder expecting the "polished version one" definition will produce a proposal, budget, and timeline that feels like a mismatch to at least one party once development actually starts — not because either side was negotiating in bad faith, but because the word "MVP" quietly carried two different scopes into the same conversation without anyone noticing the gap until it became visible in an actual deliverable. This is a more common and more avoidable source of early-stage scoping conflict than founders typically expect, precisely because both parties usually believe, right up until the mismatch surfaces, that they'd already agreed on what was being built.

## How to Actually Close the Gap Before It Costs You Time

- **Replace "MVP" with a specific, written description of the actual scope**, listing precisely which features are included and which are deliberately excluded, rather than relying on the single word to carry that information implicitly.
- **State explicitly what hypothesis the MVP exists to test**, if that's the intended framing, since this immediately clarifies whether the goal is minimal learning or a sellable first version — two different design targets that produce different appropriate scopes.
- **Ask a vendor directly which definition of MVP they're working from** before scoping begins, rather than assuming shared understanding based on both parties having used the same word comfortably in conversation.
- **Revisit the definition explicitly if the project's purpose shifts**, since a build that started as a hypothesis test can quietly turn into a "polished version one" expectation as stakeholders get more invested, without anyone formally updating the original scope agreement.

## Why Both Parties Usually Feel Confident Right Up Until the Mismatch Appears

What makes this specific ambiguity particularly costly, compared to a more obvious scoping disagreement, is that it doesn't feel like an open question to either party while it's happening. A founder saying "let's build an MVP" isn't consciously aware they're leaving a major scope decision unresolved — they have a specific picture in mind, and it feels complete and obvious to them. A vendor hearing the same phrase has their own specific, equally confident picture. Both walk out of the initial conversation believing real alignment was reached, precisely because the word felt sufficiently precise in the moment to both sides, even though it was carrying two different scopes underneath a shared surface vocabulary.

This is different from, and harder to catch than, a disagreement over an explicit number or an explicit feature — those surface immediately because at least one party notices the specific gap. An implicit definitional mismatch inside a comfortable, shared word doesn't surface until a deliverable makes the gap concrete, by which point real time and budget have usually already been spent building toward one interpretation while the other party was expecting something different. This is precisely why asking the clarifying question explicitly, even when it feels almost unnecessary because the conversation already "feels" aligned, is worth the minor awkwardness of asking it — the feeling of alignment and the fact of alignment aren't the same thing when a single ambiguous word is doing this much unexamined work.

## Four Common "MVP" Definitions in Practice

| Definition | Primary Goal | Typical Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis test | Learn if a core assumption holds | Very minimal, often rough |
| Sellable first version | Generate first paying customers | Moderate, functional core |
| Investor demo | Convincingly show potential | Polished but narrow |
| Scaled-down full vision | Early version of the eventual product | Larger, more feature-complete |
