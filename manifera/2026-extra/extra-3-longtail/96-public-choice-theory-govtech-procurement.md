A public sector IT manager watching a large, well-known vendor win a government tender over a smaller, more technically capable challenger has very likely watched a genuinely predictable pattern play out, not a failure of the specific selection committee's own judgment. Government procurement processes, across many jurisdictions and many specific procurement teams, tend to systematically favor certain kinds of vendors for reasons that have relatively little to do with which vendor would actually deliver the best software, and a specific branch of economic theory explains exactly why.

## Why Public Procurement Doesn't Simply Select for Best Delivery

A private company choosing a software vendor directly bears the real cost of a bad choice — a failed project, a budget overrun, a missed deadline all land directly on the decision-maker's own performance and the company's own bottom line. A public sector procurement decision-maker faces a meaningfully different incentive structure: the cost of a failed project is diffused across taxpayers broadly, while the cost of a procurement process that looks irregular, insufficiently documented, or genuinely difficult to defend in an audit lands squarely and personally on the decision-maker, regardless of whether the vendor selected through a defensible process actually delivers well in the end.

## The Economic Framework That Studies Exactly This Gap

Economist James Buchanan, whose work on public choice theory earned him the 1986 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, applied standard economic reasoning about self-interested behavior to public sector decision-making specifically, challenging an older assumption that government actors, unlike private market participants, act purely in the public interest without their own personal incentives shaping their choices. Buchanan's framework treats public officials and procurement decision-makers as rational actors responding to the actual incentive structure they personally face, which in most public procurement contexts rewards process defensibility and audit-proof documentation considerably more directly and reliably than it rewards the eventual, harder-to-attribute quality of what actually gets delivered.

Applied directly to government software procurement, Buchanan's framework explains a pattern many public sector IT managers have observed without necessarily having an economic name for it: a large, well-known vendor with an extensive, easily-documented track record and a polished, thorough proposal offers a procurement decision-maker a more defensible paper trail than a smaller, more technically capable challenger with a thinner but arguably more relevant portfolio — and given the personal incentive asymmetry Buchanan's framework describes, the more defensible choice frequently wins regardless of which vendor would actually deliver better software, not because anyone involved is corrupt or incompetent, but because the process's actual incentive structure rewards defensibility more reliably than it rewards outcome quality.

## Why This Matters for How a Government Buyer Should Actually Structure Procurement

Recognizing the incentive gap Buchanan's framework describes doesn't mean public procurement is somehow hopeless or inherently corrupt — it means the process needs to be deliberately structured to reward the outcome actually wanted (good software delivery) rather than defaulting to a process that, left unexamined, rewards paper-trail defensibility instead. This requires procurement criteria that make technical capability and delivery track record genuinely, verifiably part of the defensible record, not just an informal consideration a committee weighs alongside a more heavily-scored, more easily-documented set of process criteria.

## What a Better-Aligned Public Procurement Process Actually Requires

- **Weight technical evaluation and reference verification as heavily as documented process compliance**, ensuring a smaller vendor's genuine capability can be reflected in the same defensible scoring record a larger vendor's brand recognition would otherwise dominate.
- **Include a paid pilot or technical assessment stage for finalists**, generating concrete, current evidence of delivery capability that becomes part of the defensible record itself, not just an informal impression that doesn't show up in the audit trail.
- **Explicitly document why smaller or less brand-recognized vendors were or weren't selected**, closing the specific gap where a decision-maker's incentive to avoid audit risk might otherwise default toward the safer-looking, more established name regardless of actual fit.
- **Recognize that a fair process and a genuinely well-incentivized process aren't automatically the same thing**, and design procurement criteria deliberately to close the gap Buchanan's framework identifies, rather than assuming procedural fairness alone produces the best outcome.

## Why This Isn't an Argument That Public Officials Are Acting in Bad Faith

It's worth being direct about what Buchanan's framework does and doesn't claim, since public choice theory is sometimes misread as a cynical accusation that government officials are simply self-serving in a way private actors aren't. That's not the actual claim — Buchanan's contribution was applying the same standard economic assumption of rational self-interest to public and private actors alike, rather than assuming public officials are uniquely virtuous while private actors are uniquely self-interested, an inconsistency the older assumption had quietly relied on without much justification. A procurement decision-maker favoring the more defensible option isn't behaving worse than a private-sector buyer would in an analogous position — they're responding rationally to a different incentive structure, one where personal audit risk is more immediate and more personally consequential than the more diffuse, harder-to-trace cost of a suboptimal delivery outcome.

This distinction matters practically because it points toward the right fix: not appealing to individual procurement officers to simply try harder or care more about outcomes, which public choice theory would predict has limited effect against a misaligned incentive structure, but redesigning the structure itself so that rewarding genuine capability and building a defensible record become the same action rather than a trade-off the decision-maker has to personally absorb the risk of making. ## Case Study: A Kaunas Municipality's Restructured Tender

Kaunas municipal government's IT department had, in a previous tender cycle, selected a large, internationally known vendor for a citizen services platform, largely on the strength of the vendor's brand recognition and extensive documentation, over a smaller vendor's more technically tailored proposal — a decision that later proved genuinely difficult to defend once the platform shipped significantly over budget and behind schedule, with several citizen-facing features never fully delivered at all.

For a subsequent tender, the department's procurement lead restructured the evaluation criteria explicitly to weight a paid technical pilot and verified reference calls as heavily as documented process compliance, specifically to close the gap Buchanan's framework identifies. Manifera participated in the pilot stage directly, and the resulting procurement record included concrete, verifiable technical evidence alongside the standard process documentation, giving the eventual selection a genuinely defensible record grounded in demonstrated capability rather than brand recognition alone.

> *"The first time, I could defend the decision to an auditor perfectly. I couldn't defend it to the citizens actually waiting for a working platform. The second process let me defend both at once."*
> — **Procurement Lead, Kaunas Municipal IT Department**

Kaunas municipal IT now requires a technical pilot stage as standard practice for any software procurement above a defined value, treating verified delivery evidence as a core, scored part of the defensible record rather than an informal supplement to it — a change the procurement lead describes explicitly as redesigning what "defensible" means, not asking colleagues to simply care more.

### (Scenario: procurement lead trying to restructure an evaluation process) How can a procurement process be restructured to better reward actual delivery capability?

Weight technical pilots, reference verification, and delivery track record as heavily as documented process compliance in the formal scoring criteria, making genuine capability part of the defensible record rather than an informal supplement to it.

### (Scenario: smaller vendor trying to compete in public tenders) How can a smaller, less brand-recognized vendor compete effectively in public sector procurement?

Push for evaluation criteria that include a technical pilot or verified reference stage, since this converts genuine capability into the kind of concrete, defensible evidence a procurement process can weight formally, leveling the field against brand recognition alone.

It's a meaningful contributing factor — an incentive structure that rewards process defensibility over delivery quality predictably produces some selection mismatches, independent of any individual decision-maker's competence or good faith.
