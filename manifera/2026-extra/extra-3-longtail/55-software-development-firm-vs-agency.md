"Firm" and "agency" get used almost interchangeably in casual, everyday conversation about software vendors, and companies self-select one label or the other largely for branding reasons that don't consistently map to any real, meaningful operational difference. The labels themselves aren't reliably informative on their own — but the underlying dimensions people are often gesturing at, somewhat imprecisely, when they favor one word over the other genuinely are worth evaluating directly and carefully.

## What the Labels Sometimes, Unreliably, Gesture At

"Agency" often carries a loose connotation of broader creative or marketing services, sometimes including design, branding, and marketing work alongside development — a full-service positioning of sorts. "Firm" often carries a somewhat different connotation of more specialized, deeper technical or engineering focus, sometimes implying a more formal, deliberately process-driven internal structure. These remain loose, informal cultural associations rather than consistent, reliable rules — plenty of companies calling themselves "agencies" are genuinely, deeply technical in practice, and plenty calling themselves "firms" actually offer quite broad, varied service ranges regardless of the label chosen.

## The Actual Dimensions Worth Evaluating Directly

- **Overall breadth vs. depth of service offering.** Does the company offer a wide range of services (design, marketing, development, branding) or a narrower, deeper focus on software engineering specifically? Neither is inherently better overall — it genuinely depends on whether you need a single partner for multiple disciplines or deep specialization in engineering alone.
- **Project-based vs. ongoing relationship default structure.** Some companies are structured primarily around discrete, bounded projects; others default toward ongoing, evolving engagements. This matters more for your actual need than either label predicts.
- **Overall team structure and specialization depth.** Does the company have deep engineering specialists, or a more generalist team spread across multiple service lines? A company offering many services with the same core team may have less deep engineering specialization than one focused specifically on software development.
- **Typical client size and project complexity actually served.** Some companies specialize in smaller, faster-turnaround projects; others specialize in larger, more complex, longer-duration engagements — evaluable through their actual portfolio and references, not their self-chosen label.

## Why Ignoring the Label and Asking Directly Serves You Better

Rather than trying to infer real meaning from whether a company calls itself a firm or an agency, ask directly and specifically about the dimensions that actually matter for your particular project: their service breadth, their typical engagement structure, their team's specific technical depth, and their track record with projects of your particular complexity. The label is fundamentally marketing positioning; these specifics are the actual decision-relevant information a founder genuinely needs.

## Why a Single Word Can Redirect an Entire Search

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's research on framing effects, published in a series of papers through the late 1970s and early 1980s that contributed to Kahneman's 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, demonstrated that how a choice is described — its framing — measurably changes how people evaluate it, even when the underlying substance of the choice is held completely constant. Their best-known illustration involved describing an identical medical outcome in terms of survival rates versus mortality rates: the same statistical reality produced systematically different decisions depending purely on which frame was used to present it, despite the numbers themselves never changing.

The "firm" versus "agency" distinction operates as a framing effect on vendor search behavior, not a substantive difference in what's actually on offer. A founder who has decided, often for reasons unrelated to any real evidence, that "firm" connotes technical rigor is applying a self-generated frame to an otherwise neutral label — and that frame then actively shapes which companies enter the search at all, before any actual evaluation of technical depth, track record, or engagement structure has taken place. This is precisely why Loirevague's founder excluded genuinely strong vendors from her shortlist without ever comparing them on the merits: the framing effect operated upstream of evaluation entirely, at the search stage, where a word choice determined which companies she even considered before any substantive comparison could occur.

A founder at Nantes-based startup Loirevague had specifically and deliberately searched for "software development firm," genuinely believing it implied more technical rigor than "agency," narrowing her search in a way that quietly excluded several genuinely strong technical vendors who simply happened to use the word "agency" somewhere in their own branding.

## Recognizing Framing Effects in Your Own Vendor Language

The broader lesson from Tversky and Kahneman's research is that framing effects operate on essentially any word or phrase carrying an unexamined connotation, not just "firm" versus "agency" specifically. "Boutique" versus "full-service," "offshore" versus "distributed," "startup-focused" versus "enterprise-grade" — each pairing frames an otherwise neutral operational reality in a way that can quietly shape a search before any real evaluation begins. A useful discipline is treating any descriptive label a vendor applies to itself as a hypothesis to test through direct questions, rather than as information that's already been verified simply by virtue of being stated.

## Labels vs. Actual Decision-Relevant Factors

| What the Label Suggests | What to Actually Evaluate |
|---|---|
| "Agency" implies broad service range | Ask directly: what services do you actually offer? |
| "Firm" implies deeper technical focus | Ask directly: what's your team's specific technical depth? |
| Either label implies project structure | Ask directly: do you offer project-based, dedicated team, or both? |
| Either label implies typical client size | Ask for references matching your specific project complexity |

## Reframing Your Own Search Before You Start

A practical technique drawn directly from the debiasing literature that followed Tversky and Kahneman's original framing research: deliberately generate the opposite frame before acting on the first one that occurs to you. If "firm" feels more rigorous, consciously ask what specific evidence would make you believe an "agency" is equally or more rigorous, and search for it directly. This doesn't eliminate the framing effect entirely — the research suggests it's remarkably persistent even among people fully aware of it — but forcing an explicit counter-frame measurably narrows its influence on which vendors actually make it into a serious evaluation.

Applied to vendor search specifically, this means treating the label as noise to be filtered out early, and writing down the three or four substantive criteria — technical depth, engagement structure, track record at your project's complexity, service breadth — before typing a single search query. A search built around those criteria directly returns a fundamentally different, more representative set of candidates than one built around a label that was never designed to carry the specific meaning being unconsciously assigned to it.
