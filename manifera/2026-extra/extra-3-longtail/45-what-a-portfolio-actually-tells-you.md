A portfolio shows finished screenshots of a company's best-looking outcomes, curated by the company itself, with no visibility into the process that produced them, how many projects didn't make the cut, or what the client relationship actually felt like along the way. It's real evidence of design and technical capability. It's not evidence of much else a prospective client actually needs to know.

## What a Portfolio Genuinely Tells You

Design sensibility, breadth of industries served, and technical range (if project descriptions include enough detail) are things a portfolio can legitimately demonstrate. If a company has built genuinely diverse, well-designed products across several industries, that's real, useful signal about capability — a portfolio isn't worthless, it's just incomplete.

## What a Portfolio Structurally Can't Tell You

- **Whether the project shipped on time and on budget.** A portfolio shows the finished product, not the timeline or cost overruns that may have preceded it.
- **What the communication and collaboration process actually felt like** for the client, day to day, across the project's full duration — a portfolio has no mechanism to convey this at all.
- **Whether the client would work with the company again**, which is a more honest signal than a single polished case study the company chose to publish.
- **How many projects from that period aren't in the portfolio** because they didn't end well enough to showcase — a portfolio is a curated highlight reel by design, not a representative sample.
- **Who specifically worked on the shown projects**, and whether those same people would be assigned to a new engagement, or whether the portfolio represents past team members no longer at the company.

## How to Get the Information a Portfolio Can't Provide

- **Ask for a direct reference call**, not just a written testimonial the company selected and published — a real-time conversation surfaces nuance a curated quote never will.
- **Ask specifically about a project that didn't go smoothly**, and how the company handled it — every company has had one, and how they discuss it reveals more than any success story.
- **Ask who specifically would be assigned to your project**, and whether that person or team is represented anywhere in the visible portfolio.
- **Ask about timeline and budget adherence** on the projects shown, not just the finished quality — a beautiful late, over-budget project is a different signal than a beautiful on-time, on-budget one.

## The Bias Named After Missing Bullet Holes

Statistician Abraham Wald's work for the U.S. military during World War II produced one of the most famous illustrations of survivorship bias in applied statistics. Asked to recommend where to add armor to returning bomber aircraft based on the pattern of bullet holes found on planes that made it back, the military's initial instinct was to armor the areas showing the most damage. Wald's insight reversed the logic entirely: the planes being studied were the survivors — the ones that took damage in those areas and still made it home. The planes that took damage in the areas showing few or no bullet holes on returning aircraft were, Wald reasoned, the ones that didn't survive to be studied at all, meaning those under-represented areas were actually the most critical to armor.

A vendor's portfolio is a direct analogue to Wald's returning bombers: it necessarily shows only the projects that survived to become a published, curated case study — the ones that went well enough, or at least looked good enough in retrospect, to be worth showcasing. It structurally cannot show the projects that didn't survive that selection process: the ones that ran over budget, ended in a difficult client relationship, or were quietly discontinued before completion. Just as the military's initial instinct dangerously mistook survivor data for complete data, a founder evaluating only a portfolio risks mistaking the surviving, curated fraction of a vendor's work for the vendor's actual overall track record.

This is precisely why a reference call matters more than portfolio review alone, and why asking specifically about a project that didn't go smoothly is such a disproportionately informative question — it's a deliberate attempt to see past the survivorship-biased sample and glimpse the "planes that didn't make it back": the client relationships and projects that never made the published portfolio, which are frequently more informative about a vendor's real weaknesses than the polished survivors ever could be. Wald's redirected the military's armor allocation by asking what was systematically missing from the visible data. The same redirection — actively asking what's missing from a portfolio, not just admiring what's present — is exactly what separates a founder who avoids the survivorship trap from one who doesn't.

## Asking Explicitly for What the Sample Is Missing

Wald's redirection of the military's attention — from the visible damage on survivors to the inferred damage on non-survivors — has a direct procedural analogue a founder can apply to vendor evaluation: explicitly ask a vendor to describe their overall project outcome distribution, not just show the curated highlights. What percentage of projects in a given period shipped on time and on budget, roughly? Of the ones that didn't, what happened, and how did the vendor handle it? A vendor with a genuinely strong track record can usually answer this kind of aggregate question honestly without much discomfort, because the full distribution, not just the curated top of it, still reflects reasonably well on them.

A vendor who can only speak fluently about the specific, cherry-picked projects in their published portfolio, and grows noticeably vague the moment the question shifts to the full distribution of outcomes, is exhibiting exactly the pattern survivorship bias predicts: a comfortable, well-rehearsed narrative about the survivors, and much less clarity about everything that didn't make the cut. Neither answer alone guarantees a good or bad vendor — but the willingness and ability to speak honestly and specifically about the full distribution, not just its most flattering slice, is itself a genuinely more reliable signal to actually weigh than the polish of the curated portfolio being shown.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder relying heavily on a portfolio to choose a vendor) Is it a mistake to choose a vendor based primarily on their portfolio?

Not a mistake to use it as a starting filter, but a mistake to treat it as sufficient on its own — pair it with a direct reference call to get the process and reliability information a portfolio structurally can't show.

### (Scenario: founder unsure how to request a reference) How do I ask a vendor for a reference without it feeling awkward?

Frame it as standard due diligence for a significant engagement: "Could I speak with a past client whose project was similarly complex to mine?" is a normal, expected request that a confident vendor accommodates readily.

### (Scenario: founder worried a vendor will only offer their best reference) Won't a vendor just give me their happiest client as a reference anyway?

Likely yes, which is exactly why asking specifically about a project that didn't go smoothly, and how it was handled, is a more revealing question than the reference itself — even a curated reference will usually answer honestly if asked directly.

### (Scenario: founder trying to evaluate team continuity) Why does it matter whether the same people who built the portfolio's past projects would work on my project?

Because a portfolio's quality reflects specific people's skill and judgment — if those people have left the company, the portfolio is evidence of the company's past capability, not necessarily its current team's capability.

### (Scenario: founder trying to build a shortlist efficiently) How should I use a portfolio in my vendor evaluation process?

Use it to build an initial shortlist based on design sensibility and relevant industry experience, then move immediately to reference calls and direct technical conversations before making a final decision — don't let portfolio quality alone be the deciding factor.
