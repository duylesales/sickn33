A first discovery call with a software vendor feels like a straightforward sales conversation, and it partly genuinely is — but it's also a surprisingly information-dense thirty minutes if a founder knows exactly what to actually listen for beyond the specific answers given to the specific questions asked.

## What to Listen For Beyond the Direct Answers

- **Do they ask genuine questions before proposing solutions?** A vendor who spends the first half of the call asking specifically about your situation, constraints, and goals before suggesting an approach is demonstrating real discovery discipline. A vendor who launches directly into a pitch almost immediately is clearly applying a template, not genuinely scoping your actual project.
- **Is their language genuinely specific to what you actually said, or generic?** References back to the specific details you mentioned — your industry, your particular technical constraint, your timeline pressure — versus generic statements that could apply to any prospective client reveal how genuinely tailored their thinking really is, even in this very first conversation.
- **How do they actually handle a question they don't have a ready answer to?** A vendor comfortable saying "I don't know, let me find out and follow up" is generally more trustworthy than one who improvises a confident-sounding answer to everything, since real projects regularly generate genuine questions nobody has an immediate answer to.
- **Who's actually present on the call?** A technical lead present alongside or instead of a salesperson signals the company genuinely treats even a first conversation as real technical engagement, not purely a sales funnel stage.
- **What actually happens in the follow-up?** A proposal or summary that specifically references what was discussed, versus a generic template sent to every prospective client, reveals whether the call's actual real content shaped what comes next.

## Why These Signals Predict More Than They Seem To

None of these signals directly prove technical competence on their own — a vendor could genuinely ask great discovery questions and still, in principle, deliver mediocre code down the line. But they reliably and consistently predict process discipline, basic honesty, and genuine engagement with a specific client's actual situation, which correlate strongly with how the rest of a project relationship will actually unfold in practice, even well before any code is written or any single reference is checked. Revealed preference theory offers a useful reason why this correlation holds up as well as it does: process discipline demonstrated under the real, immediate constraint of a live thirty-minute call, with genuine trade-offs about how to spend that limited time, is a costlier and therefore more informative signal than a written claim about process discipline made with no comparable constraint attached to it. A vendor's website can claim any process it likes at essentially no cost; a live call forces an actual choice about where attention goes, and that choice is what a founder should be weighing most heavily.

## Why Behavior on the Call Outweighs What's Said

Economist Paul Samuelson introduced the theory of revealed preference in a 1938 paper, proposing that a person's actual choices under real conditions are a more reliable guide to their true preferences than their stated intentions or self-reported values. Samuelson's insight, foundational to much of modern consumer theory, was methodological as well as substantive: rather than asking someone what they prefer and taking the answer at face value, an economist could infer genuine preference more reliably by observing what that person actually chose when a real trade-off was in front of them, since stated preference is cheap to express while revealed preference costs something real to enact.

A discovery call is, in exactly this sense, a revealed-preference test of a vendor's real process discipline, not merely a report of it. Any vendor can state, if asked directly, that they prioritize discovery, tailor their approach, and involve technical leadership early — these are stated preferences, cheap to claim in a sales conversation. What the vendor actually does on the call — whether they spend the first fifteen minutes asking about a founder's specific compliance requirements or launch straight into a pitch — is the revealed preference, and Samuelson's framework explains precisely why it's the more trustworthy signal: the vendor's real operating priorities are on display in how the limited, real time of the call actually gets spent, in a way a claim about their process, made in the abstract, never can be.

## Reading the Gap Between Claim and Behavior

The most useful diagnostic a founder can apply, drawing directly on Samuelson's framework, isn't simply observing behavior in isolation — it's comparing a vendor's stated claims against their revealed behavior on the same call and noting the size of any gap. A vendor who explicitly claims to prioritize discovery and then spends eighteen of twenty minutes pitching is revealing a meaningful discrepancy between stated and revealed preference, a discrepancy that Taffwyn Health's two pitch-first vendors displayed clearly once the founder began comparing what they'd claimed in earlier marketing materials against what they actually did live on the call.

## Applying Revealed Preference Beyond the First Call

Samuelson's framework has a useful application past the discovery call itself: it's a lens worth carrying through the entire vendor relationship, not just the first thirty minutes. A vendor's stated commitment to transparent communication, checked against how quickly and specifically they actually respond when a real problem surfaces mid-project, is the same revealed-versus-stated comparison applied to an ongoing relationship rather than a single conversation. A vendor's claimed flexibility, checked against how they actually handle a legitimate scope-change request, works the same way.

This gives a founder a durable habit worth carrying well beyond vendor selection: whenever a claim about intentions, values, or priorities is made — by a vendor, a hire, or any counterparty in a business relationship — the more reliable read comes from observing what actually gets prioritized when a genuine trade-off arises, not from the claim stated in the abstract, before any real cost of following through has been incurred. A discovery call is simply the first, most compressed opportunity to run that test, not the only one available across an engagement's full duration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder preparing for an upcoming vendor call) What should I pay attention to during a vendor discovery call beyond the specific answers given?

Notice whether they ask substantive questions before proposing solutions, whether their language is specific to your situation or generic, and who's actually present on the call.

### (Scenario: founder comparing notes from multiple vendor calls) How do I compare discovery calls across several vendors objectively?

Take notes during each call against a consistent checklist — question-asking behavior, language specificity, call attendees, and follow-up quality — rather than relying on a general impression that can be swayed by presentation polish.

### (Scenario: founder concerned about a vendor who didn't have every answer ready) Should I be worried if a vendor couldn't answer one of my questions immediately during discovery?

Not necessarily — a vendor who says "I don't know, let me find out" is often more trustworthy than one who improvises a confident answer to everything, since real projects regularly raise questions without immediate answers.

### (Scenario: founder unsure if a salesperson-only call is a red flag) Is it a problem if only a salesperson, not a technical lead, is on my first call?

It's not automatically disqualifying, but it's worth asking directly for a technical lead's involvement before finalizing any decision, since it signals whether the company treats early conversations as genuine technical scoping.

### (Scenario: founder trying to evaluate a follow-up proposal) What should I look for in a vendor's follow-up materials after a discovery call?

Check whether the proposal specifically references what was actually discussed — your particular requirements, constraints, and concerns — rather than reading like a generic template that could have been sent regardless of the conversation's content.
