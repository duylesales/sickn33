A 2% transaction fee on a webshop platform sounds genuinely negligible when a founder is processing roughly €10,000 a month. At €2 million a month, that same 2% is €40,000 — every single month, indefinitely, growing in exact lockstep with the very revenue growth the founder originally built the business to achieve in the first place.

## Why the Early Math Hides the Later Math

Most off-the-shelf ecommerce platforms price themselves quite attractively at low volume — a modest monthly subscription plus a small percentage-based transaction fee — because that specific pricing structure is genuinely competitive against custom development at the volume a typical new webshop actually processes in its early days. What the early pricing comparison doesn't surface is that the percentage-based component isn't a flat cost that stays proportionally small; it's a cost structure explicitly designed to scale with the business's own success, meaning the platform's cost advantage at launch quietly inverts into a cost disadvantage once revenue grows past a specific, calculable threshold.

## The Threshold Where the Math Flips

A custom-built ecommerce platform carries a real, upfront development cost and an ongoing maintenance cost, both of which stay largely fixed regardless of how much transaction volume actually flows through the system. An off-the-shelf platform's transaction-based fee, by contrast, grows linearly with revenue. These two cost curves necessarily cross at some specific volume — below it, the platform's low fixed cost wins; above it, the custom build's fixed cost, spread across a much larger transaction volume, wins by an increasingly wide and compounding margin. The specific crossover point varies by platform fee structure and development cost, but the existence of a crossover point is mathematically guaranteed by the shape of the two cost curves themselves, not a matter of opinion or specific to any one platform.

## Why B2B Ecommerce Often Hits This Threshold Faster Than B2C

B2B ecommerce transactions frequently involve considerably larger order values than typical B2C retail transactions, meaning a B2B webshop can reach the crossover threshold at a meaningfully lower total transaction count than a comparable B2C store processing many small individual orders. A B2B platform handling bulk wholesale orders, custom pricing tiers, and negotiated contract terms often also requires specific functionality that off-the-shelf platforms tend to handle poorly regardless of cost — meaning the case for custom development in B2B ecommerce frequently combines both the cost-crossover argument and a genuine functional-fit argument together, reinforcing each other rather than requiring a founder to choose decisively between them.

## What the Full Comparison Actually Requires

- **Projected transaction volume over a realistic multi-year horizon**, not just current volume alone, since the crossover point is only genuinely meaningful when compared against where the business is actually headed, not merely where it currently sits today.
- **The platform's genuinely complete fee structure**, including transaction fees, payment processing surcharges, and any tiered subscription costs that increase with scale, not just the single headline percentage most prominently advertised upfront.
- **Custom development's genuinely full cost**, including the initial build, ongoing maintenance, hosting, and the team capacity needed to extend it further over time — not just the initial build quote considered in complete isolation.
- **Functionality gaps beyond pure raw cost**, since a platform's genuine inability to support a specific B2B workflow can justify custom development well before the transaction-fee math alone actually would on its own.

## Why Founders Systematically Underweight This Calculation Early On

At launch, a founder's attention is reasonably focused on getting to market quickly and cheaply — the platform's low entry cost and fast setup time are genuinely the right priority when validating a new ecommerce concept with limited capital and uncertain demand. The systematic error isn't choosing the platform early — it's never revisiting the decision once growth changes the underlying math, continuing to accept a cost structure that made sense at €10,000 a month long after it stops making sense at €2 million a month, simply because switching feels disruptive and the original decision was never designed to be revisited on a schedule.

## The Economic Concept Behind Why Fixed Cost Eventually Wins

Economist Alfred Marshall, whose 1890 work *Principles of Economics* formalized much of modern microeconomic theory, introduced the concept of economies of scale — the observation that as production volume increases, the fixed cost of production gets spread across more units, driving down the average cost per unit even as total output grows. Marshall's framework distinguishes fixed costs, which don't change with volume, from variable costs, which do, and the entire logic of economies of scale rests on that distinction: a business with predominantly fixed costs sees its per-unit cost fall as it grows, while a business with predominantly variable costs sees its per-unit cost stay flat regardless of scale.

Custom ecommerce development is a fixed-cost structure in Marshall's precise sense — the development and maintenance cost doesn't meaningfully increase whether the platform processes one thousand or one million transactions a month, so the effective cost per transaction falls continuously as volume grows. An off-the-shelf platform's percentage-based transaction fee is, by contrast, a variable cost structure almost by definition — it's explicitly calculated as a percentage of each transaction, so the per-unit cost never falls with scale at all. Marshall's framework predicts, with mathematical inevitability, that a growing business will eventually favor the fixed-cost option over the variable-cost one, precisely because that's what economies of scale means in the first place — the only genuine question is not whether the crossover happens, but at what specific volume it happens for a given business's actual numbers.

Andalucía Suministros, a Málaga-based B2B distributor, had launched on a popular off-the-shelf ecommerce platform three years earlier, when its transaction volume made the platform's 2.5% fee negligible in absolute terms. By the time monthly revenue reached €1.8 million, that same percentage had become the single largest line item in the company's operating costs, larger than its entire warehouse staffing budget, without anyone having deliberately decided that trade-off was worth continuing to accept.

Manifera's Amsterdam team calculated the specific crossover point during discovery and found Andalucía Suministros had already been well past it for over a year. The Vietnam pod built a custom B2B platform with negotiated pricing tiers and bulk order workflows the original platform had never supported well, and the transaction fee, converted into a fixed annual maintenance cost, dropped to a fraction of what the platform fee alone had cost in a single quarter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder unsure whether to reconsider their ecommerce platform) How do I know if my business has passed the point where custom development makes more financial sense?

Calculate your platform's total fee at current monthly revenue against a custom build's amortized development and maintenance cost — if the platform fee already exceeds what custom development would cost annually, you're likely past the threshold.

### (Scenario: founder assuming their platform choice was permanent) Why didn't anyone tell me this crossover point would happen when I first chose my platform?

Because at launch, the platform's low cost and fast setup were genuinely the right choice — the crossover point is a consequence of growth, not a flaw in the original decision, which is exactly why it needs revisiting periodically rather than assumed fixed.

### (Scenario: B2B founder wondering why this applies more to them) Why does this threshold tend to arrive faster for B2B ecommerce than B2C?

Larger average order values in B2B mean the same transaction fee percentage generates a larger absolute cost at a lower transaction count, reaching the crossover threshold sooner than a typical B2C store with many smaller orders.

### (Scenario: founder worried custom development means losing platform conveniences) Does switching to custom development mean giving up features an off-the-shelf platform provides?

Not necessarily — a well-scoped custom build can replicate genuinely useful platform features while adding the specific functionality, like negotiated B2B pricing, the original platform never supported well.

### (Scenario: founder trying to plan when to revisit this decision) How often should I recalculate whether my ecommerce platform is still the right cost choice?

Annually at minimum, or whenever transaction volume grows significantly — treating it as a standing periodic review rather than a one-time decision keeps the cost structure aligned with the business's actual current scale.
