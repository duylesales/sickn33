Three agencies. Three quotes for the same fifteen-screen booking app. €38,000. €61,000. €94,000. Same brief, same feature list, same launch date — and the founder who collected all three still couldn't tell you which number was real, because none of them said what was actually included.

That gap is not agencies padding numbers. It's agencies quoting different slices of the same project and calling all three slices "the app."

## Why Every Mobile App Development Cost Quote Means Something Different

A quote that only covers the build sprint looks cheap next to one that covers design, build, QA, app store submission, and a post-launch stabilization window — but the cheap quote isn't actually cheaper, it's just missing rows. When a founder compares mobile app development cost across vendors, the first job isn't picking a number, it's normalizing what each number includes.

The four components that determine real mobile app development cost, in the order they usually get underestimated:

- **Design and UX** — wireframes, a design system, and usability passes, typically 15-20% of total budget. Skipped design work resurfaces later as expensive mid-build rework.
- **Core build** — the actual engineering: frontend, backend, API integrations, native platform work. This is the number most quotes lead with, and the only number most founders remember.
- **QA and device testing** — cross-device, cross-OS-version testing that catches the crashes a demo on one iPhone never surfaces. Consistently the first line item cut under budget pressure, and the first one that shows up as 1-star reviews.
- **App store submission and post-launch stabilization** — Apple and Google review cycles, crash monitoring, and the first round of real-user bug fixes in the two to four weeks after launch, when actual usage patterns reveal what testing missed.

A quote that names only the core build is quoting maybe 55-65% of the actual mobile app development cost. The other third shows up anyway — as a change order, a "phase two," or a frantic weekend before the app store deadline.

## A Realistic 2026 Budget Range, by App Complexity

- **Simple utility app** (content display, basic forms, no real-time features): €15,000-€35,000, 6-10 weeks.
- **Standard consumer app** (auth, user profiles, moderate API integration, push notifications): €35,000-€75,000, 10-16 weeks.
- **Complex marketplace or SaaS-companion app** (payments, real-time sync, multi-role permissions, offline support): €75,000-€180,000+, 16-28 weeks.

These ranges assume a European or offshore-managed engineering team building production-grade software, not a freelance MVP shortcut. A quote that undercuts the low end of its category by 40% is not a bargain; it is a missing line item waiting to be discovered.

## Why the Estimation Gap Is Wider for Mobile Than for Most Software

Software estimation has a well-documented problem long before a founder ever requests a quote. Steve McConnell's widely cited "cone of uncertainty" — a concept that has shaped how the software industry thinks about estimation since the 1990s — describes how early-stage project estimates can be off by a factor of four in either direction, narrowing only as real requirements, design decisions, and technical unknowns get resolved. A quote produced from a one-page brief and a 30-minute call sits at the widest part of that cone, which is exactly why three agencies can hand a founder three defensible-sounding numbers that differ by more than double.

Mobile development narrows that cone more slowly than web development does, for reasons specific to the platform. A web app targets a handful of modern browsers with relatively predictable behavior. A mobile app has to account for a fragmented device landscape — different screen sizes, camera hardware, chipset performance, and OS versions still in active use — plus two separate, opaque review processes (Apple's App Store and Google Play) that can reject a submission for reasons that only become clear after the fact. Each of these is a source of variance a generic software estimate doesn't carry, and it's why mobile-specific QA and submission line items get underestimated more often than their web equivalents.

Industry research on software project outcomes has tracked this pattern for decades. The Standish Group's long-running CHAOS studies on IT project performance have repeatedly found that cost overruns are closer to the norm than the exception for projects scoped from incomplete early information — not a rare failure mode limited to badly run projects, but a structural consequence of estimating before the cone of uncertainty has narrowed. The practical implication for a founder isn't that estimation is broken; it's that a single number produced without a discovery phase is, by definition, a guess from the widest part of the cone, and comparing three such guesses tells you more about each agency's guessing habits than about the actual project.

## What a Properly Narrowed Estimate Actually Requires

Narrowing the cone of uncertainty before quoting means doing real discovery work — wireframing key screens, identifying every third-party integration, and confirming platform scope — before committing to a number. This is the difference between an estimate and a guess dressed up as an estimate, and it's checkable: ask a vendor what specific discovery artifacts (wireframes, a technical requirements document, an integration list) produced their number, not just how confident they sound stating it.

## What Changes the Number Most

| Cost Driver | Low-End Impact | High-End Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Platform scope | iOS or Android only | Native iOS + Android simultaneously |
| Backend complexity | Simple REST API, single data model | Real-time sync, multi-tenant permissions |
| Third-party integrations | 1-2 (auth, analytics) | 5+ (payments, CRM, logistics, push, SSO) |
| Design maturity | Existing brand system, reused components | Full design system built from scratch |
| Post-launch support | None included | 4-week stabilization window included |

These cost drivers rarely move in isolation, which is another reason a single "final number" from a quick quote is unreliable. A project that adds a second platform, a real-time backend, and three third-party integrations at once doesn't accumulate those costs additively — the interactions between them (a real-time sync layer that has to behave identically on both iOS and Android, each integration needing its own error handling on each platform) tend to compound the estimate rather than simply sum it. This is precisely the kind of interaction effect a discovery phase is meant to surface before a number is committed to, and precisely what a same-day quote from a feature list has no way of catching.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder comparing three very different agency quotes) Why are mobile app development cost quotes for the same app so different between agencies?

Quotes usually differ because they cover different scope, not because of different pricing philosophies. A quote covering only the core build will always look cheaper than one covering design, QA, and post-launch stabilization — ask each vendor to itemize all four components before comparing numbers.

### (Scenario: founder with a fixed budget deciding what to build first) What's the minimum realistic budget for a mobile app in 2026?

A simple utility app with no real-time features or payment processing typically starts around €15,000-€20,000 for a genuinely production-ready build, including basic QA and app store submission — anything significantly lower usually means one of those components is missing.

### (Scenario: founder worried about hidden costs after signing) What app costs get missed most often in initial quotes?

Cross-device QA and the post-launch stabilization window are the two most commonly omitted line items, because they're hard to demo and easy to defer — until real users hit edge cases the demo never covered.

### (Scenario: founder deciding between one platform or two) Does building for both iOS and Android double the cost?

Not quite double if the backend and business logic are shared, but expect 50-70% more than a single-platform build once native UI work and separate app store submission processes are counted for both.

### (Scenario: founder budgeting for after launch) Should I budget for costs after the app ships?

Yes — a 2-4 week stabilization window to fix real-user bugs, plus ongoing maintenance of roughly 15-20% of the original build cost per year, is standard for a production app that will keep working as OS versions update.
