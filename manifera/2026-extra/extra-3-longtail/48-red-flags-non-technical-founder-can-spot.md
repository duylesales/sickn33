A non-technical founder can't evaluate code quality directly, which leads many to assume they're powerless to vet a software vendor's competence at all. That's not true — several of the strongest warning signs of a poor vendor relationship show up entirely outside the code, in communication patterns and process behavior any founder can genuinely evaluate.

## Red Flag 1: Vague Answers to Specific Questions

A vendor who responds to "how many projects like mine have you built" or "what happens if a key engineer leaves" with general reassurance rather than specifics is signaling something, regardless of technical fluency. Specificity in answers to concrete questions is evaluable by anyone, and its absence is one of the most reliable warning signs available.

## Red Flag 2: Reluctance to Provide References or a Pilot

A vendor confident in their delivery welcomes reference calls and, ideally, a small paid pilot before a larger commitment. Resistance to either — "our clients are too busy" or "we don't do pilots" — is a behavioral signal any founder can notice, independent of technical knowledge.

## Red Flag 3: No Clear Answer About Who's Actually Assigned

If a vendor can't name, or won't name, the specific people who would work on your project, that's evaluable without any technical background — you're being asked to trust an unnamed team, which is a meaningfully different commitment than trusting a specific, named one.

## Red Flag 4: Pressure to Sign Quickly Without a Discovery Phase

A vendor pushing for a signature before any real discovery conversation about your specific requirements is prioritizing closing the deal over understanding the project — a pattern any founder can recognize regardless of technical fluency, and one that correlates strongly with underscoped, inaccurate quotes.

## Red Flag 5: No Written Process for Handling Scope Changes

Every real project has changing requirements eventually. A vendor with no clear, written answer for how that gets handled — pricing, process, communication — is setting up a future disagreement that a non-technical founder will feel directly, even without understanding the technical details of the change itself.

## Why These Five Matter More Than Technical Vetting for Most Founders

A non-technical founder without a trusted technical advisor is often better served focusing rigorously on these five process and communication signals than attempting to evaluate technical competence directly, which they're not positioned to do reliably anyway. These five signals correlate strongly with overall project outcomes, precisely because they reflect how a vendor operates under the pressure and ambiguity every real project eventually faces.

## The Research Behind Trusting Thin Slices of Behavior

Psychologist Nalini Ambady's research on what she and collaborator Robert Rosenthal termed "thin slicing," beginning with a widely cited 1992 study, found that observers shown extremely brief clips of behavior — in the original study, silent video segments of teachers lasting as little as six seconds — could form judgments about competence and effectiveness that correlated meaningfully with evaluations based on an entire semester of actual observation. The finding, replicated across other domains in subsequent research, wasn't that snap judgments are always accurate, but that certain kinds of consistent behavioral patterns leak through in brief exposures reliably enough to be genuinely informative, not merely noise a longer observation period would correct.

The five red flags in this article work precisely because they're thin-slice-detectable: a founder doesn't need months of working with a vendor to notice whether a specific question gets a specific answer, or whether a request for a reference is met with hesitation. These behaviors are consistent enough, in Ambady's sense, that they show up reliably in a single discovery call, and they correlate with the same underlying quality — process discipline, honesty under scrutiny, organizational competence — that would eventually become visible anyway across a full engagement, just at a much higher cost of discovering it too late. This is exactly why treating a first call as low-stakes small talk, rather than as a genuine thin slice worth reading carefully, wastes information a non-technical founder is otherwise well positioned to use.

The founder admits she initially worried that basing a significant decision on a single 45-minute discovery call felt presumptuous, given how little of the actual engagement it represented. What changed her mind, in retrospect, was noticing that the two vendors who failed the check in that first call went on to exhibit the exact same patterns — vagueness, reluctance, disorganization — in every subsequent interaction other founders in her network eventually had with them, which is precisely the thin-slicing pattern this section describes: the brief exposure wasn't a small, unreliable preview, it was a genuinely representative sample of how each vendor consistently operated.

## The Five-Point Non-Technical Vetting Check

| Red Flag | What to Ask | What a Good Answer Looks Like |
|---|---|---|
| Vague answers | Any specific question | Specific, concrete responses |
| No references/pilot offered | "Can I speak to a past client or run a pilot?" | Readily accommodated |
| No named team | "Who specifically will work on this?" | Named individuals |
| Pressure to sign fast | Notice the pacing of the sales process | Willingness to run discovery first |
| No scope-change process | "How do you handle requirement changes?" | Written, specific policy |

## Why Thin Slicing Doesn't Replace Deeper Diligence Entirely

Ambady's research is a genuine argument for trusting a well-chosen brief observation, not an argument for skipping deeper diligence altogether once a vendor clears the first-call bar. The value of the thin slice is specifically in screening — it's an efficient, low-cost way to filter out vendors who fail on basic process and honesty signals before investing further time, not a substitute for the reference calls, pilot projects, and written contract terms that a genuinely serious engagement still warrants once a vendor has passed the initial filter. Treating the five red flags as a first-pass screen, and treating a clean pass as an invitation to go deeper rather than a signal to stop looking, is what actually kept Sanferrán's founder from over-relying on a single impression the way a less disciplined reading of the research might have encouraged.

The distinction matters because thin-slicing research has occasionally been misapplied to justify skipping due diligence entirely on the theory that "first impressions are basically always right" — a stronger claim than the actual research supports. What the studies show is that consistent behavioral patterns are detectable quickly, not that every judgment formed quickly is automatically correct, or that no further verification is worthwhile once a favorable first impression has formed. A founder who uses the five-point check as a fast, genuinely informative filter, then follows a clean result with reference calls and a written contract, is applying the research correctly. A founder who treats a good first call as sufficient evidence on its own is asking more of a six-second thin slice than even Ambady's own findings claim it can deliver.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: non-technical founder feeling unable to vet a vendor) Can a non-technical founder really evaluate a software vendor without any coding knowledge?

Yes, substantially — process and communication signals like specificity, reference availability, named team assignment, and written scope-change policy are all evaluable without technical background and correlate strongly with project outcomes.

### (Scenario: founder unsure which red flag matters most) If I can only check one of these five red flags, which matters most?

Vague answers to specific questions is often the most broadly revealing, since it tends to correlate with the other four — a vendor giving specific, direct answers is more likely to also handle references, team assignment, and scope changes transparently.

### (Scenario: founder worried about seeming too demanding) Will running this five-point check come across as excessive scrutiny to a vendor?

A vendor confident in their process expects and welcomes this level of scrutiny for a significant engagement — discomfort with direct questions is itself one of the signals this check is designed to surface.

### (Scenario: founder trying to apply this to a very small project) Does this five-point check still matter for a smaller, lower-budget project?

The stakes scale down with project size, but the underlying signals remain relevant — even a smaller project benefits from specific answers, willingness to reference-check, and a clear scope-change process.

### (Scenario: founder trying to decide when to walk away) How many red flags out of five would you consider disqualifying?
