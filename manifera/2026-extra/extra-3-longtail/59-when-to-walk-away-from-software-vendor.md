The money already spent with a struggling vendor feels like a reason to keep going — surely switching now means losing all of it. That reasoning is the sunk cost fallacy applied to software vendor relationships, and it keeps founders in unsalvageable engagements longer than the actual evidence justifies, compounding the eventual cost of switching rather than reducing it.

## Signals a Vendor Relationship Has Become Unsalvageable

- **Repeated missed milestones without a credible explanation or corrected plan** — one missed deadline with a clear reason and adjustment is normal project friction. A pattern of missed deadlines without honest diagnosis is a structural problem, not a temporary setback.
- **Deteriorating communication quality over time**, especially defensive or evasive responses to direct questions about progress or problems, rather than transparent status updates even when the news is bad.
- **Technical debt or quality issues discovered that the vendor was aware of and didn't disclose**, revealing a trust problem beyond the specific technical issue itself.
- **Scope and cost creep with no clear accounting for why**, especially when change orders can't be tied to genuine, documented requirement changes on the client's side.
- **Key team members leaving without a clear continuity plan**, particularly if this has happened more than once, suggesting an internal instability that will keep resurfacing.

## Why the Sunk Cost Reasoning Is Backwards

Money already spent is gone regardless of whether you continue or switch — it's not recoverable either way, which means it shouldn't factor into the forward-looking decision about whether continuing is likely to produce a good outcome from this point forward. The only relevant question is: given everything now known about this vendor's actual capability and reliability, is continuing the fastest, cheapest path to a working product, or is switching, despite feeling like a loss, actually the faster path from here?

## How to Actually Exit a Vendor Relationship With Minimal Additional Loss

- **Secure all code, documentation, and credentials** before initiating a difficult conversation, ensuring you retain what you're contractually entitled to regardless of how the exit conversation goes.
- **Commission an independent technical assessment** of the current state of the work, giving an incoming vendor (or yourself) an accurate picture of what actually exists, rather than relying on the outgoing vendor's own characterization.
- **Review your contract for IP assignment and termination clauses** before the conversation, understanding your actual legal position and leverage.
- **Communicate the decision directly and professionally**, focused on the specific evidence behind the decision rather than becoming personal, since a professional exit preserves the option of a clean handoff period if needed.

## The Research Behind Why This Bias Is So Persistent

Psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer formalized the sunk cost effect in a widely cited 1985 study, demonstrating experimentally that people systematically continue investing in a chosen course of action once they've made a prior investment in it, even when a neutral observer, presented with the same forward-looking facts but no knowledge of the prior investment, would clearly recommend stopping. In one of their central experiments, subjects who had already paid for a ski trip were more likely to go on it even after learning a better, non-refundable alternative trip had become available — the paid-for trip's sunk cost measurably distorted a decision that, from a purely forward-looking standpoint, had already been settled by the better alternative.

Arkes and Blumer's follow-up work identified a specific psychological mechanism behind the bias: abandoning a prior investment feels like admitting the original decision was wrong, and that felt admission of error carries a real emotional cost distinct from the financial one, which is precisely why the bias persists even among people who intellectually understand sunk costs shouldn't matter. A founder who has already spent €40,000 with a struggling vendor isn't just weighing money — they're weighing the psychologically uncomfortable act of admitting the original vendor choice was a mistake, and Arkes and Blumer's research suggests that discomfort, not the money itself, is often the more powerful force keeping founders in a relationship the evidence has already flagged as unsalvageable.

## Case Study: A Poznań Founder's Overdue Exit

A founder at Poznań-based startup Wartogród had continued with a struggling vendor for five additional months past the point where missed milestones had become a clear pattern, reasoning that the €40,000 already spent made switching feel like an unacceptable loss.

An independent assessment, ultimately commissioned by Manifera's Amsterdam team, found the existing codebase was in worse condition than the outgoing vendor's status reports had indicated, with several claimed "completed" features actually non-functional. The founder switched, and while the transition required a difficult month of rebuilding trust in the actual project state, the Vietnam pod delivered a working product within the timeline the outgoing vendor had originally promised but never met.

> *"I kept thinking about the money I'd already spent instead of asking whether staying would actually get me to a working product faster than switching would. It wouldn't have — I just hadn't looked at it that way."*
> — **Founder, Wartogród**

Looking back, the founder identifies the emotional discomfort of admitting the original vendor choice was wrong as the more accurate description of what kept him staying five months too long, not the €40,000 itself — a distinction he only recognized clearly after reading about the mechanism Arkes and Blumer's research describes.

## A Practical Debiasing Technique Borrowed From the Original Research

Arkes and Blumer's own follow-up experiments tested a specific debiasing technique that reduced the sunk cost effect's influence: explicitly reframing the decision as though a different, uninvolved person were making it fresh, with no history of prior investment. Applied to a struggling vendor relationship, this means literally asking: if I were a founder today, with no money already spent, evaluating this vendor's current track record and this specific alternative, which would I choose? Stripping away the prior investment from the mental framing of the question, even as a deliberate exercise, measurably reduced sunk-cost-driven decisions in Arkes and Blumer's experiments, and it's a specific, actionable technique a founder can apply directly rather than simply being told, unhelpfully, to "ignore sunk costs" as abstract advice.

## Why This Bias Is Rarely About the Money Itself

It's worth being explicit about the practical implication of Arkes and Blumer's finding, because it changes what kind of intervention actually helps. A founder who tells themselves "the money's already spent, it shouldn't matter" and still feels stuck isn't failing to grasp basic arithmetic — the resistance is emotional, rooted in the discomfort of self-admitted error, not a miscalculation that better math would fix. This is why simply repeating "sunk costs are sunk" rarely dislodges the bias on its own: it addresses the financial framing of the problem while leaving the actual psychological mechanism, the discomfort of admitting a wrong initial choice, completely untouched.

A more effective approach treats the admission of error as a separate, smaller decision to make peace with before the switching decision itself: explicitly naming, out loud or in writing, "the original vendor choice didn't work out" as a distinct, already-settled fact, separate from and prior to the forward-looking question of what to do next. Founders who make this separation explicitly, rather than letting the two questions blur together, tend to reach a clearer forward-looking decision faster, precisely because the emotional cost of the admission has already been paid deliberately, rather than being smuggled into the financial reasoning where it's much harder to identify and address directly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: founder feeling trapped by money already spent with a struggling vendor) How do I decide whether to continue with a struggling vendor or switch?

Evaluate the pattern of evidence — missed milestones, communication quality, disclosed versus undisclosed issues — against what continuing is likely to cost from this point forward, explicitly setting aside money already spent as irrelevant to that forward-looking decision.

### (Scenario: founder worried about losing everything by switching vendors) Will I lose all the work already done if I switch vendors?

Not necessarily — with proper IP assignment terms and access to existing code and documentation, an incoming vendor can typically build on usable existing work, though some re-assessment and possible rework is common depending on the quality of what exists.

### (Scenario: founder trying to prepare for a difficult vendor conversation) What should I do before telling a vendor I'm ending the relationship?

Secure all code, documentation, and credentials you're contractually entitled to, review your contract's termination and IP clauses, and ideally commission an independent assessment of the current work's actual state before the conversation.

### (Scenario: founder trying to distinguish normal friction from a real problem) How many missed milestones is too many before I should consider switching?

There's no universal number, but a pattern without credible diagnosis and correction — rather than an isolated miss with clear explanation — is the more important signal than the raw count alone.

### (Scenario: founder trying to understand the sunk cost trap) Why does money already spent feel so relevant to the decision, even though it logically shouldn't be?

Sunk cost reasoning is a well-documented cognitive bias — humans naturally weight past investment heavily even when it's not recoverable either way, which is exactly why deliberately setting it aside and focusing only on the forward-looking comparison helps make a clearer decision.
