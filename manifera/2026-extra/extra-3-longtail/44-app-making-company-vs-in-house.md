Founders often default to whichever option feels less scary in the moment rather than genuinely working through the decision itself — non-technical founders default to an app-making company because building in-house feels impossible, while technical founders default to in-house because outsourcing feels like giving up control. Neither default is genuinely wrong often enough to actually be a reliable rule; the actual right answer genuinely depends on a few specific factors worth working through deliberately and honestly.

## Question 1: Is Engineering Your Core Competitive Advantage?

If the product's genuine competitive edge is specifically and demonstrably technical — a novel algorithm, a proprietary data pipeline, deep technical IP — building in-house genuinely makes more sense once funding actually allows, since that specific expertise is core to what makes the company defensible at all. If the competitive edge is elsewhere — market timing, distribution, domain expertise, brand — engineering is more of an enabling function than the core differentiator, and an app-making company is often a perfectly appropriate way to build that enabling function well.

## Question 2: Do You Have the Technical Judgment to Manage In-House Engineering?

Building in-house without any genuine technical leadership on the founding team means either hiring a real technical co-founder or CTO first (a significant, often slow decision) or managing engineers you can't evaluate independently, which recreates many of the same trust and verification challenges as working with an outside company, without an established company's process infrastructure to fall back on.

## Question 3: What's Your Actual Timeline to Validate the Idea?

An app-making company can typically move genuinely faster to a real working product than assembling and onboarding an in-house team entirely from scratch, particularly for a founder without an existing technical network to recruit from. If speed to validation matters more than long-term ownership right now, that favors an outside partner for the initial build, with an option to bring engineering in-house later once the idea is validated and funded.

## Question 4: What Happens After the Initial Build?

This is, quite reliably, the question founders skip most often of the four. If the plan is ongoing, evolving product development, evaluate whether an app-making company offers an ongoing relationship model (like a dedicated team) versus a one-off project engagement that leaves you without support the moment requirements inevitably change post-launch.

## Treating the Build Decision as an Option, Not a Commitment

Real options theory, developed by economists including Avinash Dixit and Robert Pindyck in their influential 1994 work extending financial option pricing to real-world business decisions, offers a precise way to think about exactly this founder's dilemma. A financial option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to take an action later, and that right has real value even before it's exercised — the flexibility to wait for more information before committing capital is itself worth something, distinct from the value of the underlying decision. Real options theory applies the same logic to business choices: a decision that preserves future flexibility is more valuable than an equally good decision that forecloses it, even when both look identical on paper today.

Engaging an app-making company for an initial build, structured with a clear path to transition to in-house or a different engagement model later, is close to purchasing a real option: it validates the product with real capital spent, at the actual cost of that build, while explicitly preserving the flexibility to exercise a different choice — hiring in-house, switching providers, scaling the relationship — once genuinely more information exists about whether the product has found traction. Committing immediately to building an in-house team before any market validation exists, by contrast, forecloses that flexibility prematurely, locking in a significant fixed cost (salaries, equity, management overhead) before the information that would make that commitment clearly correct or clearly wrong has had any chance to arrive.

This reframes Sölvi's decision below not as "outsource versus build in-house" as a permanent, binary choice, but as "which path preserves more optionality relative to what I currently know." An outside build with a defined transition option, exercised only once real traction justified it, is precisely how real options theory predicts a rational decision-maker facing genuine uncertainty should behave — paying a modest premium for flexibility now, in exchange for a much larger avoided cost if the eventual answer, once the information actually arrives, turns out to favor a different path than the one committed to today.

## What Makes an Engagement Genuinely Preserve the Option

Not every app-making company engagement actually preserves the real option a founder is paying for — some structures effectively foreclose the transition just as thoroughly as building in-house prematurely would have, simply through a different mechanism. The specific features that preserve genuine optionality: full IP assignment to the founder's own company from day one, so there's no negotiation required later to actually own what was built; documentation and architectural decision records maintained throughout, so a future internal team or different vendor can pick up the codebase without reverse-engineering it first; and an engagement structure that doesn't penalize transitioning away, whether contractually or through a codebase deliberately built to require the original vendor's continued involvement.

A vendor engagement missing any of these specific features isn't offering a real option in Dixit and Pindyck's sense — it's offering what looks like flexibility upfront but actually forecloses it later, once a founder discovers the codebase is effectively locked to that vendor through undocumented decisions or ambiguous IP terms. Evaluating a prospective app-making company against these three features specifically, before signing, is the practical version of verifying that the "option" being purchased is genuine, not just a temporarily convincing appearance of one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: non-technical founder assuming they need a technical co-founder first) Do I need a technical co-founder before I can build my product?

Not necessarily — if engineering isn't your core competitive advantage and speed to validation matters, working with an established app-making company can be a legitimate path that doesn't require finding a technical co-founder first.

### (Scenario: technical founder defaulting to building everything in-house) Is it always better to build in-house if I have the technical skill to do it myself?

Not always — even technical founders benefit from evaluating whether their time is best spent on core product differentiation versus building enabling infrastructure that an outside team could deliver just as well, faster.

### (Scenario: founder trying to plan beyond the initial build) What should I ask an app-making company about what happens after the initial build ships?

Ask specifically whether they offer an ongoing relationship model, like a dedicated team, versus only one-off project engagements — this matters significantly once real user feedback starts generating new requirements post-launch.

### (Scenario: founder trying to decide when to transition from outside build to in-house) When does it make sense to transition from an outside partner to an in-house team?

Often once the product is validated, funded, and the founder has the resources to build in-house technical leadership — the transition doesn't have to happen immediately, and a good outside partner supports that transition rather than resisting it.

### (Scenario: founder worried about losing IP or control by outsourcing) Does building with an app-making company mean giving up ownership or control of my product?

No — a properly structured engagement includes full IP assignment to the founder's company, and the founder retains product direction throughout; what changes is who executes the technical work, not who owns the outcome.
