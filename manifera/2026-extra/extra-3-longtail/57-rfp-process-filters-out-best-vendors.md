A formal RFP process exists specifically to reduce vendor selection risk through real structure and genuine comparability — a genuinely good, well-intentioned instinct at its core. What it can quietly and unintentionally select for instead, if not designed carefully from the outset, is skill at writing compelling RFP responses, which correlates far more strongly with dedicated proposal-writing resources than it actually correlates with real delivery quality, and the two skill sets simply don't automatically travel together at all.

## Why RFP Responses Can Mislead

A large, well-resourced company with a dedicated internal proposal-writing team can reliably produce a polished, comprehensive RFP response regardless of whether their actual delivery teams are genuinely strong, merely mediocre, or badly overstretched at the time — proposal writing is a specific, entirely learnable skill set, cleanly separable from actual engineering delivery capability. A smaller, genuinely highly capable engineering team without dedicated proposal-writing resources can easily submit a comparatively weaker-looking written response despite actually being the stronger real choice for delivery, simply because writing compelling RFP prose isn't where their strength or limited resources happen to be concentrated.

## What Standard RFP Criteria Often Miss

- **Rehearsed, generic responses to standard questions** can score well against a scoring rubric without reflecting genuine understanding of a project's specific technical needs.
- **Team continuity and who actually does the work** is rarely captured by RFP criteria focused on company-level credentials, missing the reality that RFP-winning companies sometimes staff projects differently than their proposal team implies.
- **Communication quality under real project pressure** is essentially untestable through a written RFP process, which only reveals how well a company communicates in a low-pressure, prepared, one-directional format.
- **Genuine technical judgment and pushback capability** rarely surfaces in an RFP response, which is structurally biased toward agreeable, comprehensive-sounding answers rather than honest technical trade-off discussion.

## How to Correct for These Blind Spots Without Abandoning Structure

- **Include a genuine technical Q&A round** with the actual people who would work on the project, not just the proposal and sales team, as a required stage rather than an optional follow-up.
- **Require a genuine paid pilot or technical exercise** as part of the evaluation for finalists, testing actual real delivery capability rather than relying entirely on written responses.
- **Weight reference calls genuinely heavily**, and specifically request references matching your project's actual complexity profile, not just any reference the vendor happens to choose to offer.
- **Score responses for genuine specificity over sheer comprehensiveness**, since a response tailored specifically to your stated requirements is far more informative than one that reads as a template applied broadly across many RFPs.

## The Law Behind Why Metrics Get Gamed

Social scientist Donald T. Campbell articulated a principle in a 1976 paper that has since become known as Campbell's Law: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making, the more it becomes subject to corruption pressures, and the more it will distort and corrupt the very process it was intended to monitor. Campbell's original examples spanned standardized testing and social program evaluation, but the underlying mechanism is domain-general: once people know a specific measure determines an important outcome, some portion of their effort predictably shifts from improving the underlying reality the measure was designed to track toward improving the measure itself, and those two things reliably diverge once the shift is large enough.

An RFP scoring rubric is a quantitative indicator in exactly Campbell's sense, and a scored RFP process is precisely the kind of high-stakes decision-making context his law predicts will attract corruption pressure. A rubric that scores written response comprehensiveness and polish creates a direct incentive to invest in comprehensiveness and polish — which a well-resourced proposal-writing team can produce regardless of underlying delivery capability — rather than in the delivery capability the rubric was actually meant to proxy for. This is exactly the Baldenberg case: the written-response score had, in Campbell's terms, already begun to substitute for and diverge from what it was originally designed to measure, and the corrective pilot and technical Q&A rounds worked specifically because they introduced evaluation methods much harder to game through proposal-writing investment alone.

## Case Study: A Karlsruhe Manufacturer's Corrected RFP Process

Baldenberg Systems, a Karlsruhe-based manufacturer, ran an initial RFP for a supply chain platform that, scored purely on written response quality, ranked a large agency's polished but generic proposal above Manifera's more specific but less elaborately formatted response.

The procurement lead, aware of this exact blind spot, added a technical Q&A round and a two-week paid pilot for the top two scorers before final selection. The pilot revealed the highly-scored agency assigning a largely junior team inconsistent with their proposal's implied seniority, while Manifera's pilot demonstrated the specific technical depth its more modest written response hadn't fully conveyed. The final selection reversed the initial written-response ranking.

> *"Our RFP scoring almost picked the better writer instead of the better builder. The pilot round is what actually corrected for that."*
> — **Procurement Lead, Baldenberg Systems**

Baldenberg's procurement lead now explicitly names this as "Campbell's Law risk" in internal process documentation, treating any single scored metric with inherent suspicion and deliberately pairing it with at least one evaluation method that's structurally harder to optimize toward without also improving the actual underlying capability.

## Designing Metrics That Resist Gaming

Campbell's Law doesn't imply that quantitative scoring is useless — it implies that a single, gameable metric used in isolation, especially for a high-stakes decision, will predictably be optimized toward rather than toward the underlying quality it's meant to represent. The practical defense isn't abandoning structured scoring, but triangulating with methods that are harder to game specifically because they require the real underlying capability, not just its written description, to perform well — a live technical Q&A that a proposal-writing team can't answer on an engineering team's behalf, or a paid pilot that directly tests delivered work rather than a description of hypothetical delivered work.

## RFP Blind Spots and Corrections

| RFP Blind Spot | What It Misses | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Written response quality | Actual engineering delivery capability | Technical Q&A with real team members |
| Company-level credentials | Who specifically staffs the project | Named team confirmation, reference checks |
| Comprehensive, polished prose | Genuine fit for your specific requirements | Score for specificity, not just polish |
| No direct capability test | Real delivery quality | Paid pilot or technical exercise for finalists |

## Applying Campbell's Law Beyond Vendor Selection

The same reasoning is worth applying to how a procurement team scores itself, not just how it scores vendors. If a procurement department's own internal performance is measured primarily by "time to vendor selection" or "number of vendors evaluated," Campbell's Law predicts the team will optimize toward speed and volume, potentially at the direct expense of the deeper, more time-consuming evaluation methods — technical Q&A, paid pilots, thorough reference checks — that this article recommends. A procurement process redesigned to resist gaming on the vendor side can quietly reintroduce the exact same problem one level up, if the metrics used to evaluate the procurement function itself aren't examined with the same scrutiny.

Recognizing this compounding risk is part of why Baldenberg's revised process explicitly protects time for the pilot and technical Q&A stages in its own internal timeline targets, rather than treating them as optional steps that get cut first whenever schedule pressure builds — a direct, deliberate acknowledgment that a good evaluation method under time pressure tends to degrade back toward the faster, more gameable one it was designed to replace.

## Designing a More Accurate Procurement Process

Before finalizing an RFP scoring process, deliberately build in a technical Q&A round, a paid pilot exercise for finalists, and reference calls weighted genuinely heavily — the written proposal alone is a meaningfully weaker predictor of real delivery quality than most standard RFP scoring rubrics quietly assume. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: procurement lead designing a new RFP process) How do I design an RFP process that doesn't just select for the best proposal writers?

Include a technical Q&A round with actual project team members and a paid pilot exercise for finalists, weighting these more heavily than the written proposal's polish and comprehensiveness alone.

### (Scenario: CTO reviewing RFP responses that all sound similarly polished) How do I distinguish genuine technical depth from well-written but generic RFP responses?

Look for specificity tailored to your stated requirements rather than comprehensive, template-like language, and follow up with direct technical questions that a generic response wouldn't have anticipated.

### (Scenario: procurement team worried a pilot adds too much time to selection) Is it worth adding a paid pilot stage to an RFP process, given the extra time it takes?

Usually yes for significant engagements — the cost and time of a pilot is typically much smaller than the cost of selecting a vendor based on written response quality alone and discovering a capability mismatch mid-project.

### (Scenario: vendor evaluator trying to weight references correctly) How much weight should reference calls carry relative to the written RFP response?

Substantial weight, and specifically references matching your project's complexity — a written response reflects proposal-writing skill, while a reference call reflects actual delivered experience from a comparable engagement.

### (Scenario: procurement lead trying to prevent a large vendor from underdelivering) How do I prevent a large, well-resourced vendor from winning based on brand and proposal polish, then assigning a weaker team than implied?

Require named team confirmation as part of the contract, not just the proposal, and include a right to review or approve specific team member assignments before work begins.
