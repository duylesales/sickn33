Eight weeks versus five months — same scope, same rigor requirement, radically different pace. That was the gap a procurement lead at a European retail group was handed by her CIO for a new e-commerce development partner: "just move faster than last time," he told her, referencing a prior search that had dragged past five months. Eight weeks turned out to be enough time to run a competent process, but only because she cut the right corners rather than the wrong ones: she compressed the vendor long-list stage aggressively but refused to compress technical due diligence or reference checks, the two stages where rushing creates risk that surfaces months after signature, not before it.

"How long should this take" is one of the most common questions procurement leads face from leadership, and it rarely has a satisfying single-number answer, because the honest answer depends heavily on which stages get compressed and which do not. A rushed RFP that skips technical validation can move fast and still produce a bad decision. A properly paced RFP that protects the stages where real risk lives can move considerably faster than the traditional five-to-six-month enterprise procurement cycle without sacrificing diligence quality.

This article breaks the RFP process into its component stages with realistic timing for each, so a procurement lead facing pressure to compress the schedule knows exactly where compression is safe and where it is not.

## Stage One: Internal Requirements Definition — One to Two Weeks

Before any vendor sees a document, internal stakeholders need to agree on what is actually being procured — scope, budget range, must-have technical capabilities, and success criteria. Skipping or rushing this stage is the single most common cause of a slow, chaotic RFP downstream, because ambiguous requirements produce vendor proposals that are impossible to compare apples-to-apples, forcing a second round of clarification that costs far more time than the requirements-gathering stage would have taken upfront. Budget one to two weeks here, involving IT, finance, and the business sponsor directly rather than drafting requirements in isolation and circulating for sign-off after the fact.

## Stage Two: Vendor Long-List and RFP Distribution — One Week

Building an initial long-list of six to ten candidate vendors and distributing the RFP document itself is typically the fastest stage and the easiest to compress without risk, provided the requirements from stage one are genuinely clear. Use existing relationships, industry references, and a structured search rather than starting from zero, and give vendors a realistic but not excessive response window — two to three weeks is standard for a mid-market software development RFP, long enough for a serious vendor to prepare a thoughtful proposal without so long that momentum stalls.

## Stage Three: Proposal Review and Long-List Narrowing — One to Two Weeks

Once proposals arrive, this stage is where the long-list narrows to a workable shortlist, typically three to four finalists. This is a reasonable stage to compress if requirements were clear upfront, since a well-structured weighted scorecard applied consistently across proposals can narrow a field of eight to a shortlist of three in a matter of days rather than weeks. Resist the temptation to shortlist based purely on price at this stage — the point of narrowing here is eliminating clearly unqualified vendors, not making the final decision prematurely before deeper diligence has happened.

## Stage Four: Technical Due Diligence and Reference Checks — Two to Three Weeks, Do Not Compress

This is the stage where rushing creates the risk that surfaces months later, and it is the one procurement leads under schedule pressure are most tempted to cut. Live technical interviews with the actual proposed team, not just sales engineers, reference calls with at least two to three past clients on comparable projects, and security and compliance documentation review all belong here. A reference call rushed into a fifteen-minute box-checking exercise rarely surfaces the honest, specific detail that a properly scheduled thirty-to-forty-five-minute conversation does — and it is precisely in that extra detail that red flags about communication gaps, scope creep, or missed deadlines tend to surface. Budget two to three weeks here and treat any pressure to compress it as a signal to push back, not comply.

## Stage Five: Final Scoring and Internal Decision — One Week

With technical due diligence complete, applying the weighted scorecard and reaching a final internal decision should move quickly — roughly one week — provided the earlier stages were done properly. If this stage is dragging, it is usually a symptom that requirements were ambiguous at the start or that due diligence in stage four was insufficient to give stakeholders confidence, not a sign that the decision itself is genuinely difficult. Procurement leads should treat a stalled final decision as a diagnostic pointing backward to an earlier stage rather than a problem to solve by adding more meetings at this stage.

## Stage Six: Contract Negotiation and Sign-Off — One to Three Weeks

Contract negotiation timing varies more than any other stage depending on legal complexity, data processing agreement requirements, and how much the vendor's standard contract needs modification. Building in buffer here — particularly for engagements involving cross-border data transfer or specific compliance addenda relevant to [offshore software development](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) engagements — prevents a rushed contract stage from becoming the reason a well-run RFP produces a poorly protected agreement.

## Realistic Total Timeline and Where Compression Actually Helps

Adding these stages together, a well-run RFP process for a mid-market software development vendor decision realistically takes eight to twelve weeks end to end — considerably faster than the traditional five-to-six-month enterprise cycle, but not the two-to-three week sprint some leadership teams initially expect. The compression opportunity lives almost entirely in stages one through three and stage five: clear requirements upfront, an efficient long-list and scoring process, and a fast final decision once due diligence is genuinely complete. Stage four — technical due diligence and reference checks — is where compression should be resisted regardless of schedule pressure, because it is the stage that actually protects the business from a bad decision made quickly. ## Making the Final Call

The procurement lead who compressed her RFP from five months to eight weeks did not do it by rushing every stage equally — she did it by identifying which stages were genuinely time-sensitive risk points and which were simply slow by habit or lack of structure. A CIO asking "how long should this take" deserves an answer broken down by stage, not a single number, because the honest answer is that a fast RFP and a rigorous RFP are not actually in tension, provided the schedule pressure lands on the right stages.

If your current RFP process is taking five or six months without a clear sense of which stage is consuming the time, mapping it against this stage-by-stage structure is usually enough to find eight to twelve weeks of realistic schedule hiding inside a process that has simply never been timed deliberately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a typical software development vendor RFP process take?
A well-run process typically takes eight to twelve weeks end to end for a mid-market vendor decision, compared to the traditional five-to-six-month enterprise procurement cycle, provided requirements are clear upfront and technical due diligence is not rushed.

### Which stage of the RFP process should never be compressed?
Technical due diligence and reference checks, typically requiring two to three weeks, should not be compressed regardless of schedule pressure. This is the stage where rushing creates risks — communication gaps, scope creep, missed deadlines — that surface months after the contract is signed rather than before it.

### What is the most common cause of a slow RFP process?
Ambiguous or rushed internal requirements definition at the outset. Unclear requirements produce vendor proposals that cannot be compared apples-to-apples, forcing a slow second round of clarification that costs more time than proper upfront requirements gathering would have taken.

### How many vendors should be on the initial long-list?
Six to ten candidate vendors is a workable initial long-list, narrowed to three or four finalists after proposal review using a consistent weighted scorecard, before proceeding to full technical due diligence on the shortlist.

### What should a procurement lead do if the final decision stage is dragging?
Treat a stalled final decision as a signal pointing backward to an earlier stage rather than a sign the decision itself is genuinely difficult. It usually indicates requirements were ambiguous at the start or that technical due diligence did not give stakeholders enough confidence to decide quickly.
