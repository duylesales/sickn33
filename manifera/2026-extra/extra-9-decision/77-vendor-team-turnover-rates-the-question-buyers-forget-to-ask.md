An IT Manager evaluating three shortlisted vendors will typically ask about hourly rates, technology stack, time zone overlap, and reference clients. Almost none will ask the one question most predictive of whether the relationship survives its second year: what is your annual staff turnover rate, and what happens to my project when one of my named engineers leaves? It's an uncomfortable question to ask in a sales conversation, which is exactly why it gets skipped — and exactly why the vendors with something to hide are counting on that discomfort.

Turnover isn't a soft HR metric when it comes to a dedicated development team; it's an operational risk with a direct, measurable cost. Every engineer who rotates off your account takes institutional knowledge with them — architecture decisions, undocumented edge cases, the reasoning behind a particular integration choice — and every replacement engineer needs weeks of ramp-up before reaching the same productivity, during which your delivery velocity quietly drops even if the vendor keeps billing you at the same rate. This article covers why the question matters, how to actually ask it, and what contract clauses turn the answer into a real protection rather than a one-time reassurance.

## Why Turnover Is a Delivery Risk, Not Just an HR Statistic

When a named engineer leaves a project mid-engagement, the cost isn't limited to their replacement's salary — it's the ramp-up period during which the new engineer is billed at full rate while operating at a fraction of the departed engineer's productivity on your specific codebase. Industry estimates for onboarding a new engineer to full productivity on an established codebase commonly range from four to eight weeks depending on system complexity, and that ramp-up cost lands entirely on the client in most staff augmentation and dedicated team contracts, since you're still paying for the seat regardless of the learning curve happening inside it.

For an IT Manager managing budget accountability to a CIO or finance function, unplanned turnover on a vendor team is functionally equivalent to an unbudgeted re-hire — except it happens on the vendor's schedule, not yours, and you frequently have no advance warning it's coming. Global technology sector attrition benchmarks published by industry HR analysts have placed average annual turnover in offshore development markets anywhere from the low teens to over 25%, depending on region and specialization — a wide enough range that asking a specific vendor for their actual number, rather than accepting an industry average as a proxy, is the only way to know what you're actually signing up for.

## How to Actually Ask the Question in Vendor Evaluation

Don't accept a vague answer like "we have great retention" — ask for a specific number: annual attrition rate for the team category relevant to your engagement (not company-wide, which can dilute a high-turnover delivery team's number with a stable sales or admin staff figure), and ask how that number is calculated — voluntary departures only, or including internal transfers and involuntary terminations, which tell a different story about team stability. A vendor with a genuinely strong retention practice will have this number ready and will typically volunteer supporting context, such as average tenure of engineers on comparable long-term accounts.

It's also worth asking a more specific, harder-to-fake question: of the engineers currently on your prospective team, how long has each been with the company, and how long have they worked together as a unit. A team assembled the week before your kickoff call, regardless of individual credentials, carries different collaboration risk than a team that has delivered three prior projects together — the second group has already worked out its internal communication patterns, which matters more to your delivery velocity than most buyers initially assume.

## Benchmark Ranges and What "Good" Actually Looks Like

There's no single universal benchmark, but for context, well-run dedicated software teams with strong retention practices commonly report annual voluntary attrition in the 8% to 15% range, meaningfully below the wider offshore market figures cited above. Numbers meaningfully above that range — 30% or higher — deserve a direct follow-up conversation about root cause, not automatic disqualification, since some specializations genuinely see higher natural turnover. What matters most for your decision isn't the number in isolation but whether the vendor can explain it credibly and show what they're doing to actively manage it, such as defined career progression paths, above-market compensation benchmarking, or a bench deep enough to absorb departures without disrupting active accounts.

## Contract Clauses That Turn the Answer Into a Protection

Asking the question during evaluation is only half the discipline — the answer needs to be backed by contractual protections that survive beyond the sales conversation. Three clauses matter most: a defined replacement SLA specifying how quickly a departing engineer must be replaced with someone of equivalent seniority and how much overlap time (ideally two to four weeks) the outgoing and incoming engineers spend together for knowledge transfer; a named-resource clause guaranteeing the specific individuals you interviewed and approved are who actually works on your project, with advance notice required before any substitution; and a documentation-currency requirement obligating the team to maintain architecture and decision documentation continuously, not retroactively, so institutional knowledge doesn't live only in departed engineers' heads.

## Making the Final Call

Turnover will never be zero — treating it as a disqualifying factor rather than a risk to be managed and contracted around sets an unrealistic bar that eliminates otherwise strong vendors over a single honest number. What separates a manageable risk from a hidden one is whether the vendor answers the question directly with real figures, whether their retention practices are substantive rather than aspirational, and whether the contract includes replacement SLAs and overlap requirements that soften the impact when turnover inevitably happens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does vendor staff turnover matter more than it seems during evaluation?
Every engineer who leaves mid-project takes institutional knowledge with them, and their replacement typically needs four to eight weeks to reach equivalent productivity on your specific codebase — a cost that usually lands on you even though it wasn't budgeted or scheduled by you.

### What's a reasonable annual turnover rate to expect from a dedicated development team?
Well-run teams with strong retention practices commonly report annual voluntary attrition in the 8% to 15% range. Numbers meaningfully above 30% warrant a direct conversation about root cause rather than automatic disqualification, since some specializations naturally see higher turnover.

### What should I ask a vendor about turnover beyond just the overall rate?
Ask how the number is calculated (voluntary only, or including transfers and terminations), how long the specific engineers on your proposed team have been with the company, and how long that team has worked together as a unit — team cohesion matters as much as individual tenure.

### What contract clause protects against turnover disruption?
A replacement SLA is the key clause: it specifies how quickly a departing engineer must be replaced with equivalent seniority, and requires a defined overlap period — commonly two to four weeks — for direct knowledge transfer between the outgoing and incoming engineer.

### Should I disqualify a vendor with above-average turnover?
Not automatically. What matters more is whether the vendor answers with real numbers, explains the root cause credibly, and has substantive retention practices and contractual replacement protections in place — a vendor that's transparent about a higher number is often a safer bet than one that won't answer at all.
