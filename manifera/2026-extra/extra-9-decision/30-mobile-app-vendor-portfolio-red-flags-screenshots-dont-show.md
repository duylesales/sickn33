A polished portfolio page with twenty beautiful app screens tells you almost nothing about whether the vendor who built them can deliver your app on time, on budget, and still be reachable when something breaks eight months after launch. Founders evaluating mobile app vendors consistently over-index on visual polish because it is the easiest thing to evaluate at a glance — and it is also the easiest thing for a vendor to fake, borrow, or selectively curate. A screenshot shows you a finished UI. It does not show you the three previous clients who abandoned the relationship mid-project, the actual App Store rating those apps carry today, or whether the person who designed those screens is even still employed by the agency showing them to you.

This gap between what a portfolio displays and what it actually proves is exactly where founders get burned — not by outright fraud, usually, but by a portfolio that is technically accurate while being deeply unrepresentative of what your specific engagement will look like. This article walks through the specific red flags worth checking before you sign, and the verification steps that take fifteen minutes but save months of regret.

## Red Flag One: No Live App Store or Google Play Links

A legitimate, currently-maintained app should have a live, findable listing on the App Store or Google Play, and a vendor confident in their work will link to it directly rather than showing you an isolated screenshot or a Dribbble-style mockup. If a portfolio shows a polished UI with no accompanying store link, ask directly why. Reasonable answers exist — an enterprise internal tool with no public store presence, an NDA-protected pre-launch project — but a vendor with a portfolio full of screenshots and conspicuously few live links deserves a direct question, not an assumption of good faith.

When you do find a live link, check three things beyond the screenshots: the actual current star rating and review volume (not just the promotional quote a vendor pulled for their own site), the date of the most recent update (an app untouched for eighteen months suggests either an abandoned client relationship or a product that failed — worth knowing either way), and whether the listed developer name on the store matches the client company, not the agency — which tells you whether the agency actually delivered ownership of the finished product, a detail that matters enormously for your own engagement.

## Red Flag Two: Case Studies With No Numbers

A real case study describes a specific problem, a specific technical approach, and a specific measurable outcome — user growth, performance improvement, a launch timeline actually hit. A portfolio piece that describes only "we built a beautiful, intuitive app for [Client]" with no numbers attached is not a case study; it is marketing copy dressed as one. Ask a vendor directly, for two or three of their strongest portfolio pieces, what the original timeline estimate was versus actual delivery, and what technical challenge came up mid-project that required a real solution. A vendor who has actually done the work will answer this specifically and often enthusiastically, because engineers generally like discussing real problems they solved. A vendor reciting only the polished marketing version will visibly struggle to go one layer deeper.

## Red Flag Three: A Portfolio Spanning Every Industry With No Depth in Any

A portfolio showing a fintech app, a healthcare app, a logistics platform, a dating app, and a B2B SaaS dashboard, all displayed with equal prominence and equal confidence, is not necessarily a lie — but it should raise a specific question: how deep is the actual expertise behind each one, versus how broad is the marketing claim? A vendor genuinely skilled across that many distinct domains, each with its own regulatory, UX, and technical patterns, is unusual. More often, a broad portfolio reflects a generalist team capable of competent execution but without the deep domain pattern-recognition that comes from having built multiple products in the same space — the kind of experience that catches a fintech-specific compliance issue or a healthcare-specific data flow problem before it becomes expensive.

If your project sits in a specific, demanding domain, ask how many of the vendor's total delivered projects — not just the ones shown in the portfolio — were in that same domain. A portfolio can display three great examples out of three hundred total projects, which is a very different depth signal than three examples out of fifteen.

## Red Flag Four: Team Photos and Bios That Don't Match Who You'll Actually Work With

Many agency websites feature a polished "our team" page with senior-looking engineers and designers — and then staff your actual project with a completely different, often more junior, team once the contract is signed. This is one of the most common and most damaging portfolio-adjacent red flags, because it is not caught until you are already committed. Ask directly, before signing: "Who specifically, by name and role, will be working on my project, and can I see their individual portfolio contributions, not just the agency's collective portfolio?" A vendor who can answer this concretely, and who is willing to have a short call with the actual assigned lead engineer before contract signature, is showing you real staffing transparency. A vendor who deflects this to "our whole team collaborates" without naming individuals is preserving flexibility to staff you however is convenient for their utilization, not what was implied by the portfolio that sold you.

## Red Flag Five: References Who Are All Recent and All Glowing

A reference list of exclusively recent clients, all offering uniformly enthusiastic feedback, is a weaker signal than it appears. Ask instead for one reference from a project that is at least two years old — this tells you whether the app the vendor built is still functioning well, still maintained if that relationship continued, and whether the client would make the same vendor choice again with the benefit of hindsight rather than the fresh optimism of a just-completed project. A vendor confident in their long-term work will have no hesitation connecting you with an older client; one who only offers recent references may be managing which relationships have soured enough to no longer serve as a positive reference.

## Making the Final Call

A portfolio is a marketing artifact, curated by definition, and treating it as sufficient due diligence is one of the most common and costly mistakes a founder makes when selecting a mobile app vendor. The verification steps above — live store links checked independently, specific numbers behind case studies, honest domain-depth questions, named staffing commitments, and an older reference — take under an hour combined and reveal far more than another round of portfolio browsing ever will.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I verify a mobile app vendor's portfolio is genuine?
Look up the live App Store or Google Play listing for portfolio apps directly rather than relying on screenshots, and check the current star rating, review volume, and last update date. Also confirm the store's listed developer name matches the client company, which tells you whether the agency actually delivered full product ownership.

### What should I ask about a vendor's case studies?
Ask for the original timeline estimate versus actual delivery, and a specific technical challenge that arose mid-project. A vendor who genuinely did the work answers concretely; a vendor reciting only polished marketing language will struggle to go deeper.

### Is it a red flag if a vendor's portfolio spans many different industries?
Not automatically, but it warrants a direct question about depth versus breadth. Ask how many of the vendor's total delivered projects — not just the portfolio highlights — were in your specific domain, since a broad portfolio can reflect thin coverage in any one area.

### Why should I ask who specifically will work on my project?
Many agencies feature senior team photos in marketing materials but staff actual projects with a different, often more junior, team after signing. Asking for named engineers and a call with the actual assigned lead before signature prevents this mismatch from being discovered only after commitment.

### Why ask for an older reference instead of just recent ones?
A reference from a project at least two years old reveals whether the app is still functioning well and whether the client would choose the same vendor again with hindsight, which is a stronger signal than the fresh enthusiasm of a just-completed engagement.
