Source code escrow costs a few thousand euros a year to maintain. Losing access to a production codebase because a vendor collapsed mid-contract can cost a company months of rebuild time and the institutional knowledge behind a year's worth of undocumented decisions — a bill with no fixed ceiling. That asymmetry is the entire argument for business continuity planning in a vendor contract, and it's why the single most important question in a final sign-off meeting is rarely about pricing or delivery speed: it's what happens to your codebase, your data, and your roadmap on the day the vendor's bank account hits zero. A signed multi-year development contract is, financially, a bet on a counterparty's continued existence — and counterparties do fail, quietly and without much warning.

This is not a hypothetical risk reserved for struggling startups. Small and mid-sized development shops close, get acquired and restructured, or lose key leadership at rates that rarely make headlines but regularly disrupt client roadmaps. A CFO signing off on a vendor contract of any meaningful size is underwriting a relationship risk that deserves the same rigor applied to any other material counterparty exposure on the balance sheet — yet it is frequently the least scrutinized line item in the entire vendor selection process.

This article assumes your technical evaluation is essentially done and the deal is close to signature. What remains is the financial and continuity due diligence that protects the business if the vendor you are about to commit to does not survive the length of the engagement — and the specific contract mechanisms that turn that risk from existential to merely inconvenient.

## Why Vendor Financial Health Deserves the Same Scrutiny as Any Supplier

Procurement teams routinely run credit checks and financial stability reviews on hardware suppliers and logistics partners, yet a software development vendor holding your entire codebase, institutional knowledge, and roadmap continuity often gets a lighter financial review than a company supplying office furniture. That asymmetry does not match the actual risk exposure. A vendor failure mid-engagement does not just delay a project — it can mean losing access to source code, documentation, and the only engineers who understand a year of undocumented decisions, all at once.

Request basic financial transparency as a standard part of final due diligence: years in operation, revenue trend if the vendor is willing to share it, client retention rate, and whether the vendor is bootstrapped, VC-backed, or private-equity owned, since each ownership structure carries a different continuity risk profile. A VC-backed vendor burning cash toward a funding cliff carries a materially different risk than a profitable, decade-old bootstrapped shop, even if their proposals look identical on a slide.

## Source Code Escrow: The Mechanism Most Contracts Skip

The single most concrete protection against vendor failure is a source code escrow arrangement — a third-party escrow agent holds a current, verified copy of your codebase, build scripts, and deployment documentation, released to you automatically if the vendor ceases operations, fails to maintain the software as contracted, or enters insolvency proceedings. Without escrow, a vendor's sudden closure can leave a client with a production system they cannot legally or technically maintain, because the only complete, current copy of the code and its build process lived entirely inside the vendor's own infrastructure.

A properly structured escrow agreement specifies verification: the escrow agent should periodically confirm the deposited materials actually build and run, not just that a zip file was uploaded once and forgotten. CFOs should treat escrow as a non-negotiable line item for any engagement exceeding roughly six months in duration or handling any system classified as business-critical, and should push back on any vendor who treats the request as unusual or unnecessary — a vendor confident in its own stability has no real reason to resist it.

## Transition Assistance Clauses That Actually Work

Escrow protects the code; a transition assistance clause protects the operational handoff. This clause should obligate the vendor, or its successor entity in an acquisition scenario, to provide a defined period of documented knowledge transfer, access to key personnel, and infrastructure handoff support if the contract ends for any reason, including vendor insolvency, within a specified window — typically 30 to 90 days depending on system complexity. Without this clause, even a code escrow release leaves you holding a codebase with no map of how it actually runs in production.

Negotiate specificity into this clause rather than accepting boilerplate language: name the deliverables (architecture documentation, deployment runbooks, credential handoff procedures), name a minimum number of hours of key-personnel availability, and specify what happens to any outstanding work-in-progress. A vendor unwilling to commit to specifics here is signaling, in practice, how seriously they take continuity planning generally.

## Reading the Warning Signs Before the Contract, Not After

Financial distress rarely announces itself directly, but it leaves detectable patterns during the sales and onboarding process: unusual eagerness to lock in long payment terms upfront, sudden staff turnover on your proposed team between the pitch and kickoff, a reluctance to provide client references from engagements longer than a year, or a parent company structure that has changed more than once in recent history. None of these alone is disqualifying, but a CFO running due diligence should treat a cluster of two or more as a reason to slow down and ask directly, rather than assume the sales team will volunteer the information unprompted.

It is also worth asking directly about the vendor's own succession and continuity planning — who runs the business if a founder or key executive departs, and whether the delivery model depends on a small number of irreplaceable individuals or a genuinely distributed team structure. A [dedicated development team](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) model built around institutional processes rather than a handful of star engineers is inherently more resilient to individual departures than a boutique shop where all client knowledge sits with one or two people.

## What a Financially Resilient Vendor Structure Looks Like

The most durable vendors share a few structural traits: profitability or a long operating runway rather than dependence on the next funding round, a diversified client base where no single account represents an outsized share of revenue, low key-person dependency through documented processes and cross-trained teams, and a track record measured in years rather than a single funding cycle's worth of growth. These are the traits worth verifying directly rather than inferring from a polished pitch deck. ## Making the Final Call

No amount of continuity planning eliminates vendor risk entirely — every counterparty relationship carries some exposure, and a CFO's job is not to demand zero risk but to price it correctly and contract around it deliberately. A vendor with strong financials, a demonstrated multi-year track record, and a willingness to sign escrow and transition assistance clauses without resistance is a fundamentally different risk profile than one who treats these requests as friction to be negotiated away.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is source code escrow and why does a CFO need to require it?
Source code escrow is an arrangement where a third-party agent holds a verified, current copy of the vendor's codebase and build documentation, released to the client if the vendor ceases operations or breaches maintenance obligations. It is the primary protection against losing access to a production system if a vendor fails.

### What financial information should we request from a software vendor before signing?
Ask for years in operation, client retention rate, ownership structure (bootstrapped, VC-backed, or private equity), and willingness to share basic financial stability indicators. This is standard supplier due diligence that is often skipped for software vendors despite the risk they carry.

### What should a transition assistance clause specifically include?
It should name specific deliverables such as architecture documentation and deployment runbooks, commit a minimum number of hours of key-personnel availability, and define a specific handoff window, typically 30 to 90 days, that applies even if the contract ends through vendor insolvency.

### What are early warning signs a software vendor may be financially unstable?
Watch for unusual eagerness to lock in long upfront payment terms, staff turnover on your assigned team between pitch and kickoff, reluctance to provide references from engagements over a year, and a parent company structure that has changed more than once recently.

### Does a larger vendor automatically carry less business continuity risk?
Not necessarily. Size matters less than profitability, client base diversification, and low key-person dependency. A smaller, profitable, process-driven vendor can carry lower continuity risk than a larger vendor burning cash toward a funding milestone.
