Three browser tabs. Three finalist proposals for a Rotterdam logistics company's development contract. A dozen half-finished rows in a comparison spreadsheet, and a Friday deadline to recommend one to the VP. Every proposal claimed strong Agile process. Every proposal showed a polished portfolio. Every day rate sat within 12% of the others. The IT manager running the evaluation had spent three weeks on legitimate diligence and still could not tell, with any real confidence, which vendor deserved the recommendation — because nobody had told her how to weight the criteria against each other before the numbers arrived.

This is the exact moment where software development partner selection breaks down for most IT managers: not from a lack of information, but from too much unweighted information arriving all at once. Price, process, and portfolio all matter, but they do not matter equally, and treating them as equally important is how organizations end up picking the vendor with the best-designed proposal document instead of the best actual fit for the project at hand. What follows is a concrete, repeatable method for weighting these three factors correctly, built around the sequence you should actually follow rather than the sequence most evaluations default to by habit.

Most evaluation templates ask you to score price, process, and portfolio on the same 1-to-5 scale and then simply add the numbers, as if a strong portfolio and a strong process were interchangeable sources of confidence. They are not. A weak process with a beautiful portfolio is a liability waiting to surface in your third sprint. A strong process attached to a thin portfolio is often the safer bet, particularly for a project type the vendor has not showcased publicly but can clearly execute. The five steps below correct for this by forcing you to decide, deliberately and in writing, which factor should carry the most weight before you ever see a proposal's specific numbers.

## Step 1: Rank Your Real Constraint Before Opening Any Proposal

Before comparing a single vendor, identify which of the three factors — price, process, or portfolio — is your actual binding constraint, because exactly one of them usually is, even when it feels like all three matter equally. If your budget was fixed by finance before the search began, price is not really a variable to weight; it is a filter, and any vendor above your ceiling should be eliminated before scoring begins rather than scored down gently. If your constraint is a hard compliance requirement or an existing legacy stack that only certain engineering teams can safely touch, process maturity and technical fit outweigh price by a wide margin, because the cost of a compliance failure or a broken legacy integration dwarfs any day-rate savings.

Naming this constraint explicitly, in writing, before you open a single proposal prevents the most common failure mode in the entire evaluation: quietly re-weighting your criteria mid-process because one vendor's pricing looked unexpectedly attractive, or one vendor's sales representative was unusually persuasive on a call. Write the constraint down, share it with whoever else is involved in the decision, and treat any deviation from it during scoring as a flag worth discussing openly rather than adjusting silently.

## Step 2: Separate Process Claims From Process Evidence

Every finalist will describe an Agile process, sprint cadence, and code review discipline in nearly identical language, because these are now table-stakes claims rather than real differentiators — nobody submits a proposal admitting their process is chaotic. The selection step that actually separates vendors is asking for evidence rather than description: a sample sprint board, a real, redacted code review thread, or a walkthrough of how a specific past project handled a mid-sprint requirement change from a client. A vendor that can produce this evidence within a day has an actual functioning process they can point to on demand. A vendor that needs a week to "put something together" is very possibly assembling a process retroactively for your benefit, which tells you plenty about what daily delivery would actually look like once the contract is signed and the sales team steps back.

Push for specificity on one more point: how does the vendor handle a disagreement between the product owner and the development team over how a ticket should be implemented? A mature process has a named resolution path — usually the technical lead makes the final call within an agreed timeframe, with the reasoning documented on the ticket itself. A vendor who has never had to think about this question has probably not run enough real projects to have hit it yet, or has not run them with enough process rigor to notice when it happened.

It is also worth asking how retrospectives actually change anything. A vendor that runs a sprint retrospective every two weeks but cannot point to a single process change that resulted from one in the last six months is going through the motions of Agile ceremony without the underlying discipline the ceremony is meant to produce.

## Step 3: Read the Portfolio for Relevance, Not Polish

A portfolio full of visually striking case studies is the easiest thing for any vendor to produce and the least reliable signal in the entire evaluation, because a slick case study says nothing about whether the underlying project shipped on time, stayed within budget, or required a partial rebuild eighteen months later once the original team moved on. Weight portfolio evidence by relevance to your specific technical context — same tech stack, similar scale, comparable regulatory environment — over general visual impressiveness or brand-name client logos.

Ask each finalist for one portfolio example that is deliberately close to your own project's complexity, and ask what went wrong on it, not just what went right. A vendor willing to discuss a genuine complication in a past project, and specifically how they resolved it, is showing you more useful information than any highlight reel could. If a vendor can only offer polished success stories with no acknowledged friction anywhere, treat that as an incomplete picture rather than a spotless one — every real project has at least one moment of friction worth learning from.

Where possible, ask to speak directly with a technical lead who worked on the relevant portfolio project, not just an account manager summarizing it secondhand. The difference in the level of detail you get back — specific architectural decisions and trade-offs versus a general summary of business outcomes — is itself a useful signal about how close to the actual delivery work you are being allowed to get during the sales process.

## Step 4: Build a Weighted Scorecard Before the Final Call

With your binding constraint identified and evidence gathered on process and portfolio, assign numeric weights to each of the three factors that reflect Step 1's finding — for example, 25% price, 45% process evidence, 30% portfolio relevance for a compliance-sensitive legacy modernization project, or a very different split, perhaps 40% price and 25% each for process and portfolio, for a budget-constrained MVP build where speed and cost dominate. Score each finalist from 1 to 5 on each weighted factor, using the specific evidence gathered in Steps 2 and 3 rather than a general impression, and calculate a composite score before your final internal call takes place.

This single step removes the single largest source of bias in software development partner selection: the tendency to retroactively justify a decision that was actually made on gut feel during the most recent, most memorable sales conversation. It also gives you something concrete to bring to a skeptical stakeholder — a finance lead or a VP who was not in the room for the demos but needs to sign off on the spend.

## Step 5: Pressure-Test the Winner's Communication and Flexibility

Before signing, run one additional check on your leading finalist specifically: confirm working-hours overlap with your team, response-time commitments for blocked tickets, and how team size can flex if the project scope changes six months into the engagement. Flexibility should also be verified rather than assumed: ask specifically how quickly the team can scale from three engineers to six, and back down again, since project scope rarely stays static across a full engagement — priorities shift, budgets get revisited, and a rigid team structure becomes a constraint of its own. It is also worth reviewing how the vendor documents its [way of working](https://www.manifera.com/about-us/our-way-of-working/) more broadly, since that page-level detail often reveals more about day-to-day discipline than a sales proposal ever will.

## A Note on Red Flags That Override Any Score

A weighted scorecard is a strong decision-making tool, but it should not be treated as infallible. Certain findings during the evaluation are serious enough to override an otherwise strong composite score, and it is worth naming them before you start scoring so you do not talk yourself past them later. A finalist that will not provide a single reference from a client with a comparable technical stack is one such flag. A finalist that becomes evasive when asked what happens to your source code and data if the engagement ends early is another. And a finalist whose quoted day rate seems meaningfully below every other proposal, with no clear explanation of what is different about their cost structure, deserves closer scrutiny rather than automatic credit for being the cheapest option on the table.

None of these red flags are automatically disqualifying in isolation, but each one should prompt a direct follow-up question before you proceed, and a pattern of two or more should weigh heavily against an otherwise attractive composite score. As Gartner has noted in guidance on vendor risk management, unresolved due-diligence gaps at the selection stage are consistently more predictable of downstream contract disputes than any single pricing or technical factor.

## Making Software Development Partner Selection a Repeatable Process

The IT manager in the opening scenario eventually built exactly this kind of weighted scorecard, and the recommendation she brought to her VP on Friday included a composite score, a documented rationale for the weighting, and specific evidence behind each finalist's process and portfolio scores — not a narrative preference dressed up as analysis. That distinction matters beyond this one decision: a repeatable, weighted method for software development partner selection means the next evaluation, six months or two years from now, does not start from zero. It becomes an organizational capability that outlives whoever happened to run this particular search, rather than a one-time scramble against a Friday deadline.

There is a secondary benefit worth naming directly: a documented, evidence-based process also protects the relationship with the vendor you eventually select. When both sides know the decision was made against explicit, weighted criteria rather than an impression from a single sales call, the working relationship starts from a foundation of mutual clarity about what mattered and why — which tends to reduce the kind of misaligned expectations that quietly sour vendor relationships within the first two quarters.

Treat price, process, and portfolio as three separate questions with three separate weights, gathered through evidence rather than description, and the final decision stops being a coin flip between similarly polished proposals. It becomes a documented, defensible choice — the kind that holds up when someone asks about it a year later, and the kind that makes the next evaluation faster because the framework already exists.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should I weight price versus process in software development partner selection?
Weighting should reflect your actual binding constraint rather than a default split. If your budget is fixed by finance, price becomes a filter rather than a weighted factor, and process maturity should carry the heavier weight in your scoring, particularly for compliance-sensitive or legacy modernization projects.

### What is the best way to verify a vendor's Agile process claims?
Ask for concrete evidence rather than a description — a sample sprint board, a redacted code review thread, or a specific account of how a past project handled a mid-sprint scope change. A vendor that produces this quickly has a functioning process; one that needs days to assemble it likely does not.

### How much should portfolio examples influence the final decision?
Portfolio relevance to your specific technical context and scale matters far more than visual polish. Ask for one example close to your project's complexity and ask what went wrong on it, since that reveals more about real delivery capability than a highlight reel.

### Why does a weighted scorecard matter more than a narrative recommendation?
A weighted scorecard removes retroactive bias, where a decision made on gut feel during a memorable sales call gets justified afterward with selective reasoning. It also creates a documented rationale you can reference if the engagement is questioned later.

### How important is timezone overlap in software development partner selection?
It is a practical, high-impact factor rather than a nice-to-have, since a confirmed daily overlap window with your core team directly affects how quickly blockers get resolved. A four-to-five-hour overlap between Vietnam and Central European Time is generally sufficient for daily standups and same-day escalation.
