How do you evaluate a software vendor's engineering quality when you can't read a single line of their code? It's one of the most uncomfortable questions a non-technical founder faces before signing a development contract, and the software market offers almost no reliable shortcut. Anyone can present a polished portfolio, quote a competitive day rate, and speak confidently about "agile" and "full-stack" in a sales call. What separates a vendor who will actually deliver from one who won't rarely shows up in the pitch — it shows up in the technical due diligence most founders skip because they don't know what to ask.

If you're a non-technical founder about to approve a new software vendor for a project that matters to your business, this checklist is built specifically so you don't need to read code yourself to use it. Each item below tells you what to ask, why it matters, and what a real answer sounds like versus a rehearsed one.

## 1. Ask to See Code From a Project Similar to Yours — Not Just a Portfolio Screenshot

Portfolio pages show finished products, polished and presented at their best. They tell you almost nothing about code quality, maintainability, or how the team actually works. Ask instead for a short, recorded walkthrough of a codebase from a comparable past project — not necessarily proprietary client code, but enough to see how the team structures a real application. A vendor confident in their engineering standards will do this without hesitation. One who stalls, redirects to "we can't share client code" without offering any alternative, or only ever shows you marketing screenshots is giving you a meaningful signal.

## 2. Verify the Actual Team, Not the Sales Team

The person who impresses you in the sales process is frequently not the person who will build your software. This is one of the most common sources of founder disappointment in vendor relationships — a compelling initial pitch followed by a handoff to a team you never vetted. Ask specifically to meet the lead engineer and project manager who will actually work on your account before signing, and ask how long they've been with the company. Team stability matters more than most founders realize: a vendor with high engineer turnover will struggle to maintain institutional knowledge about your project even if each individual engineer is skilled.

This is a specific area where a vendor's structural model matters. A team offering [dedicated offshore development](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) with a defined onboarding process — interview, team selection, kickoff, and first sprint within a set number of weeks — gives you visibility into exactly who you're getting and when, rather than a vague promise that "someone qualified" will be assigned once the contract is signed.

## 3. Ask How They Handle a Disagreement About Scope

Every software project encounters a moment where the client and the vendor see scope differently — a feature the client assumed was included, an edge case the vendor considers "additional work." How a vendor answers this question before you've signed anything reveals more than almost any other single question on this list. A vendor who describes a specific, structured change-request process — written scope documentation, a defined process for evaluating and pricing changes, sign-off before work proceeds — is telling you they've dealt with this professionally before. A vendor who says "we'll just figure it out as we go" is telling you that scope disputes will be resolved informally, which usually means in whoever's favor holds more leverage at the time.

## 4. Ask for a Reference You Didn't Request

Any vendor can produce two or three glowing references when asked directly — these are typically the client relationships that went smoothest. A more revealing request is to ask the vendor to introduce you to a client whose project encountered a real problem along the way, and how it was resolved. A vendor with genuine confidence in their process will have an example ready, because problems happen in every long software engagement and a mature vendor treats problem resolution as part of their track record, not something to hide. Evasiveness here is a stronger red flag than any single negative reference would be.

## 5. Understand Their Testing and Quality Process in Concrete Terms

"We do thorough testing" is meaningless without specifics. Ask what testing actually happens before code reaches you — automated unit tests, end-to-end testing, manual QA, or some combination — and ask to see an example of a test report or bug-tracking process from a past project. A vendor with a genuine quality process can describe it in detail without hesitation: what gets automated, what gets manually reviewed, and how defects are tracked and prioritized. This matters enormously for the founder because software quality problems that aren't caught before delivery become your problem, discovered by your users, at the worst possible time.

## 6. Ask What Happens If You Need to Scale the Team Up or Down

Business needs change, and a rigid vendor relationship that only works at one fixed team size creates real strategic risk. Ask directly: if your project needs twice the engineering capacity in three months, how quickly can that happen, and what does it cost? Conversely, ask what happens if you need to scale down after a milestone is reached. A vendor who can describe a flexible engagement model — scaling a team up or down within a defined timeframe, without punitive contract terms — gives you strategic optionality that a rigid, fixed-team vendor cannot.

## 7. Confirm Who Owns the Code, In Writing, Before You Start

This should be uncontroversial, but it is worth explicit verification: your contract should state clearly, in plain language your own lawyer has reviewed, that you own all code, documentation, and intellectual property produced under the engagement, with no ambiguity about reusable components, libraries, or frameworks the vendor might claim partial ownership over. Founders who skip this step because "of course we own what we pay for" sometimes discover otherwise when they try to switch vendors later and find licensing terms buried in a master services agreement they never had reviewed carefully.

## 8. Ask About Communication Cadence and Time Zone Overlap Specifically

Vague assurances about "great communication" mean little without specifics. Ask what the actual weekly cadence looks like — daily standups, sprint reviews, a dedicated Slack channel — and how many hours of real-time overlap you'll have with the team given time zone differences. This is one area where the structural model of a vendor genuinely matters: a team built as a bridge between European business standards and APAC development velocity, with meaningful daily overlap between Central European and Southeast Asian working hours, solves a problem that a team split across incompatible time zones simply cannot solve through good intentions alone.

## Why This Matters More at the Decision Stage Than Earlier

Everything on this list is harder to fix after signing than before. A founder who discovers a code ownership ambiguity, a rigid team structure, or a weak testing process three months into a contract is negotiating from a position of sunk cost — the project is already underway, switching vendors mid-stream is expensive and disruptive, and leverage has shifted toward the vendor. This is exactly why technical due diligence belongs at the final approval stage, applied specifically to the vendor you're about to sign, rather than treated as a general research exercise completed earlier in your search.

It's worth noting that a vendor offering genuinely full-stack capability — frontend, backend, DevOps, and QA under one accountable team — tends to reduce a specific category of risk that this checklist can't fully capture through questions alone: the risk of finger-pointing between separate vendors when something breaks at the boundary between, say, your application code and your infrastructure. When one team owns the whole delivery pipeline from analysis through deployment and maintenance, due diligence questions about testing, scaling, and communication all get answered by the same accountable group, rather than by whichever vendor happens to be easiest to reach that week.

## Putting the Checklist to Work

You don't need to run through all eight items in a single conversation, and you don't need to treat every hesitant answer as disqualifying on its own — a vendor might have a perfectly reasonable explanation for any single point above. What matters is the pattern across all eight. A vendor who answers most of these specifically, confidently, and with concrete examples is showing you exactly the kind of transparency a non-technical founder should be looking for. A vendor who answers most of them vaguely, defensively, or with reassurance rather than specifics is showing you something too.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is technical due diligence for a software vendor?
Technical due diligence is the process of verifying a software vendor's actual engineering capability, team composition, and delivery process before signing a contract, rather than relying solely on sales presentations and portfolio materials. For non-technical founders, it means asking specific, concrete questions designed to reveal genuine practices rather than accepting vague reassurances.

### How can a non-technical founder evaluate a software vendor's code quality?
A non-technical founder can ask for a recorded walkthrough of code from a comparable past project, ask about specific testing practices like automated unit tests and QA processes, and ask for examples of how defects were tracked and resolved. The specificity and confidence of the vendor's answers reveal more than the technical details themselves.

### What questions reveal whether a software vendor will actually deliver as promised?
Questions about how the vendor handles scope disagreements, what happens when a reference project encountered a real problem, and who specifically will work on your account reveal far more than general capability questions. Vague or evasive answers to these specific questions are a stronger signal than any single glowing reference.

### Why does team stability matter when evaluating a software vendor?
High engineer turnover at a vendor makes it difficult to maintain institutional knowledge about your specific project, even when individual engineers are skilled. Asking how long the actual assigned team members have been with the company, and meeting them before signing, helps verify the stability behind a vendor's promises.

### Should code ownership be specifically addressed during technical due diligence?
Yes. Your contract should explicitly state that you own all code, documentation, and intellectual property produced under the engagement, reviewed by your own legal counsel, with no ambiguity about reusable components or frameworks the vendor might claim partial ownership over. This is one of the most common gaps founders discover only when trying to switch vendors later.
