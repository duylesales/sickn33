"Would you recommend them?" is the question almost every reference call opens with, and it is also the question least likely to produce useful information. Most references say yes to that question regardless of how the engagement actually went, either out of professional courtesy, because the specific problems they experienced feel awkward to volunteer unprompted, or because "would you recommend them" allows for a technically true but practically unhelpful qualified yes that never gets probed further. In an internal review of vendor due diligence calls at mid-market IT departments, generic satisfaction questions produced a "yes" response in the overwhelming majority of calls regardless of documented delivery issues that surfaced later during the actual engagement — meaning the standard reference call is, for most IT Managers, closer to a formality than a genuine diligence step.

A reference call that actually reveals vendor quality requires specific, behavioral questions that make it hard for a reference to give a pleasant but empty answer. This article provides exactly those questions, organized by what each one is designed to surface, along with guidance on reading between the lines of the answers you get back.

## Start With Questions That Require a Specific Number, Not an Impression

Open with questions that force a concrete answer rather than a general impression, because specific numbers are harder to soften diplomatically than vague praise. "What percentage of your sprints or milestones were delivered on the originally committed date?" is a very different question from "were they generally on time?" — the first requires the reference to recall and report an actual figure, and references who have to stop and think about the number are giving you more honest signal than one who answers instantly with reassuring generality.

Follow with: "How many times did you need to escalate an issue above your day-to-day contact, and how was that handled?" Any vendor relationship of meaningful length has had at least one real problem — a reference who claims zero escalations ever occurred across a multi-month engagement is either being diplomatically selective or the engagement was too short or too simple to be a representative reference in the first place. What you are actually listening for is not whether a problem occurred, but how it was handled once it did: quickly and transparently, or slowly and defensively.

## Ask About the Team That Actually Showed Up, Not the Team That Was Pitched

A gap between the team profiled during sales and the team that actually delivered the work is one of the most common and most consequential vendor issues, and it rarely surfaces unless asked about directly. Ask: "Was the team that delivered the work the same team you met during the sales process, and if not, how was that transition communicated?" A vendor who staffs consistently, or who communicates team changes proactively with a proper handoff, is behaving very differently from one who bait-and-switches senior talent for junior substitutes once the contract is signed and switching costs have shifted in their favor.

Also ask specifically: "How much turnover did you experience on the assigned team during the engagement, and how was continuity managed when it happened?" Some turnover is normal in any team of meaningful size over a multi-month engagement — the differentiator is not whether it happened but whether the vendor had a documented, low-friction handoff process or whether each departure meant weeks of lost context and slowed delivery while a replacement got up to speed.

## Probe Communication Quality With a Story, Not a Rating

Rather than asking a reference to rate communication on a 1-to-10 scale — an easy, low-effort answer that rarely surfaces anything specific — ask them to describe one specific instance where something went wrong and walk you through exactly how the vendor communicated about it. "Tell me about a time a deadline slipped or a bug made it to production. How did they tell you, how much notice did you get, and what did the resolution process look like?"

This question is difficult to answer well with an empty, reassuring response, because it requires a specific narrative rather than a rating. A strong vendor reference will typically describe proactive notification before the reference even noticed the problem themselves, a clear explanation of root cause, and a documented remediation plan. A weaker reference answer — reluctant specifics, a story where the reference discovered the problem before the vendor disclosed it, or a vague "they handled it fine" without detail — is a meaningfully different signal worth taking seriously even if the overall tone of the call remains polite.

## Ask What They Would Do Differently — Not Whether They're Happy

"If you were starting this engagement again today, what would you structure differently in the contract or the working relationship?" is one of the highest-yield questions available in a reference call, because it reframes the question away from a binary satisfaction judgment and toward specific, actionable detail the reference has genuinely thought about. Almost every reference has an answer to this question, even ones who are overall very satisfied, because almost every real engagement surfaces something the client would tighten up with hindsight.

Listen specifically for answers involving reporting cadence, escalation paths, or contract flexibility — these are structural, fixable issues distinct from output quality, and they tell you what to negotiate proactively into your own contract rather than discovering the same gap the hard way. A reference who cannot answer this question at all, insisting everything was perfect with no exceptions, is either an unusually smooth engagement or, more often, a reference who has not been asked to think critically about the relationship before this call.

## Ask About the Engagement's Ending, Not Just Its Middle

How a vendor relationship ends — whether it ended at all, and if so, how smoothly — is a different signal than how it ran day to day, and IT Managers frequently skip this question entirely. Ask: "Is the engagement still active, and if not, why did it end and how was the transition handled?" A vendor relationship that ended due to a client's internal reorganization or a project's natural completion is a different, more neutral signal than one that ended due to a quality or trust breakdown, and the reference's willingness to be candid about which one applies tells you something about how forthcoming they will be for the rest of the call too.

If the reference relationship is ongoing, ask directly: "Would you expand this vendor's scope on new work if the opportunity arose?" This is a more revealing question than a satisfaction rating, because it asks the reference to commit real future budget and risk to the vendor rather than offer a costless compliment about the past.

## Verify the Reference Is Representative, Not Cherry-Picked

Every vendor selects which references you get to call, which means the reference pool itself is inherently biased toward their strongest relationships. Counteract this by asking the vendor directly for a reference at least eighteen to twenty-four months into an active or completed relationship, specifically because longer-tenured relationships are harder to fake and more revealing than a recent client still in the honeymoon period of a new engagement. A vendor's willingness or reluctance to produce a long-tenured reference is itself a data point worth weighing alongside whatever the reference actually says.

Where possible, also ask the reference directly during the call: "Is there anyone else at your company who worked more closely with the day-to-day delivery than you did, who I could also speak with?" Executive sponsors who approved a vendor relationship often have a rosier, higher-altitude view than the technical or project management staff who worked with the vendor daily — and a vendor's comfort connecting you to that second, more operationally embedded contact is itself informative.

## Turning Reference Answers Into Your Own Contract Terms

The real payoff of a well-run reference call is not just a go or no-go signal on this specific vendor — it is a list of specific, contractual terms to negotiate proactively into your own agreement, drawn directly from what previous clients wish they had asked for. If a reference mentions they wish escalation paths had been clearer, write a defined escalation SLA into your own contract before signature. If a reference describes a rough team transition, ask for a documented handoff protocol in writing upfront. Reference calls done this way stop being a formality performed after the real decision is already made emotionally, and become one of the most concretely useful steps in the entire vendor evaluation process.

Request references from clients at multiple engagement lengths, including relationships active for two years or more, and structure your own call using the questions above before you finalize your vendor decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the single most revealing reference call question?
"What would you structure differently in the contract or working relationship if starting again today?" is among the highest-yield questions, because it moves past a binary satisfaction judgment and surfaces specific, actionable detail the reference has genuinely considered, even for engagements they are overall satisfied with.

### Why shouldn't I just ask "would you recommend them"?
Because most references answer yes to that question regardless of how the engagement actually went, out of professional courtesy or because a qualified yes rarely gets probed further. Specific, behavioral questions that require a concrete answer produce far more useful signal.

### How do I know if a vendor is giving me cherry-picked references?
Ask the vendor directly for a reference at least eighteen to twenty-four months into an active or completed relationship — longer-tenured relationships are harder to fake and more revealing than a recent client still in an engagement's early honeymoon period. Reluctance to produce one is itself informative.

### Should I ask about team turnover during a reference call?
Yes. Some turnover is normal over a multi-month engagement; the differentiator is whether the vendor had a documented, low-friction handoff process or whether each departure caused weeks of lost context and slowed delivery.

### What should I do with the answers I get from a reference call?
Turn specific concerns or wishes the reference mentions into contractual terms you negotiate proactively into your own agreement — for example, a defined escalation SLA or a documented team-transition protocol — rather than treating the call as only a go or no-go signal.
