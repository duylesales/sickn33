Every vendor portfolio page looks impressive by design — that is, after all, what a portfolio page is for. Polished screenshots, confident outcome statements, logos from recognizable brands. As a Head of Product evaluating a development partner, the skill you need is not admiring the polish; it is reading past it to figure out how much of a given case study reflects genuine delivery ownership versus a favorable retelling of work the vendor played a smaller role in than the page implies. This distinction matters more for a product leader than almost any other stakeholder in a vendor evaluation, because you are the one who will actually work alongside this team on backlog decisions, trade-off conversations, and iteration cycles once the contract is signed — not just receive a finished deliverable at arm's length.

A portfolio review checklist gives you a repeatable way to separate real delivery evidence from marketing narrative, applied consistently across every finalist so the comparison is fair. This article walks through exactly what to check, in what order, and what specific gaps in a portfolio page should prompt a direct follow-up question during your next vendor call.

## Check Whether the Case Study Names an Actual Role, Not Just an Outcome

The first and most revealing filter is specificity about the vendor's actual role in the delivered outcome. A case study that says "helped grow user engagement by 40%" without specifying what the vendor actually built, over what timeframe, or how much of the surrounding product and marketing effort belonged to the client's own internal team is telling you almost nothing verifiable. A case study that says "designed and built the onboarding flow redesign, reducing signup drop-off from 34% to 19% over a six-week sprint cycle, working alongside the client's internal product team" is giving you something you can actually probe further and, ideally, verify with the client directly.

During your next vendor call, pick the two or three most impressive-sounding case studies and ask directly: "Walk me through exactly what your team owned versus what the client's internal team owned on this project." A vendor confident in their actual contribution will answer this specifically and without hesitation, often adding detail beyond what the case study page shows. A vendor who gets vaguer rather than more specific when pressed on ownership is very likely retelling a project where their actual contribution was narrower than the marketing framing suggests.

## Look for Evidence of the Messy Middle, Not Just the Polished Result

Real product work involves false starts, scope revisions, and iteration based on user feedback that didn't confirm the original hypothesis — and a portfolio page that shows none of this, presenting every project as a clean, linear success from kickoff to triumphant launch, should raise your skepticism rather than your confidence. Ask a vendor directly, for any case study that interests you, "what didn't work in this project on the first attempt, and how did you adjust?" A vendor who cannot answer this question for a real project either was not meaningfully involved in the actual product decisions, or is presenting a sanitized narrative that omits exactly the kind of iterative thinking you will depend on them for once your own project inevitably hits its own unexpected complications.

This question is particularly revealing for a Head of Product specifically, because your working relationship with this vendor will be defined far more by how they handle the messy middle of a real product cycle than by how cleanly they present a finished case study after the fact. A team that can speak fluently about a pivot, a scrapped initial approach, or a user-testing result that contradicted an assumption is demonstrating exactly the collaborative, iterative capability a case study page cannot show on its own.

## Verify Technical Depth Signals, Not Just Visual Polish

A beautifully designed portfolio page tells you the vendor either has a strong in-house design or marketing function, or hired an agency to build their own portfolio — neither of which reliably predicts engineering quality on your project. Look instead for signals of genuine technical depth: mentions of specific architecture decisions and why they were made, testing and QA approach described concretely rather than as a bullet-point buzzword, and post-launch metrics tracked over a meaningful period rather than a single celebratory number captured at peak performance shortly after launch.

Ask to see, if the case study client's confidentiality allows it, an actual artifact from the project beyond the marketing summary — a sprint report format, a technical architecture diagram, or a snippet of documentation. Vendors who readily produce this kind of supporting evidence are demonstrating the same transparency you will need throughout your own engagement; vendors who can only offer the polished case study page itself, with nothing underneath it, are giving you a preview of how much visibility you can expect once you are a client rather than a prospect.

## Cross-Reference the Timeline Against What's Plausible

A surprising number of inflated case studies reveal themselves through simple timeline arithmetic. If a case study claims a complex, multi-integration platform was delivered in four weeks by a small team, that claim is either describing a much narrower scope than the description implies, describing a much larger team than typically assigned, or simply not accurate. Cross-reference the stated timeline, team size, and scope against your own realistic sense of what similar work requires — as a Head of Product, you likely already have enough internal experience with your own delivery timelines to sanity-check whether a vendor's claimed pace is plausible or marketing-inflated.

When a timeline claim seems implausibly fast, ask directly: "how many engineers were assigned to this project, and what percentage of their time was dedicated versus split across other client work?" This single question frequently resolves an implausible-sounding timeline into a perfectly reasonable one — a larger dedicated team than the case study implied — or reveals that the stated timeline compressed a much longer, less impressive actual delivery window into a more marketable headline figure.

## Ask for the Portfolio Piece That Isn't on the Website

Every vendor curates their public portfolio toward their strongest, most flattering examples — that is a reasonable and expected marketing decision, not itself a red flag. What is more revealing is asking directly for a project that did not go smoothly, or one that is not featured publicly for a specific reason: "tell me about a project from the last two years that you would not put on your portfolio page, and why." A vendor willing to discuss a difficult project honestly, including what went wrong and what they changed as a result, is demonstrating a level of transparency that predicts well for how they will handle your own project's inevitable rough patches. A vendor who insists every project has gone smoothly is either being selectively honest or has not been in business long enough to have hit real complexity yet.

## Building Your Own Portfolio Review Scorecard

Turn the checks above into a simple scorecard applied consistently across every finalist: role specificity (does the case study name what the vendor actually owned), evidence of iteration (can they discuss what didn't work initially), technical depth signals (architecture and QA detail beyond visual polish), timeline plausibility (does the claimed pace match realistic delivery expectations for the described scope), and candor under a direct, uncomfortable question (will they discuss a project that didn't go smoothly). Score each finalist consistently across two or three of their most prominently featured case studies, not just one, since a single strong example can exist even within an otherwise thin portfolio.

A portfolio review done this way stops being a passive scroll through a marketing page and becomes an active diligence exercise that predicts your actual working relationship with this vendor far better than the polish of their website ever could. As a Head of Product, that working relationship — how they handle ambiguity, iteration, and honest trade-off conversations — is what you are really evaluating, and a case study page is only useful to the extent it gives you specific, verifiable claims to probe further.

Request detailed project breakdowns, including team composition and timeline specifics, for the case studies most relevant to your product, and ask us directly about a project that didn't go exactly as planned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell if a vendor's case study reflects real ownership?
Ask the vendor directly what their team owned versus what the client's internal team contributed. A vendor confident in their actual role answers specifically and often adds detail beyond the case study page, while a vendor retelling a project with a narrower real contribution tends to get vaguer under direct questioning.

### Why should I ask about projects that didn't go well?
Because a vendor's ability to discuss what didn't work initially and how they adjusted predicts how they will handle your own project's inevitable complications far better than a curated highlight reel of clean, successful launches ever could.

### What if a case study's timeline seems too fast for the described scope?
Ask how many engineers were assigned and what percentage of their time was dedicated to that project. This often resolves an implausible timeline into a reasonable one by revealing a larger dedicated team, or reveals that the headline figure compressed a longer, less impressive actual delivery window.

### How many case studies should I review per vendor?
Review at least two or three of a vendor's most prominently featured case studies rather than just one, since a single strong example can exist within an otherwise thin portfolio and doesn't reliably represent the vendor's typical delivery quality.

### What technical details should a strong case study include beyond outcomes?
Look for specific architecture decisions and the reasoning behind them, a concrete description of the testing and QA approach, and post-launch metrics tracked over a meaningful period rather than a single number captured at peak performance shortly after launch.
