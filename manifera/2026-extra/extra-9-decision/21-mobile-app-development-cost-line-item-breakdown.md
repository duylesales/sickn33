Sixty-one percent of mobile app budgets run over their original estimate by more than 20%, according to industry benchmarking studies on custom software delivery — and the single biggest predictor of a blown budget isn't scope creep. It's a quote that never itemized what you were actually paying for in the first place. If you're staring at three vendor proposals right now, each with a single lump-sum number and little else, you're not comparing prices. You're comparing guesses.

This matters most at the exact moment you're in: the final decision. You've already screened out the agencies that couldn't answer basic technical questions. You've shortlisted two or three vendors. Now the only thing standing between you and a signature is understanding what's actually inside that mobile app development cost figure — because a €45,000 quote and a €75,000 quote for "the same app" usually aren't pricing the same deliverable at all. One of them is going to generate a change order within six weeks of kickoff. Your job right now is figuring out which one before you sign, not after.

Most executives at this stage have already read the generic "how much does an app cost" explainers. What nobody hands you is the internal template a vendor's own project manager uses to build the number — the one that shows exactly where your money goes, which line items are padded, and which are dangerously thin. That's what this breakdown gives you.

## Why Mobile App Development Cost Estimates Vary So Wildly

Ask five vendors to quote the same app brief and you'll get five different numbers, often with a 2-3x spread between the lowest and highest. That spread isn't primarily about hourly rates — it's about what's included. A lean quote might exclude backend infrastructure, push notification setup, App Store and Google Play submission handling, or post-launch bug-fix windows. A padded quote might include buffer time nobody asked for, or design exploration rounds priced as if you hadn't already approved wireframes.

The fix is forcing every vendor onto the same line-item template before you compare a single number. Ask each finalist to break their quote into: discovery and requirements, UX/UI design, native or cross-platform development, backend/API work, third-party integrations, QA and device testing, deployment to app stores, and a defined post-launch support window. Vendors who resist this — who insist the number "just is what it is" — are telling you something important about how disputes will get resolved later, mid-contract, when you have the least leverage.

There's also a geographic dimension to the spread that rarely gets discussed openly. A vendor quoting from a Western European agency's blended hourly rate of €90-140 and a vendor delivering through a Southeast Asian engineering hub at €35-55 per hour aren't just priced differently — they may be scoping the exact same feature list with entirely different assumptions about how many senior engineering hours it takes to build it correctly the first time. The number on the page only becomes comparable once you know what seniority mix, what QA depth, and what support window sit behind it.

## The Real Cost Drivers Behind Every Line Item

Once you have comparable line items, the next question is which ones are actually negotiable and which reflect real, unavoidable engineering effort.

**Platform choice** moves the number more than almost anything else. A single native iOS or Android app is the cheapest to build but the most expensive to maintain long-term if you eventually need both platforms. Cross-platform frameworks like React Native or Flutter typically cut combined iOS/Android development cost by 30-40% by sharing a majority of the codebase, which is why so many vendor quotes for startups and mid-market companies default to it.

**Backend complexity** is the line item most often underpriced by inexperienced vendors and most often the source of change orders later. A simple content app with no real-time features needs far less backend investment than a marketplace app handling payments, live inventory, or multi-tenant user roles. If a quote's backend line looks suspiciously small relative to your feature list, that's not a discount — it's a future invoice.

**Integrations** — payment gateways, CRM sync, mapping APIs, SSO — are billed per-integration by serious vendors and bundled vaguely by less rigorous ones. Get each integration itemized. "Third-party integrations: included" is not a line item; it's a placeholder for a future dispute.

**QA and device coverage** determines how many support tickets you'll field in month one. A quote that allocates 5% of the budget to testing across a handful of devices is optimizing for a fast handoff to your team, not for a stable launch.

**Team seniority mix** is the least visible driver and the one that most affects long-term quality. A quote staffed mostly by junior developers with a single senior reviewer will look cheaper per hour, but it typically produces more rework, meaning the effective cost per shipped feature can end up higher than a team with a richer mix of senior engineers from day one. Ask each vendor for the actual seniority breakdown of the team assigned to your project, by name if possible, not just a job-title list from their marketing page.

## Total Cost of Ownership, Not Just Build Price

The build quote is only the first of at least three payments you'll make for this app. A realistic total cost of ownership model over 18 months looks like: 55-65% initial build, 15-20% post-launch stabilization and iteration in the first two quarters, and 20-25% ongoing maintenance, OS updates, and feature additions in year two. Vendors who quote only the first number and stay silent on the other two are handing you an incomplete picture by design — either because they haven't thought past the handoff, or because they know a fuller number would make their bid less competitive on paper.

Run a simple sanity check: ask each finalist what percentage of their existing client base has been with them for more than 12 months. A vendor with strong retention has, by definition, priced their build quotes in a way that supports a sustainable ongoing relationship rather than a one-time transaction. That's a better proxy for real total cost than any single number on a proposal.

## What a Properly Structured Quote Looks Like

A defensible quote for a mid-complexity app — think a two-sided marketplace or a subscription content app with user accounts, push notifications, and one payment integration — typically breaks down something like this: 8-10% discovery and technical scoping, 15-18% UX/UI design, 40-45% development (split roughly evenly between frontend and backend/API work), 10% third-party integrations, 12-15% QA across your target device matrix, and 5-8% deployment plus a defined post-launch support window (typically 30-90 days).

If a vendor's proposal collapses several of these into one bucket, ask them to unbundle it. A vendor who has actually built the app they're quoting can produce this breakdown in an afternoon. One who's guessing will need days, or will push back on the request entirely — which is itself useful signal at the decision stage.

It also helps to weight the percentages against your specific app category. A content or utility app with minimal backend needs can shift more of the budget toward design and less toward development, while a fintech or logistics app with heavy compliance and integration requirements should show a noticeably larger backend and QA allocation, sometimes 20-25% for QA alone given the cost of a production bug in a payments flow. If a vendor hands you a generic percentage split that doesn't flex based on your actual feature list, that's a sign the quote was built from a template rather than from your brief.

## The Contract Clauses That Change the Real Number

Two contract terms move your effective cost more than any single line item, and both are easy to miss when you're focused on the headline number.

**Change order pricing.** Every real project changes scope somewhat. What matters is whether your contract defines change orders as a rate you agreed to upfront, or as "to be discussed" — the latter is where fixed-price projects quietly become open-ended ones.

**Post-launch support terms.** A quote that looks 15% cheaper but includes zero post-launch bug-fix window will cost more within the first 90 days after launch than the "expensive" quote that bundles 60 days of fixes. Compare total cost of ownership through your first two release cycles, not just the build price.

A third clause worth scrutinizing is **IP and source code ownership**. Some lower-cost vendors retain rights to reusable components or frameworks they build for you, which sounds harmless until you try to switch maintenance providers a year later and discover you don't fully own the codebase you paid for. Get an explicit clause confirming full IP transfer on final payment, including any custom internal tooling built specifically for your project.

A fourth, subtler clause is **milestone payment structure**. Vendors who ask for 50% upfront and 50% on delivery have little financial incentive to flag problems early — they've already been paid for most of the work regardless of quality. A structure tied to 4-6 smaller milestones, each requiring your sign-off, keeps the vendor accountable throughout the build rather than only at the finish line, and it gives you a natural exit point if the relationship isn't working after the first milestone.

## A Practical Comparison Checklist Before You Sign

Before you approve any vendor, run their quote through this filter:

- Does the quote separate design, development, integrations, QA, and deployment into distinct line items?
- Is the platform decision (native vs. cross-platform) explicitly justified against your feature list, not just assumed?
- Are third-party integrations priced individually, with API costs (Stripe, Twilio, mapping providers) noted as pass-through rather than buried?
- Does the contract define a fixed hourly or daily rate for change orders?
- Is there a named post-launch support window with a specific end date, not an open-ended "we'll help if needed"?
- Can the vendor explain, in plain language, why their QA allocation covers the device matrix your actual users are on?

A vendor who answers all six confidently, with numbers rather than reassurance, is quoting from real delivery experience. One who answers with generalities is quoting from a spreadsheet template.

It's worth adding a seventh test that most executives skip: ask to see one anonymized past quote-versus-actual comparison. A vendor confident in their estimating process will happily show you how close a previous project's final invoice landed to its original quote. A vendor who dodges the request is telling you their track record on this exact question — the one you care about most — isn't one they want to discuss before you sign.

Finally, resist the temptation to make this decision over email. A 30-minute call where you walk through the line-item breakdown together, live, surfaces more inconsistencies than three rounds of written back-and-forth. Vendors who are confident in their numbers welcome that conversation; vendors who are hoping you won't ask too many questions tend to find reasons to keep it in writing.

The lowest number on the page is rarely the lowest total cost. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a realistic mobile app development cost for a mid-complexity app in 2026?

A mid-complexity app with user accounts, one payment integration, and push notifications typically ranges from €40,000 to €90,000 depending on platform choice and backend complexity. The wider that range gets in a single vendor's quote, the more you should push for a line-item breakdown before comparing it to other proposals.

### Why do two vendors quote such different mobile app development costs for the same brief?

Differences usually come from what's included, not from wildly different hourly rates. One vendor might exclude QA across your full device matrix, integrations, or post-launch support, while another bundles all of it into the headline number. Always request the same line-item template from every finalist.

### Is cross-platform development actually cheaper than native for both iOS and Android?

Yes, in most cases. Frameworks like React Native or Flutter typically reduce combined iOS/Android build cost by 30-40% through code sharing, though apps with heavy platform-specific features (advanced camera processing, complex animations) can narrow that gap.

### What's the biggest hidden cost founders miss when comparing final quotes?

Change order pricing and post-launch support terms. A quote that looks cheaper upfront but leaves change orders undefined or excludes a bug-fix window often costs more in total within the first 90 days after launch than a higher initial quote with clear terms.

### Should I choose the vendor with the lowest mobile app development cost?

Not automatically. Compare total cost of ownership through your first two release cycles, including support windows and change order rates, and weigh that against the vendor's ability to itemize their quote credibly — a vendor who can't explain their own number in detail is a bigger financial risk than a slightly higher price tag.
