Most vendor engagements lose their most critical month before the work has even properly begun — not because the vendor is incompetent, but because everyone treats the signed contract as the finish line instead of the starting gun. One European fintech CTO saw the difference with unusual precision after running two onboardings a year apart. The first vendor took the signed contract as the finish line — access credentials trickled in over two weeks, the kickoff call happened without a written architecture briefing, and the team's first meaningful commit landed five weeks in. The second vendor treated signature as the starting gun for a structured 30-day plan, with credentials provisioned before the kickoff call and a working, reviewed pull request from the new team by day nine. Both vendors were technically competent. Only one of them understood that contract signature is not the end of the decision process — it is the beginning of the highest-risk period in the entire engagement.

The weeks immediately following a vendor decision are where a well-chosen vendor either confirms the choice was right or quietly starts eroding the confidence that got the deal signed. Momentum lost in the first month is difficult to recover, both practically — a slow ramp delays the roadmap — and politically, since stakeholders who were skeptical of the decision watch these early weeks closely for evidence they were right to doubt it. A structured 30-day onboarding plan is what protects a good vendor decision from an avoidably slow start.

This article lays out that plan week by week, covering what should happen before day one, what the first two weeks should accomplish technically, and how to know by day thirty whether the engagement is actually on track.

## Before Day One: What Should Already Be Done at Signature

The most common onboarding mistake is treating contract signature as the moment access provisioning begins rather than the moment it should already be underway. In the days between final contract sign-off and the official kickoff date, a CTO's internal team should have prepared credentialed access to the relevant repositories, project management tooling, and communication channels, scoped appropriately to the vendor's role rather than granted broadly by default. Architecture documentation, coding standards, and any existing technical debt inventory should be compiled and shared ahead of kickoff, not handed over verbally during the first call. A vendor arriving to a kickoff call with access already provisioned and documentation already in hand can start productive work within days rather than losing the first week to administrative setup.

## Week One: Access, Architecture Briefing, and Working Agreements

The first week should accomplish three things concretely: full access provisioning confirmed and tested, not just granted; a structured architecture briefing where the vendor's technical lead walks through the existing system with your internal engineers, asking questions in real time rather than passively receiving a document dump; and a written working agreement covering sprint cadence, code review expectations, communication channels, escalation paths, and reporting format. This working agreement should be specific enough to reference later if expectations drift — naming exact turnaround times for code review, defined standup times accounting for time zone overlap, and a named point of contact on each side for escalations.

By the end of week one, the new team should have a small, low-risk task assigned — not yet a critical feature, but something real enough to exercise the full development-to-deployment pipeline end to end, surfacing any environment or process friction while the stakes are still low.

## Week Two: The First Real Commits and Process Calibration

Week two is where the engagement should produce its first genuinely meaningful contribution — a completed ticket that has gone through the full code review and deployment process your team actually uses, not a sandboxed exercise. This is also the point to calibrate, not just execute, the working agreement established in week one: does the promised code review turnaround actually hold in practice, is the standup overlap window working for both sides, are blockers being raised and resolved within the timeframe agreed. Minor friction at this stage is normal and expected; the goal of surfacing it early is fixing it while it is still cheap to adjust, rather than letting a workable-on-paper agreement quietly fail in practice for months.

For a well-structured [offshore software development](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) engagement specifically, week two is also when CET working-hours overlap and communication fluency should be stress-tested directly — not assumed from the sales process — through real standups, real blocked-ticket escalations, and a genuine demo of completed work to internal stakeholders.

## Weeks Three and Four: The First Real Sprint and Formal Check-In

By week three, the new team should be operating inside your actual sprint cadence, taking on a normal share of the backlog rather than isolated onboarding tasks. This is the point where velocity starts becoming a meaningful, trackable signal rather than noise from the ramp-up period. Week four should close with a formal 30-day check-in involving the CTO or technical sponsor directly, not just the day-to-day project manager: reviewing velocity against initial expectations, revisiting the working agreement against how the first month actually went, and explicitly naming anything that needs to change before the engagement scales further or takes on higher-stakes work.

This 30-day check-in is also the natural point to confirm whether stakeholder concerns raised during selection — a security question, a specific technical capability the vendor needed to demonstrate — have been genuinely addressed in practice rather than only on paper. ## Signals That Onboarding Is Off Track by Day Thirty

A handful of concrete signals by day thirty should prompt a direct conversation rather than a wait-and-see approach: repeated code review turnaround delays beyond what was agreed, standup attendance or communication quality that has not matched the working agreement, velocity meaningfully below what was reasonably projected for a ramp-up period, or a technical lead on the vendor side who has already changed once without clear explanation. None of these individually is necessarily fatal to the engagement, but a CTO who waits until month three to address a pattern visible by day thirty loses the leverage that comes from raising it while the relationship is still new and both sides are motivated to demonstrate good faith.

## Making the Final Call

The gap between the two fintech onboardings — five weeks to a first commit versus nine days — was not a difference in engineering talent. It was a difference in whether signature was treated as the finish line or the starting gun. A structured 30-day plan, with access and documentation ready before kickoff, a real working agreement established in week one, and a formal check-in at day thirty, converts a good vendor decision into a fast, confidence-building start rather than leaving that outcome to chance.

If your last vendor onboarding felt slower and messier than it should have, the fix is rarely a better vendor — it is usually a more deliberate first thirty days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should be ready before a new vendor's official kickoff call?
Credentialed access to relevant repositories and tooling, scoped appropriately, along with architecture documentation, coding standards, and any technical debt inventory should be prepared and shared before kickoff, so the vendor can begin productive work within days rather than losing the first week to administrative setup.

### What should a vendor working agreement specifically include?
It should name exact code review turnaround times, defined standup times accounting for time zone overlap, communication channels, an escalation path with named points of contact on each side, and reporting format, specific enough to reference later if expectations drift.

### When should a new vendor produce its first meaningful contribution?
By the end of week two, the team should complete a ticket that goes through the full code review and deployment process actually used in production, not a sandboxed exercise, surfacing any process friction while it is still cheap to fix.

### What should happen at the 30-day mark of a new vendor engagement?
A formal check-in involving the CTO or technical sponsor directly, reviewing velocity against initial expectations, revisiting the working agreement against how the first month actually went, and confirming any concerns raised during vendor selection have been addressed in practice.

### What are warning signs that vendor onboarding is off track by day thirty?
Repeated code review delays beyond what was agreed, communication quality not matching the working agreement, velocity meaningfully below reasonable ramp-up expectations, or an unexplained change in the vendor's assigned technical lead all warrant a direct conversation rather than a wait-and-see approach.
