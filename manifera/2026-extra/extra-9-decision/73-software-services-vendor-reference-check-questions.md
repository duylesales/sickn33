What if the glowing reference call your shortlisted vendor arranged for you was handpicked for exactly one reason: that client would never mention the missed deadline, the mid-project scope fight, or the three-week silence after the invoice was paid? Every vendor selling software services hands you their best-case reference. That's not dishonest — it's just sales. The mistake is treating that curated call as due diligence instead of what it actually is: a testimonial with extra steps.

If you're a non-technical founder in the final stretch of comparing software services vendors, the reference check is very likely the single highest-leverage hour you'll spend before signing a contract. Yet most founders either skip it entirely, assuming a strong portfolio speaks for itself, or run through it as a formality — three polite questions, a positive answer to each, and a rubber-stamp decision made two calls earlier anyway. This guide walks through how to run a reference check that actually surfaces the information a proposal document is designed to hide.

The reason this matters more at the decision stage than earlier in your search is simple: by the time you're comparing two or three finalists, every remaining vendor looks credible on paper. Portfolios are polished, case studies are compelling, and every sales conversation has been rehearsed to answer your obvious objections. The reference check is one of the few remaining tools that lets you see past the pitch and into how a vendor actually behaves once your money is on the table and the relationship gets less glamorous than the proposal made it sound.

## Step 1: Ask for a Reference the Vendor Didn't Choose

Start by requesting the reference the vendor volunteers, but don't stop there. Ask specifically for one additional contact: a client from a project that was paused, ended early, or didn't renew — every vendor with more than a handful of engagements has at least one. Frame it plainly: "I'd like to speak with someone whose experience wasn't perfect, so I understand how you handle a project that goes sideways." A vendor who can produce this contact, even reluctantly, is demonstrating something valuable — that they're not afraid of an honest conversation about how they handle friction. A vendor who insists every single client relationship has been flawless is either newer than they're presenting themselves to be, or filtering harder than you'd like.

If they genuinely cannot produce a second reference beyond their curated one, treat that as a data point rather than disqualifying evidence on its own — smaller or newer software services providers may have a thinner client base. But weigh it against everything else in this checklist, because it removes one of your best tools for catching problems before they become yours.

It also helps to be explicit about timing when you make this request. Ask early in your final-round conversations, not the day before you're expected to sign, so the vendor has no plausible excuse about scheduling conflicts limiting who they can connect you with. A vendor who stalls on scheduling a second reference call for more than a few business days, without a clear explanation, is often quietly hoping the deadline pressure will make you skip the step altogether.

## Step 2: Ask About the Worst Week of the Project, Not the Best

When you get a reference on the phone, resist the instinct to ask "how was your experience?" — it's a question built for a positive, generic answer. Instead ask: "Tell me about the hardest moment in this project. What went wrong, and how did the vendor handle it?" Every real project, regardless of how skilled the vendor, has a rough patch — a missed estimate, a miscommunication about scope, a technical decision that had to be reversed. What you're listening for isn't whether something went wrong; it's how it was handled. Did the vendor communicate proactively, or did the client have to chase them down? Was the fix transparent, or did the vendor quietly absorb the cost and never explain what happened?

The specificity of the answer matters as much as its content. A reference who says "there was a tense moment around month four when we needed to change the payment integration, and their lead called us the same day to walk through options" is telling you something concrete and credible. A reference who says "honestly, everything was smooth" for an engagement lasting more than a few months is either being diplomatic or the relationship was managed in a way that shielded them from visibility into normal project friction — neither is the confidence-inspiring signal it sounds like on the surface.

## Step 3: Verify How Long the Relationship Actually Lasted — and Whether It's Still Active

A one-off project completed two years ago tells you less than an ongoing relationship. Ask the reference directly: "Are you still working with this vendor? If not, why did the relationship end?" Renewal and repeat engagement is one of the more honest signals of software services quality available to you, because it reflects a client voting with their budget after having already experienced the vendor's real day-to-day work — not their sales pitch. Manifera's own track record includes many clients who have returned for additional phases or new projects after their initial engagement, out of more than 160 delivered projects and 120-plus clients over a decade of operation, and that pattern of renewal is something you should expect any serious vendor to be able to demonstrate with real numbers, not just an adjective.

If the relationship ended, ask plainly whether it ended because the project was simply complete and scoped as a fixed engagement, or because of dissatisfaction. Both are legitimate answers — fixed-scope projects end by design — but you want to hear the distinction clearly rather than infer it.

## Step 4: Ask How the Vendor Handled Scope Change and Unexpected Cost

Nearly every software project changes scope at least once — new requirements emerge, a business assumption shifts, a technical constraint forces a redesign. What separates a trustworthy services vendor isn't the absence of scope change; it's the transparency of how it's priced and communicated. Ask the reference: "When the scope changed, how did the vendor communicate cost or timeline impact, and did anything feel like it was sprung on you after the fact?" McKinsey's research on technology delivery has long identified unclear scope-change handling as one of the more common sources of client-vendor conflict in outsourced engagements — not because change itself is unusual, but because ambiguity about who absorbs the cost of change erodes trust quickly once it happens.

Listen for whether the reference describes a documented change-request process — a written estimate for the new scope, an agreed decision point before work started — versus a vague memory of "things just kind of got more expensive." The former reflects a mature services provider; the latter is a preview of what you should expect if your own project's scope shifts, which for most founders building a first product, it eventually will.

It's also worth asking whether the vendor ever pushed back on a requirement the client wanted, rather than simply agreeing to everything and billing for the extra hours afterward. A services provider willing to say "that approach will cost you three extra sprints and here's a leaner alternative" before work starts is protecting your budget in a way that pure order-taking never will. This kind of proactive pushback is one of the clearer signs that a vendor is functioning as a genuine development partner rather than a pass-through contractor executing whatever is asked without question.

## Step 5: Confirm the Communication Story Matches What You Were Told in Sales

By this stage in your vendor comparison, you've likely heard promises about communication: dedicated project managers, regular updates, fast response times. Use the reference call to verify these specifically. Ask: "How often did you actually hear from the team, and who did you contact when something urgent came up?" Compare the answer against what the sales team told you during your own conversations. A gap between the sales pitch and the reference's lived experience — even a small one — is worth probing further, because it tells you whether the account team you're speaking with today accurately represents how delivery teams actually operate once the contract is signed. This is also where you can ask about internal links worth following up on directly with the vendor, such as reviewing their [custom software development](https://www.manifera.com/services/custom-software-development/) process page or requesting to see examples from their [portfolio](https://www.manifera.com/portfolio/) that resemble your own project in scope or industry.

## Step 6: Cross-Check the Verbal Story Against Public Information

Finally, before you finalize your decision, spend twenty minutes cross-referencing what you heard against what's publicly available: the vendor's LinkedIn presence for the specific team members mentioned, any public case studies matching what the reference described, and review platforms if the vendor has profiles there. You're not looking for perfection — you're looking for consistency. If a reference mentioned a specific project type or team size that doesn't align at all with what's publicly documented, ask a direct follow-up question rather than assuming it's a simple discrepancy.

This step also protects you against a subtler risk: a reference who was genuinely satisfied but whose project bears little resemblance to yours in complexity, industry, or scale. A glowing reference from a simple marketing website project tells you very little about how the same vendor will perform building a multi-sided marketplace with payment processing and regulatory requirements. Weight each reference by how closely its scope matches your own, and if none of the references offered resemble your project closely enough, ask the vendor directly for one that does before you proceed further.

## Why This Step Matters Even More for Non-Technical Founders

If you don't have an in-house CTO to evaluate a vendor's technical claims, the reference check becomes one of your most reliable non-technical proxies for quality. You may not be able to personally assess a vendor's code architecture, but you can absolutely assess whether their past clients describe a transparent, accountable, well-communicated partnership — and that assessment translates directly into whether you'll get a trustworthy partner rather than a vendor who disappears once the deposit clears. This is precisely the kind of scrutiny Manifera welcomes from prospective clients specifically because 160-plus completed engagements give founders a large and honest pool to draw references from, rather than a handful of curated success stories.

It also helps to remember that a reference check isn't just about avoiding a bad outcome — it's about calibrating your expectations for the relationship you're about to enter. A founder who hears, in a reference's own words, exactly how communication worked, how disagreements were resolved, and how invoices were handled walks into their own engagement with realistic expectations rather than the idealized version painted during the sales process. That single difference — realistic expectations versus idealized ones — is often what separates founders who feel like a vendor delivered on their promise from founders who feel blindsided by normal friction they were never warned to expect.

## Making Your Final Decision

Run this checklist against every vendor still on your shortlist, not just the frontrunner — a comparative reference check across two or three finalists tells you far more than a single deep check on one. Pay closest attention to how each vendor responds to the request itself, because willingness to be checked thoroughly is often as revealing as anything a reference actually says.

Keep a simple written scorecard as you go: for each vendor, note how quickly they arranged references, how specific and consistent the answers were, and whether public information matched what you heard on the calls. When two finalists otherwise look comparable on price and portfolio, this scorecard is frequently the tiebreaker — and it's a far sturdier basis for a six-figure decision than which sales team you personally liked best.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What questions should I ask a software services vendor's reference client?
Ask about the hardest moment in the project and how it was handled, whether the relationship is still active or why it ended, how scope changes were priced and communicated, and whether actual communication matched what the sales team promised. Avoid generic questions like "how was your experience," which tend to produce generic, unhelpful answers.

### How do I get an honest reference instead of a curated success story?
Request one reference the vendor chooses and one additional reference from a project that was paused, ended early, or didn't renew. A vendor willing to share a less-than-perfect reference is demonstrating confidence in how they handle friction, which is more informative than a uniformly positive testimonial.

### Why does reference checking matter more for non-technical founders?
Without in-house technical expertise to evaluate a vendor's code quality directly, references become one of the most reliable proxies available for assessing transparency, communication, and accountability — qualities that matter as much to project success as raw technical skill.

### How many vendor references should I check before making a decision?
Speak with at least two references per shortlisted vendor, and run the same reference-check questions across every finalist rather than just your frontrunner. Comparing answers across vendors reveals patterns that a single deep check on one vendor cannot.

### What is a red flag during a software services vendor reference call?
Vague, uniformly positive answers with no specific examples, an inability or refusal to provide a second reference beyond the vendor's chosen one, and a noticeable gap between what the reference describes and what the sales team promised during your own conversations are all significant warning signs.
