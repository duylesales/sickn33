€280,000 to €412,000. A 47% overrun on a fixed-price software contract at a European manufacturing company, and it didn't come from a single dramatic failure — it came from 23 separate change requests over fourteen months, each individually reasonable-sounding, each priced at a rate roughly double the original contract's effective hourly rate, and each approved in isolation by a project manager who never once totaled the cumulative impact against the original budget until finance flagged it during a routine review. The vendor hadn't done anything contractually improper. The contract simply hadn't been built to prevent this outcome, and change requests are, by a wide margin, the single most common reason a fixed-price software contract fails to stay fixed.

This is not a rare or unusual story — it is close to the default outcome for fixed-price contracts that don't specifically govern change request pricing and approval from the outset, because scope changes are a near-certainty on any software project running longer than a few months, and the contract's silence on how those changes get priced and approved is exactly the vacuum vendors are structurally incentivized to fill favorably to themselves. This article covers where that vacuum forms, how vendors price into it, and the specific contract mechanics a CFO should require before signing.

## Why Change Requests Are Where Fixed-Price Contracts Quietly Become Time-and-Materials

The entire value proposition of a fixed-price contract, from a CFO's perspective, is budget certainty — a known number for a known scope. What frequently gets lost is that this certainty only holds for the scope as originally defined; the moment a change request is raised, that specific piece of work typically reverts to a pricing model closer to time-and-materials, priced by the vendor at whatever rate they choose to set for change work, since the original fixed-price agreement, by definition, no longer covers it. A contract that doesn't pre-negotiate the pricing mechanism for change requests is effectively a fixed-price agreement for the base scope and an open-ended time-and-materials agreement for everything else — which, on a project with meaningful scope evolution, can end up being the larger share of total spend by the time the project closes.

## The Vague Scope Document That Guarantees Change Requests

Change requests aren't primarily caused by clients changing their minds arbitrarily — they're overwhelmingly caused by scope documents that were ambiguous or incomplete at signing, discovered only once development is underway and a genuine gap or ambiguity surfaces. A scope document that describes features in business terms without technical specificity — "a reporting dashboard" rather than a defined list of specific reports, filters, export formats, and user permission levels — effectively guarantees a stream of change requests as each ambiguity gets resolved one ticket at a time during implementation, at change-request pricing rather than base-contract pricing. Before signing, a CFO should ask directly how detailed and technically specific the scope document is, and treat vendor pushback on adding specificity as a warning sign rather than an acceptable efficiency tradeoff — the vagueness that speeds up the sales process is frequently the same vagueness that inflates the final invoice.

## How Vendors Price Change Requests, and Where the Markup Hides

Change request pricing varies significantly across vendors, and the markup, when it exists, is rarely presented as a markup — it typically shows up as a higher effective hourly rate applied to change work than the blended rate embedded in the original fixed-price quote, justified as covering the overhead of re-planning, re-testing, and re-integrating unplanned work into an existing sprint structure. That justification has some legitimate basis, but the size of the markup varies enormously across vendors, and a CFO evaluating a proposal should ask for the specific change request rate in writing, compared explicitly against the effective rate in the base contract, before signing — not discover the differential for the first time on the first change order invoice.

## Change Request Governance: Approval Thresholds and Caps

The most effective structural defense against change request budget creep isn't refusing all scope changes, which is unrealistic on any real project, but building explicit governance into the contract before it's needed: a defined approval threshold above which a change request requires sign-off from a specific named stakeholder rather than a project manager alone, a running cumulative total of approved change request spend visible to finance in real time rather than reconciled only at project close, and, where feasible, a contractual cap on total change request spend as a percentage of the base contract value, beyond which a formal renegotiation is triggered rather than continued incremental approval. A cap set around 15% to 20% of base contract value is a reasonable starting point for most projects, calibrated up or down based on how mature and detailed the original scope document actually is.

## Building a Change Request Budget Line Into the Original Contract

Rather than treating change requests as an unplanned, purely reactive expense, the more disciplined approach is to budget for them explicitly from the outset, since some level of scope evolution is close to inevitable on any project of meaningful duration. Negotiating a pre-agreed change request rate into the original contract — locked in before project pressure gives the vendor leverage to set it unilaterally mid-project — combined with an internal budget reserve of roughly 10% to 15% above the base contract price specifically earmarked for anticipated changes, converts an unpredictable overrun risk into a planned, bounded line item that finance can actually forecast around rather than discover after the fact.

## Distinguishing a Legitimate Change Request From a Vendor Estimation Error

Not every change request is legitimate, and a CFO's finance team benefits from being able to tell the difference, because the two categories should be handled very differently in terms of who bears the cost. A legitimate change request originates from new information genuinely outside the original scope — a regulatory requirement that emerged after signing, a business decision to add functionality nobody had specified at contract time, or a third-party integration whose complexity only became clear once technical discovery began. A vendor estimation error, by contrast, surfaces work that was reasonably foreseeable within the original scope as described, but that the vendor underestimated or overlooked during the original quoting process — and this category of gap should generally be absorbed by the vendor, not billed to the client as a change request, since the client didn't change anything; the vendor's original estimate was simply wrong. In practice, distinguishing these categories requires the scope document to be detailed enough to make the distinction checkable, which circles back to why scope specificity at signing matters so much: a vague scope document doesn't just generate more change requests, it also makes it nearly impossible to tell which of those change requests the client should fairly be asked to pay for.

## Making the Final Call

Change requests are not inherently a sign of vendor bad faith or poor project management — they are a near-universal feature of software development, since no scope document written before implementation begins can anticipate every detail that surfaces once real development work is underway. What separates a controlled, budgeted change process from a 47% overrun is entirely a function of what the original contract specifies about pricing, approval governance, and cumulative caps before the first change request is ever raised.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do fixed-price software contracts so often end up over budget?

The overrun typically doesn't come from a single failure but from an accumulation of individually approved change requests, each priced above the base contract's effective rate, that aren't tracked cumulatively against the original budget until the project is well underway or complete.

### What causes most change requests on a software project?

Most change requests stem from scope documents that were ambiguous or incomplete at signing, rather than clients arbitrarily changing their minds. A scope document written in vague business terms rather than technical specifics tends to generate a steady stream of change requests during implementation.

### Should change requests be priced at the same rate as the base contract?

Not necessarily, since re-planning and re-integrating unplanned work carries genuine additional overhead, but the change request rate should be negotiated and stated explicitly in writing before signing, compared against the base contract's effective rate, rather than discovered on the first change order invoice.

### What's a reasonable cap on total change request spend?

A cap around 15% to 20% of the base contract value is a reasonable starting point for most projects, calibrated based on how detailed and technically specific the original scope document is. Beyond that threshold, a formal renegotiation should be triggered rather than continued incremental approval.

### How should a company budget for change requests before a project starts?

Rather than treating change requests as purely reactive, negotiate a pre-agreed change request rate into the original contract and set aside an internal budget reserve of roughly 10% to 15% above the base contract price specifically for anticipated scope evolution.
