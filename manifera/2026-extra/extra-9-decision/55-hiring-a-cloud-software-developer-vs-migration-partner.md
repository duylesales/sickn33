Hiring for a cloud project looks like a staffing decision. It isn't — it's a scoping decision wearing a staffing decision's clothes, and CEOs who treat it as the former routinely end up understaffed for problems they didn't know were coming. The real question was never "how many people do I need," it's "how many different kinds of expertise does this specific project actually require, and can one person credibly hold all of them at once." Most CEOs answer that question by instinct rather than by scoping the work first, which is exactly how a contained cloud task and a full-blown migration end up staffed identically — and priced identically wrong.

This comparison is written for exactly that decision point — you've already accepted that cloud work needs to happen, and now you're deciding whether one skilled cloud software developer can carry it, or whether the project genuinely needs a structured migration team behind it.

## What a Cloud Software Developer Can (and Can't) Do Alone

A strong individual cloud software developer can be genuinely excellent at specific, bounded tasks: configuring a CI/CD pipeline, setting up infrastructure-as-code for a single service, optimizing a cloud bill, or resolving a particular performance bottleneck. If your need is narrow, well-defined, and doesn't touch multiple interdependent systems, one skilled hire — whether an employee or a contractor — can move fast and cost less than a full team.

What a single developer generally can't do, no matter how skilled, is carry the full weight of a multi-system migration alone. Analysis of existing data flows, architecture planning that accounts for compliance requirements, staged implementation across several environments, and ongoing monitoring after go-live each require different skills and, more importantly, different amounts of sustained attention than one person can realistically sustain across a project that runs for months. A single developer working on a migration is also a single point of failure: if they're out sick during a critical cutover week, or if they leave mid-project, there's no bench behind them to absorb the gap.

## What a Full Migration Partner Brings That a Single Developer Doesn't

A structured migration partner brings a team built around the specific phases of a cloud migration — typically analysis, planning, implementation, and monitoring — with different people covering different phases rather than one generalist trying to be strong at all four simultaneously. That specialization matters more than it sounds like it should, because the skills needed to correctly assess a legacy system's dependencies are genuinely different from the skills needed to execute a zero-downtime cutover, and a team built around this division tends to catch risks that a generalist working solo would miss simply from being spread too thin.

A migration partner also brings redundancy and continuity that a single hire structurally cannot. If a specific engineer is unavailable during a critical week, the team absorbs it rather than the project stalling. And a partner engaged for the migration itself, rather than treating it as a side project for someone's existing workload, tends to bring documented process discipline — a defined analysis phase before implementation begins, checkpoints between phases, and a monitoring period after go-live specifically designed to catch configuration drift before it becomes a customer-facing incident.

## Head-to-Head: Cloud Software Developer vs. Full Migration Partner

**Single Cloud Software Developer.** Best suited to narrow, well-bounded tasks within an existing architecture — pipeline configuration, a single service migration, cost optimization work. Faster to engage, lower initial cost, but carries continuity risk and limited capacity for multi-system complexity. Works well when your cloud need is a feature, not a project.

**Full Cloud Migration Partner.** Best suited to end-to-end infrastructure moves involving multiple systems, compliance requirements such as GDPR data residency, or a full transition from on-premise to cloud infrastructure. Higher initial engagement cost and a longer onboarding period, but structured phases, built-in redundancy, and continuity that survives individual staff changes. Works well when your cloud need is the project itself, not a task within a larger one.

The deciding factor isn't company size or budget — it's project shape. A well-funded startup with a single, contained cloud task can reasonably hire one strong developer. A leaner startup facing a full migration with compliance stakes attached genuinely needs the structured partner model regardless of headcount, because the risk of an unmanaged migration going wrong outweighs the apparent cost savings of a solo hire.

## Cost and Timeline Reality Check

A single cloud software developer, hired as a contractor, typically runs €60-€90 per hour depending on seniority and location, with no fixed project timeline commitment — you're paying for time, and the project finishes when it finishes. A full migration engagement is usually quoted as a project or phased retainer, often in the range of several tens of thousands of euros for a mid-sized infrastructure move, but with a defined four-phase structure and target completion window built into the proposal.

The comparison that actually matters isn't the headline rate — it's the total cost including the risk of rework. A solo developer working through unfamiliar territory on a complex, multi-system migration frequently underestimates the discovery phase, leading to scope creep that erodes any hourly-rate savings within the first two months. ## Which One Actually Fits Your Project

Ask yourself three questions before deciding. First, does this project touch more than one system or environment? If yes, lean toward a partner. Second, does compliance — GDPR data residency, industry-specific regulation — attach real business risk to getting the architecture wrong? If yes, lean toward a partner with demonstrated experience in that specific compliance area. Third, can your business tolerate the project stalling for weeks if your one hired developer becomes unavailable? If the honest answer is no, a single hire is a fragile foundation for something this important.

This is also where combining Scrum discipline from the Netherlands with Vietnam's deep technical talent pool changes the economics of the "full partner" option in a way that narrows the gap with a solo hire. A structured migration team doesn't have to mean a large, slow-moving enterprise engagement — a dedicated pod model can scale up or down within two to four weeks as the project's phase changes, meaning you pay for a larger team during the intensive implementation phase and a smaller one during monitoring, rather than carrying a fixed team size for the full engagement regardless of workload. That flexibility is often the deciding factor for a CEO trying to reconcile a lean budget with a project that genuinely needs more than one person.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is hiring a single cloud software developer enough for a project?
A single skilled developer is usually sufficient when the work is narrow and bounded within an existing architecture, such as configuring a pipeline, optimizing a specific service, or managing cloud costs. It becomes insufficient once the project spans multiple interdependent systems, involves compliance requirements like GDPR data residency, or requires the continuity a solo hire structurally can't guarantee.

### What does a full cloud migration partner do that a freelance developer typically doesn't?
A full migration partner brings a structured, phased process — usually analysis, planning, implementation, and monitoring — executed by a team rather than one generalist, along with built-in redundancy if a specific team member becomes unavailable. This structure is specifically designed to catch risks in multi-system migrations that a single developer working alone is more likely to miss.

### How much does hiring a full cloud migration partner typically cost compared to a solo developer?
A solo contractor typically bills €60-€90 per hour with no fixed completion timeline, while a full migration engagement is usually quoted as a project or phased retainer often totaling several tens of thousands of euros for a mid-sized infrastructure move. The more useful comparison is total cost including rework risk, since underestimated discovery work on a complex migration frequently erodes a solo hire's apparent hourly-rate savings.

### Can a migration partner team scale up or down during the project?
Yes, a dedicated team model built around scalable engagement can typically adjust team size within two to four weeks as a project moves between phases, allowing a larger team during intensive implementation and a smaller one during the monitoring phase. This flexibility helps reconcile budget constraints with a project that genuinely requires more capacity than a single hire can provide.

### What are the biggest risks of choosing a solo developer for a project that actually needs a full migration team?
The primary risks are continuity (the project stalling if the developer becomes unavailable), underestimated discovery work leading to scope creep, and gaps in compliance-related architecture decisions that a generalist may not have specific experience navigating. These risks tend to surface a few months into the project rather than immediately, which is why the initial cost comparison alone can be misleading.
