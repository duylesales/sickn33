€35,000 vs. €65,000 — two quotes for what looks like the same app, submitted by two vendors in the same week. That gap is normal, and it's exactly the moment most non-technical founders make an expensive mistake: treating the lower number as the better deal instead of asking what's missing from it. The lowest quote isn't the cheapest option — it's usually a deferred bill, one that arrives three months after launch disguised as a "necessary rebuild" or an "unexpected scope change." By the time you're signing a final contract, you've likely collected three or four quotes with wildly different numbers attached to what feels like the same app. This article exists to bust the myths that make founders pick the wrong one.

None of this is a criticism of frugality — a tight runway is real, and every founder should scrutinize spending closely. The problem isn't caring about cost; it's measuring the wrong number. Build price is visible on day one. Total cost of ownership, technical debt, and the price of a rebuild eighteen months later are invisible until they happen, which is exactly why they're so easy to underweight when you're staring at three quotes side by side and one is 40% lower than the others. The myths below are the specific reasoning patterns that make that underweighting feel rational in the moment.

## Myth #1: A Lower Quote Means the Vendor Is More Efficient

**Myth ❌:** If Vendor A quotes €35,000 and Vendor B quotes €65,000 for what looks like the same app, Vendor A must simply be more efficient, or working with lower overhead, and you should take the savings.

**Fact ✅:** In the vast majority of cases, a quote that's 40-50% below the median isn't reflecting efficiency — it's reflecting scope that's been quietly narrowed. Vendor A's €35,000 quote almost never includes the same QA depth, the same number of design iteration rounds, or the same post-launch support window as Vendor B's. Ask both vendors for a line-item breakdown of design, development, QA, and support, and the "efficiency" usually turns out to be omission. A vendor that has actually scoped your full feature list accurately, including edge cases and device testing, will land in a fairly narrow band relative to competitors doing the same work. Wild variance is a signal to investigate, not a signal to celebrate.

Picture two quotes for the same fitness-tracking app concept. Vendor A's €35,000 quote covers a single platform, three rounds of design revision, and testing on four devices. Vendor B's €65,000 quote covers both iOS and Android from a shared codebase, five design rounds including usability testing with real target users, and a device matrix covering twelve models across both operating systems. These aren't the same app priced differently — they're two different products that happen to share a name. The founder who picks Vendor A because it's "cheaper" often ends up commissioning the Android version six months later at a price higher than Vendor B would have charged for both platforms together from the start.

## Myth #2: Cheaper Now Means Cheaper Overall

**Myth ❌:** The build price is the number that matters. Once the app is live, ongoing costs are minor and roughly the same regardless of which vendor you chose.

**Fact ✅:** The build price is typically 55-65% of what you'll actually spend on the app across its first 18 months. The rest goes to post-launch stabilization, feature iteration based on real user behavior, and ongoing maintenance — OS updates, security patches, and bug fixes that accumulate the moment real users start hitting edge cases your test plan didn't cover. A vendor who underpriced the build by cutting QA corners doesn't save you money; they shift the cost forward into a stabilization phase that now competes with your actual product roadmap for engineering time. Total cost of ownership, not build price, is the number that determines whether an app was actually "cheap."

Think about it in terms of the two founders who eventually end up spending the same total amount, but on very different timelines. Founder One pays €50,000 upfront to a vendor who scoped the project properly and delivers a stable app with a defined 90-day support window. Founder Two pays €32,000 upfront to a vendor who cut QA to hit that number, then pays another €12,000 over the next four months fixing crash reports and rebuilding a login flow that never worked reliably on Android, then another €8,000 for the maintenance retainer nobody mentioned during the sales call. Founder Two spent more, later, under worse conditions — mid-launch, with users already frustrated — while believing the entire time that they'd made the fiscally responsible choice.

## Myth #3: A Fixed-Price Quote Means the Price Is Actually Fixed

**Myth ❌:** Once you sign a fixed-price contract, that number is locked in no matter what happens during the build.

**Fact ✅:** A fixed-price quote is only as fixed as the change-order terms buried in the contract. Nearly every real project changes scope somewhat — you'll see the first working version and want to adjust a flow, or a feature you thought was simple turns out to require a third integration nobody scoped. What determines your real final cost of app development is whether those changes are priced at a rate you agreed to upfront, or negotiated ad hoc once you're already committed and have no leverage to walk away. Ask explicitly, before signing: what is the hourly or daily rate for out-of-scope work, and how is "out of scope" defined? A vendor without a clear answer here is the vendor most likely to hand you a very different final invoice.

It's worth reading the change-order clause of a contract as carefully as the price itself. Some contracts define scope so narrowly — down to a specific number of screens or a specific list of features with no room for the inevitable refinements — that almost any real-world adjustment technically counts as "out of scope," and gets billed accordingly at whatever rate the vendor decides to invoke after you've already paid the first milestone. A well-structured contract instead defines a reasonable buffer for minor adjustments within the agreed scope, and a clear, pre-agreed rate for anything genuinely beyond it. That distinction, more than the headline number, is what separates a founder who stays on budget from one who doesn't.

## Myth #4: You Should Optimize for the Fastest Delivery Timeline

**Myth ❌:** Since speed to market matters most for a first-time founder, the vendor promising the fastest delivery timeline is doing you the biggest favor.

**Fact ✅:** An aggressive timeline is often the flip side of the same coin as an aggressive lowball quote — both usually mean reduced QA cycles and less senior oversight, not genuinely superior process. A realistic mobile app timeline for a functional MVP with a handful of core features runs 10-16 weeks depending on complexity. A vendor promising half that isn't necessarily better organized; they may simply be compressing testing into a window too short to catch the bugs that surface once real users start using the app in ways your spec never anticipated. Ask any vendor promising an unusually fast timeline exactly how many QA cycles and device tests fit inside it — the math either works or it doesn't.

There's also a strategic cost to shipping too fast that has nothing to do with bugs: a rushed MVP built without proper discovery often solves the wrong version of the problem. Time saved in development gets spent several times over in a post-launch pivot, because the team never had the runway to validate assumptions about what users actually needed before writing code. A vendor who pushes back gently on an unrealistic timeline — proposing 12 weeks instead of promising 6 — is often doing you a bigger favor than one who says yes to whatever number gets the deal signed.

## Myth #5: Technical Details Are the Vendor's Job to Worry About, Not Yours

**Myth ❌:** As a non-technical founder, you shouldn't need to understand the technical tradeoffs — that's what you're paying the vendor for, and asking detailed questions just slows things down.

**Fact ✅:** You don't need to understand code, but you do need to understand the handful of decisions that materially change your app development cost and your future flexibility: native versus cross-platform, whether the backend is built to scale past your first thousand users or just to demo well, and who owns the source code and IP once the invoice is paid. A vendor worth hiring will explain these tradeoffs in plain business language without being asked twice, and a vendor unwilling to do that in language you understand is a vendor who may not be planning to explain the invoice in equally plain language either.

A useful test: ask any finalist to explain, in two minutes and without jargon, why they'd recommend native development, cross-platform, or a hybrid approach specifically for your app's use case. A vendor with real experience will connect the answer to your actual requirements — camera-heavy features favoring native, a simple content app favoring cross-platform for cost efficiency — rather than defaulting to whatever they happen to specialize in. If every vendor you talk to recommends the same technology regardless of what you describe, you're hearing their default sales pitch, not an assessment of your specific project.

## What This Means for Your Actual Decision

Once you strip away these five myths, the decision in front of you gets simpler, even if it's not more comfortable. Instead of ranking your finalists by the number at the bottom of the quote, rank them by three questions: does the line-item breakdown account for your actual feature list, does the contract define change orders and support terms explicitly, and can the vendor explain their architecture decisions in language you can verify against your own business goals.

## A Founder's Pre-Signature Gut Check

Before you sign with any vendor, run through this short list one more time. Does the quote break down design, development, QA, and support as separate line items, or is it one number? Does the contract specify a rate for out-of-scope work, or leave it open-ended? Is there a named support window after launch with a specific end date? Can the vendor explain, in a sentence you could repeat to a co-founder, why they chose the technical approach they did? And critically — does the total number in front of you reflect the build alone, or does it come with an honest estimate of what the first six months after launch will realistically cost on top of it?

A founder who asks these five questions before signing rarely ends up surprised three months later. A founder who picks based on the lowest number alone almost always does — not because the vendor was dishonest, necessarily, but because the myths above make it easy to compare the wrong thing. Print the five questions out, literally, and bring them to your final call with each finalist. The vendor who answers all five without hesitation is showing you exactly how they'll behave once you're a signed client rather than a prospect they're still trying to close.

The most expensive mistake in app development isn't overpaying. It's underpaying for something you'll have to pay for twice. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the cheapest app development cost quote ever actually the right choice?

Occasionally, but only when the low quote comes with the same line-item breakdown, QA depth, and support terms as competing quotes. If a low number comes with vague scope or no defined support window, it's rarely the better value once total cost of ownership is considered.

### How much of my total app spending happens after the initial build?

Typically 35-45% of total cost over the first 18 months goes to post-launch stabilization, iteration, and maintenance rather than the initial build. A quote that only addresses the build price is giving you an incomplete picture of what the app will actually cost.

### What's the biggest red flag in a low app development cost quote?

Vague or missing change-order terms. If a contract doesn't specify a rate for out-of-scope work, the fixed-price number you signed is not actually fixed once real development begins and requirements inevitably shift.

### Should I trust a vendor promising a much faster delivery timeline than competitors?

Ask specifically how many QA cycles and device tests fit inside that timeline. An unusually fast promise often means compressed testing rather than superior process, which shows up as post-launch bugs rather than time saved.

### As a non-technical founder, what questions should I ask about app development cost before signing?

Ask for a line-item breakdown across design, development, QA, and support; ask how change orders are priced; and ask the vendor to explain their technical approach in plain business language. A vendor who can't do the last one clearly may not communicate clearly about invoices later either.
