A website redesign quoted at €25,000 finishes at €68,000, and the founder who signed the original contract can point to exactly none of the individual change requests as unreasonable in isolation — a new integration here, an "quick" extra page there, a request to "just make it feel more premium" that turned into three additional design rounds. This is the defining pattern of website redesign scope creep: no single moment feels like the trap closing, and by the time the total is visible, walking away means losing everything already invested.

Founders are particularly exposed to this pattern because a website redesign often lacks the rigorous technical specification a software build gets — it feels more subjective, more "we'll know it when we see it," which is exactly the ambiguity a scope-creep-prone contract structure exploits. This article breaks down exactly how the trap works mechanically, and the specific contract language that prevents it, so the next redesign quote you sign is the number you actually pay.

## How the Trap Actually Works: The Anatomy of a €25K-to-€68K Overrun

Scope creep on a website redesign rarely arrives as one large, obviously out-of-scope request. It arrives as a sequence of individually small, individually reasonable-sounding additions, each approved quickly because it feels too minor to negotiate over. A discovery phase that was supposed to lock the sitemap discovers, mid-project, that the client actually needs a members-only resource section — reasonable, but a real scope addition. A design review surfaces that the homepage "doesn't feel premium enough," triggering a second and then third round of visual exploration that a rigid one-round-of-revisions clause should have priced separately but didn't, because the original contract never defined how many revision rounds were included. A late integration request — a CRM connection, a booking widget — turns out to need custom development because the vendor's initial estimate assumed an off-the-shelf plugin would suffice, and it doesn't.

Individually, each of these might be a legitimate €2,000-€6,000 addition. Collectively, over a 10-14 week project, they compound into the type of overrun described above, and by the time the founder notices the pattern, roughly 60-70% of the original budget may already be spent, making a vendor switch financially worse than absorbing the overrun. Recognizing this pattern before signing — not after the third "small" change order — is the actual point of leverage.

## The Discovery Phase Is Where Scope Should Be Locked, Not Skipped

The single most effective prevention mechanism is a dedicated, paid discovery phase that produces a detailed, written scope document — sitemap, page-by-page functional requirements, integration list, revision-round count — before the fixed-price build contract is signed. Many vendors skip or minimize this phase specifically because a vague scope gives them room to bill change orders later; a discovery phase that genuinely locks scope reduces their change-order revenue, which is a real, if rarely stated, incentive misalignment worth being aware of.

Insist on discovery being a distinct, separately deliverable phase — typically 1-3 weeks for a standard business website — producing a document you can hold the subsequent fixed-price contract against. If a vendor proposes skipping straight from a sales call to a fixed-price contract with no documented discovery output, that missing step is not a shortcut in your favor; it is the exact gap where scope creep later enters, disguised as "things we didn't know about at the start."

## Revision Rounds: The Clause Almost Every Contract Gets Wrong

"Includes design revisions" is one of the most quietly expensive phrases in a typical redesign contract, because it implies unlimited iteration while actually meaning whatever the vendor decides it means once a dispute arises. A properly scoped contract specifies an exact number of revision rounds per major deliverable — commonly two structured rounds for homepage design, two for the overall design system — with any additional round priced explicitly, typically at an hourly or fixed per-round rate agreed in advance rather than negotiated mid-project when your leverage is weakest.

Ask directly, before signing: "What exactly happens, contractually, if we want a third round of homepage revisions?" A vendor with a well-structured contract will have an immediate, specific answer — a defined rate, a defined process. A vendor who says "we'll just work it out" is preserving flexibility that, in practice, tends to resolve in the vendor's favor once you are emotionally and financially invested in the relationship.

## Change Orders: Make the Process Visible Before You Need It

Legitimate scope changes happen on almost every redesign project — new requirements surface, priorities shift, and a contract that pretends this will never happen is unrealistic. The protection is not preventing all change, it is making every change visible and priced at the moment it is requested, rather than absorbed silently into "the project" until the final invoice reveals the cumulative total. Insist on a defined change order process: any request outside the locked discovery-phase scope gets a written estimate before work begins, requiring your explicit sign-off, with a running total of approved change orders visible throughout the project rather than surfacing only at final invoicing.

This single practice — a visible, cumulative change-order total updated in real time — is the most effective single defense against the slow-compounding overrun pattern, because it converts an invisible drift into a series of explicit decisions you make consciously, at the moment each request is made, rather than discovering retroactively. ## Fixed-Price vs. Time-and-Materials: Neither Is Automatically Safer

Founders often assume a fixed-price contract inherently protects them from scope creep, but a fixed-price contract with a vague underlying scope document is arguably more exposed, not less, because every ambiguity becomes a change-order opportunity for the vendor. A time-and-materials contract, conversely, is transparent about cost per hour but requires disciplined budget tracking on your side to avoid a runaway total. The real protection is not the pricing model — it is the specificity of the underlying scope document the pricing model is built against. A fixed-price contract against a genuinely detailed discovery-phase scope document is safer than either a vague fixed-price contract or an unmonitored time-and-materials arrangement.

## Making the Final Call

Website redesign scope creep is rarely a single dramatic vendor failure — it is a sequence of individually reasonable-sounding additions that compound because the original contract never defined its own boundaries clearly enough to make each addition visible as an addition. A paid discovery phase producing a real scope document, an explicit revision-round count, and a visible, sign-off-required change order process are the three mechanisms that convert an open-ended relationship into a bounded one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do website redesign projects so often go over budget?
Overruns typically happen through a sequence of individually small, reasonable-sounding additions — an extra integration, additional design rounds, a late-discovered requirement — rather than one dramatic scope change. By the time the pattern is visible, a significant share of the original budget is often already spent, making a vendor switch financially unattractive.

### What should a discovery phase produce before I sign a fixed-price contract?
A detailed, written scope document including the sitemap, page-by-page functional requirements, integration list, and the exact number of included revision rounds. A vendor proposing to skip straight from a sales call to a fixed-price contract without this documented output is skipping the step that would otherwise prevent scope creep.

### How many design revision rounds should be included in a redesign contract?
There is no universal number, but the contract should specify an exact count per major deliverable — commonly around two structured rounds for a homepage design — with any additional round priced explicitly and agreed before the project starts, not negotiated mid-project.

### Is a fixed-price contract safer than time-and-materials for avoiding scope creep?
Not automatically. A fixed-price contract with a vague scope document is arguably more exposed, since every ambiguity becomes a change-order opportunity. The real protection is the specificity of the underlying scope document, not the pricing model itself.

### What's the single most effective defense against scope creep during a project?
A visible, cumulative change-order process — every request outside the locked scope gets a written estimate and requires explicit sign-off before work begins, with a running total visible throughout the project rather than surfacing only at final invoicing.
