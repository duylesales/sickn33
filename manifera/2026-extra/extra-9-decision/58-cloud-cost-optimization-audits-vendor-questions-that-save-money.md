Cloud cost audits routinely find 25% to 35% of enterprise cloud spend going to waste — but the audit finding the waste is rarely the part that fails. One European media-tech company's finance team approved an audit expecting a modest cleanup and got a report citing 41% potential savings, a number so large it triggered its own skepticism before anyone had reviewed the methodology. The number turned out to be real, but almost none of it was rightsizing: the bulk came from a reserved-instance commitment strategy the vendor recommended but was never contractually obligated to help implement, and eight months later, with no one owning the follow-through, less than a third of the identified savings had actually been captured. The audit itself had been accurate. What was missing was a structure that converted the findings into realized savings rather than a report that sat in a shared drive.

This is the pattern CFOs most need to guard against with cloud cost optimization audits: the audit itself is rarely the hard part, since cloud waste is genuinely common and genuinely findable. The hard part is asking the right questions upfront so the audit produces something your organization can actually act on, rather than an impressive-looking report that becomes shelf-ware within a quarter. This article covers the specific questions worth asking a cloud cost optimization vendor before you commission the audit, not after you're holding the results.

## Question 1: What's Our Current Waste Percentage, With Evidence?

Ask the vendor for a preliminary, evidence-based estimate of your current cloud waste before the full audit even begins, based on a quick review of your billing data and account structure. A vendor with genuine expertise can usually give a rough, appropriately caveated range within a short initial review — informed by patterns across their client base — rather than committing to a full engagement before offering any indication of scale. Industry benchmarks consistently show 25% to 35% of enterprise cloud spend going to waste in the form of idle resources, oversized instances, and unused reservations, so a vendor's preliminary estimate should be evaluated against that range: an estimate wildly above it deserves scrutiny of the methodology, and an estimate near zero raises the question of why the audit is being commissioned in the first place.

## Question 2: Are You Recommending Committed Spend, or Just Rightsizing?

Cloud cost savings generally come from two different levers that carry very different risk profiles: rightsizing and eliminating waste, which is close to pure savings with minimal downside, and committing to reserved instances or savings plans, which trades a discount for reduced flexibility and requires accurately forecasting usage over a one- to three-year horizon. A vendor's audit report should clearly separate these two categories rather than blending them into a single headline savings figure, because a CFO evaluating the recommendation needs to weigh the commitment risk of the second category differently from the essentially risk-free savings of the first. Ask specifically what percentage of the total projected savings depends on a multi-year spend commitment, and stress-test that commitment against your own usage growth forecast before treating it as guaranteed savings.

## Question 3: How Does the Vendor Get Paid, and Does It Align With Your Savings?

This is the single question most likely to reveal whether a vendor's incentives are aligned with your actual outcome. A vendor paid a flat audit fee regardless of findings has no direct financial incentive to prioritize the highest-impact recommendations over easier, lower-value ones. A vendor paid a percentage of realized savings — not projected savings, realized — has direct skin in the game to make sure recommendations actually get implemented, since their fee depends on it. Ask explicitly how the engagement is priced, and if it's a flat fee, ask what mechanism ensures the vendor is still incentivized to prioritize your highest-value opportunities over the audit's easiest line items to write up.

## Question 4: What's Explicitly Excluded From the Audit Scope?

Every cloud cost audit has a scope boundary, and the boundary matters as much as what's included, because vendors sometimes exclude the categories that are hardest to audit but also often the most expensive — custom data transfer patterns, third-party SaaS spend routed through the cloud bill, or workloads owned by teams the vendor didn't get stakeholder access to during the engagement. Ask for the exclusions explicitly, in writing, before the audit starts, so a report showing impressive savings on the included scope doesn't create a false sense that your total cloud spend has been comprehensively reviewed when a meaningful category was never examined at all.

## Question 5: Can You Show Verified Before-and-After Data From a Comparable Client?

Ask for actual before-and-after billing data — not a case study summary, the underlying numbers — from a client of comparable size and cloud maturity to yours. A vendor with a genuine track record will have this readily available, often with the client's permission to share anonymized figures, because it's their strongest sales evidence. A vendor who can only offer general claims about "typical client outcomes" without a specific, verifiable before-and-after example is asking you to take the value proposition on faith, which is a reasonable thing to decline before committing budget to the engagement.

## One-Time Audit or Ongoing FinOps Engagement: Which Fits Your Spend

A one-time audit makes sense for a company whose cloud environment is relatively stable and hasn't been reviewed in a year or more, where a single cleanup pass captures most of the available waste. For a company with a fast-growing or frequently changing infrastructure footprint, a single audit produces a snapshot that starts decaying the moment new resources are provisioned after the engagement ends, which is why an ongoing FinOps engagement — recurring reviews on a quarterly or monthly cadence, often paired with automated anomaly detection that flags new waste as it appears rather than waiting for the next scheduled audit — tends to deliver more durable savings for companies above a certain growth rate. Ask a vendor proposing a one-time audit directly whether they also offer an ongoing option, and ask them to make the case for which model actually fits your company's specific growth trajectory rather than defaulting to whichever engagement type is easier for them to sell. A vendor who only offers one-time audits regardless of client profile is optimizing for their own sales cycle rather than for your long-term cost trajectory, and it's worth weighing the ongoing option's higher cumulative cost against the compounding waste a single snapshot audit will inevitably miss within a few quarters of infrastructure growth.

## Turning Audit Findings Into a Contract, Not Just a Report

The single most important structural decision a CFO can make is refusing to let the audit end with a report. Build the engagement so that implementation accountability is contractually tied to the findings — either through a percentage-of-realized-savings fee structure that keeps the vendor engaged through implementation, or through a defined implementation phase with named owners, deadlines, and a follow-up review at 90 and 180 days to confirm the savings were actually captured, not just identified. An audit that stops at the recommendation stage places the entire implementation burden back on an internal team that is, in most cases, already stretched thin — which is precisely why so many accurate, well-researched cloud cost audits end up producing far less realized savings than their headline number promised.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's a realistic amount of cloud waste to expect from an audit?

Industry benchmarks consistently show 25% to 35% of enterprise cloud spend going to waste in the form of idle resources, oversized instances, and unused reservations. A vendor's preliminary estimate should be evaluated against that range before committing to a full audit engagement.

### Should a cloud cost optimization vendor be paid a flat fee or a percentage of savings?

A percentage-of-realized-savings structure generally aligns incentives better, since the vendor only gets paid once savings are actually captured rather than merely identified. A flat fee structure isn't inherently wrong, but it requires a separate mechanism to ensure the vendor prioritizes high-impact recommendations.

### Why does it matter whether savings come from rightsizing or committed spend?

Rightsizing and waste elimination carry minimal downside risk, while committed spend discounts trade flexibility for savings and depend on accurately forecasting usage over one to three years. These two categories carry different risk profiles and should be evaluated separately, not blended into a single headline savings figure.

### What should be explicitly excluded from a cloud cost audit's scope?

Ask the vendor for exclusions in writing before the audit begins. Common gaps include custom data transfer patterns, third-party SaaS spend routed through the cloud bill, and workloads owned by teams the vendor lacked stakeholder access to during the engagement.

### How can I verify a cloud cost audit vendor's track record before signing?

Request actual before-and-after billing data, not a case study summary, from a client of comparable size and cloud maturity. A vendor with a genuine track record will typically have this evidence readily available with client permission to share it.
