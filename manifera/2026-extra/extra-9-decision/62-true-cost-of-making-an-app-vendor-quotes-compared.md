€38,000. €96,000. €61,000. Three quotes for the same one-page specification, sent by a VP of Engineering at a Dutch logistics scale-up to three vendors within the same week, for what was on paper identical work — a customer-facing tracking app with account management, live shipment status, and a notifications layer. The confusing part wasn't the size of the spread; specs always draw a range. It was that none of the three proposals explained why their number was the one it was. That gap is the real subject of this article: not which number is "right," but what each number actually buys, because the cost of making an app only means something once you know what is priced inside it.

This comparison walks through three representative vendor quote types for the same specification, structured the way you should structure your own vendor evaluation — line by line, not headline number against headline number. If you are the person who has to defend the final vendor choice to a CFO or a board, being able to explain the gap between quotes matters just as much as picking the right one, and that explanation only exists once you have pulled the numbers apart.

## Why the Cost of Making an App Can Vary by 3x for the Same Spec

A specification document describes what the app should do. It rarely describes how thoroughly QA will be run, what warranty period applies, how many stakeholder review cycles are included, or what happens when a requirement turns out to be more complex than it read on paper. Vendors fill in those gaps differently based on their business model, their risk tolerance, and how badly they want the deal. A freelancer marketplace bid and a boutique agency bid can respond to the identical document with wildly different assumptions about scope, and both can honestly believe their number is fair.

The three quotes below are composites built from patterns we see repeatedly when engineering leaders share competing bids with us during vendor selection — not one specific client's numbers, but a realistic representation of the three tiers most CTOs and VPs of Engineering encounter. Each tier corresponds to a different business model, and understanding that model explains far more about the price than the feature list ever will. A marketplace coordinator, a boutique agency, and a structured dedicated team are not competing on the same cost curve, because their underlying overhead, staffing model, and risk tolerance are fundamentally different from one another.

## Quote A: The Freelancer Marketplace Bid — €38,000

This quote came in fastest and cheapest, assembled by a project coordinator who would subcontract the actual coding to individual freelancers sourced per-module. The proposal listed features but no named engineering team, no stated QA process beyond "testing included," and a single revision round. There was no mention of a device testing matrix, no defined warranty period, and no detail on who would own the codebase documentation after handover.

The risk here is not that the work will be bad — some freelancer-sourced builds turn out fine — but that there is no continuity if a subcontractor drops off mid-project, which happens often enough in marketplace models to be a real planning risk, not a hypothetical one. For a VP of Engineering accountable for a delivery date, an unnamed and swappable team is a harder risk to defend to your own leadership than a documented cost gap.

## Quote B: The Boutique Western Agency Bid — €96,000

This quote was the most polished, with a detailed process document, named senior staff, and a five-stage delivery methodology. It also included the most padding: senior-rate hours billed for tasks that do not require senior engineers, a discovery phase priced at nearly a fifth of the total budget, and account management overhead baked into every line item. The quality bar is likely genuine, but so is the premium — much of which reflects the agency's own cost structure (Western European senior salaries, office overhead, sales commission) rather than the specific complexity of this app.

For engineering leaders with a strict budget ceiling, this tier often prices out the project entirely, forcing a scope cut that undermines the very quality the higher price was supposed to buy.

## Quote C: The Structured Offshore Dedicated Team Bid — €61,000

This quote sat in the middle and, on closer inspection, had the most complete line-item breakdown of the three: named engineers with stated seniority levels, a defined QA process with a specific device matrix, a 60-day warranty period, and documentation handover specified as a deliverable rather than an afterthought. The pricing reflected [European project governance paired with Southeast Asian engineering talent](https://www.manifera.com/services/custom-software-development/) — Agile ceremonies, sprint reporting, and code review standards run to a European delivery process, executed by engineers whose day rates are structurally lower than Western Europe's without cutting corners on process.

This is the model Manifera builds around for [custom software development](https://www.manifera.com/services/custom-software-development/) engagements: the governance layer that makes a mid-priced quote trustworthy is not marketing language, it is a specific set of practices — sprint demos, written acceptance criteria, and a named point of contact — that a VP of Engineering can actually verify before signing.

## Side-by-Side: What Each Quote Actually Included

Laid next to each other, the real differences were not in the feature list — all three covered the same core features — but in what surrounded them. Quote A included no named team and one revision round. Quote B included a named senior team and five revision rounds, but at senior billing rates for every task. Quote C included named mid-to-senior engineers matched to task complexity, a defined QA matrix, and a 60-day warranty — the combination that most closely matched what the VP of Engineering actually needed to defend the choice internally.

None of the three quotes was objectively "wrong." Each reflected a coherent business model. The mistake would be comparing only the top-line number without pulling apart what sits underneath it, because the €38,000 and €96,000 bids were not more or less expensive versions of the same thing — they were different products wearing the same specification. A CFO reading only the totals would see a €58,000 spread and assume the decision is about risk appetite; an engineering leader who has read the line items sees a decision about process maturity, team continuity, and post-launch support instead, which is a very different conversation to have internally.

## Red Flags That Only Surfaced Under Scrutiny

Two details emerged only when the VP of Engineering pushed for clarification. Quote A's coordinator could not name which specific freelancers would be assigned until after the deposit was paid — a sequencing that puts the buyer's money down before the buyer knows who is doing the work. Quote B's discovery phase, on closer reading, was scoped broadly enough that "discovery" could plausibly stretch to six weeks before a single feature was built, delaying the delivery timeline the VP needed to hit for a board commitment.

Quote C had its own caveat worth naming honestly: because pricing reflected offshore day rates, the VP needed to confirm working-hours overlap explicitly, since a dedicated team without confirmed CET overlap can undercut its own cost advantage through slower feedback loops. Manifera's teams are structured around a 4-5 hour working overlap between Ho Chi Minh City and the Netherlands specifically to close that gap, and this is the kind of detail worth confirming with any offshore vendor rather than assuming it is standard practice across the industry.

It is worth being fair to all three vendors here: none of these caveats are disqualifying on their own. A marketplace model can work for a low-stakes internal tool with a flexible timeline. A premium agency can be the right call when brand reputation and Western-market presence matter more than budget. The point of this comparison is not that one tier is universally correct, but that each tier carries a specific, nameable trade-off that a VP of Engineering should be able to state out loud before committing budget to it.

## The Cost Nobody Puts in Year One: Post-Launch Ownership

None of the three quotes said much about what happens twelve months after launch, which is precisely why it is worth asking directly. Quote A's freelancer model offered no maintenance retainer at all — once the app shipped, the coordinator's involvement effectively ended, leaving the VP of Engineering to either rehire the same freelancers individually or start a fresh vendor search for maintenance alone. Quote B's agency offered an ongoing retainer priced at nearly €4,500 per month, calculated at the same senior billing rates used for the original build, regardless of how light the actual maintenance workload turned out to be.

Quote C proposed a scaled maintenance arrangement tied to actual ticket volume and infrastructure monitoring, starting near €900 per month and adjustable as usage grew — a structure that reflects the flexibility to scale a team up or down within two to four weeks rather than locking the client into a fixed retainer sized for the busiest possible month. For a VP of Engineering thinking in multi-year total cost of ownership rather than a single launch invoice, this difference compounds significantly over 24 months.

## Using the Comparison as Negotiation Leverage

Once you have broken down three quotes to this level of detail, you have more than a decision — you have leverage. Sharing the specific line items missing from a cheaper bid with a mid-tier vendor, or asking a premium agency to justify why senior rates apply to routine tasks, routinely produces revised quotes that are both more accurate and more competitive than the initial numbers. Vendors price defensively when they suspect you are comparing headline totals only; they price more honestly once they know you are reading the line items.

This is also the point in the process where it is worth asking each vendor for a reference client with a similar app complexity and tech stack, and actually calling that reference rather than treating the case study as sufficient. A vendor confident in their line-item pricing will connect you directly; a vendor uncomfortable with scrutiny will stall, delay, or offer a reference so generic it tells you nothing about how they handle a project like yours.

## What This Comparison Means for Your Own Evaluation

If you are staring at your own set of competing quotes, resist ranking them by total price alone. Build a simple comparison table with rows for named team members, QA process detail, warranty period, revision rounds, and documentation handover, and score each vendor's quote against those rows rather than against each other's headline number. The vendor whose quote answers the most rows in writing, not the vendor whose number is smallest, is the one most likely to deliver what you actually signed up for.

The cost of making an app was never really the question worth asking in isolation. A VP of Engineering who can walk into a budget review and explain precisely why a €61,000 quote beat a €38,000 one — naming the QA matrix, the warranty period, and the documentation handover as the deciding factors — is in a far stronger position than one who can only say the number "felt right." That difference in defensibility is worth more than the few thousand euros separating the middle bids in almost every case we have seen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does the cost of making an app vary so much between vendor quotes for the same spec?
Vendors fill in the gaps a specification leaves open — QA depth, warranty period, revision rounds, and team seniority — very differently based on their business model. A specification alone does not fix these variables, so identical documents can produce quotes that differ by two to three times.

### Is the cheapest app development quote ever the right choice?
Sometimes, but only when the low price reflects genuine efficiency rather than omitted scope. Check whether the quote names the actual engineers assigned, states a QA process, and defines a warranty period before assuming the lower number is the better deal.

### What should I compare beyond the total price when evaluating app development quotes?
Compare named team members and seniority, QA process and device coverage, number of revision rounds, warranty period length, and documentation handover terms. These line items explain most of the price difference between competing quotes far better than the feature list alone.

### Are offshore dedicated team quotes for app development reliable on quality?
Yes, when the team operates under clear Agile governance with confirmed working-hours overlap and code review standards matched to what you would enforce in-house. Quality in a dedicated team model depends on process discipline, not on where the engineers are physically located.

### How long should discovery take before I get a firm app development quote?
A properly scoped discovery phase for a mid-complexity app typically takes one to two weeks, not several. A discovery phase priced or scheduled significantly longer than that is often a sign the agency is billing broadly rather than scoping tightly.
