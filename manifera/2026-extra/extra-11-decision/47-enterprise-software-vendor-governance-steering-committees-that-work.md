A quarterly vendor steering committee meets for ninety minutes, the vendor presents a status deck with green status indicators across every workstream, three people ask clarifying questions, and everyone leaves with a shared sense that things are on track. Two months later, a delivery milestone slips by six weeks, and when the IT Manager who chairs the committee reconstructs what happened, the slippage had been visible in the vendor's own internal tracking for over a month before the quarterly meeting — it just never made it into the green-status deck, and the committee's cadence was too infrequent and too passive to catch it before it became unrecoverable. The committee existed. It met on schedule. It simply wasn't structured to surface the information that would have let anyone act on it in time.

For an IT Manager responsible for standing up or fixing a vendor governance structure, the difference between a steering committee that provides real oversight and one that's a ceremonial quarterly check-in usually comes down to four structural choices: cadence matched to risk level, a genuine RACI defining who can actually act on what's raised, an escalation path that doesn't depend on someone volunteering bad news, and a KPI dashboard built from data the vendor doesn't fully control.

## Cadence: Matched to Risk, Not to Calendar Convenience

A quarterly cadence is common because it's administratively convenient, not because it matches the pace at which vendor delivery risk actually develops. For an active, high-risk engagement — a major implementation, a system with direct customer or revenue impact, or a vendor relationship still in its first year where trust and working patterns are still being established — a monthly operational review paired with a quarterly strategic steering session is a more realistic cadence: the monthly review catches emerging delivery issues while they're still recoverable, and the quarterly session handles the higher-level relationship, contract, and roadmap decisions that don't need monthly attention.

For a mature, stable, lower-risk vendor relationship — a long-tenured vendor managing well-understood, non-critical systems — quarterly alone may genuinely be sufficient, and monthly reviews for every vendor relationship regardless of risk level just produces meeting fatigue that erodes the seriousness of the sessions that actually matter. Tier vendor relationships by risk and criticality, and set cadence per tier rather than applying one calendar to every vendor uniformly.

## RACI: Who Can Actually Decide Something in the Room

A steering committee that includes people with no actual decision authority produces meetings that generate discussion but no action — issues get raised, acknowledged, and carried to the next meeting because nobody in the room could authorize the resourcing, budget, or scope change needed to resolve them. Define a clear RACI for the committee itself: who is Accountable for the vendor relationship overall (typically the IT Manager or a designated vendor relationship owner), who is Responsible for specific workstream delivery (both client-side and vendor-side leads), who must be Consulted (security, legal, or business stakeholders affected by decisions), and who is merely Informed (broader stakeholders who receive summaries but don't attend working sessions).

Critically, ensure the people in the room with Accountable or Responsible roles actually hold budget or decision authority proportionate to what the committee needs to resolve — a steering committee stacked with informed-only attendees and no one who can approve a change order or reallocate resource is a reporting exercise, not governance.

## Escalation Paths That Don't Depend on the Vendor Volunteering Bad News

The failure in the opening example — a slipping milestone that never made it into the status deck — is structurally common because the default reporting flow relies on the vendor's own team to self-report problems, and vendor account teams have real professional incentive to present status optimistically, particularly ahead of a renewal or expansion conversation. A working governance structure builds in reporting mechanisms that don't depend entirely on vendor self-disclosure: client-side project management visibility into the vendor's actual delivery tracking (not just a vendor-curated summary), defined objective status criteria (a milestone is "on track" only if specific, verifiable conditions are met, not based on subjective vendor assessment), and a defined escalation trigger — for example, any milestone showing no measurable progress for two consecutive reporting periods automatically escalates to the steering committee regardless of the vendor's self-reported status.

This isn't adversarial vendor management — it's structuring governance so that a problem surfaces through the process rather than depending on any individual's judgment call about when something is "bad enough" to flag.

## The KPI Dashboard: Built From Data the Vendor Doesn't Fully Control

Vendor-reported KPIs are useful but insufficient on their own, because they measure what the vendor chooses to measure and report through their own systems. A more resilient governance dashboard combines vendor-reported delivery metrics (velocity, milestone status, defect rates) with independently verifiable data the client side controls directly — actual system uptime from client-side monitoring rather than the vendor's own SLA dashboard, ticket resolution times from the client's own ticketing system, and user adoption or satisfaction data gathered independently of the vendor's account team.

This combination matters especially for [enforceable SLA relationships](https://www.manifera.com/blog/enterprise-vendor-contracts-uptime-slas-that-are-actually-enforceable) — a steering committee reviewing only the vendor's self-reported uptime dashboard has no independent way to verify an SLA breach even occurred.

## Documenting Decisions: The Governance Record That Survives Turnover

Every steering committee meeting should produce a written decision record — not just meeting minutes, but a specific log of decisions made, who made them, what alternative was considered and rejected, and what action items resulted with named owners and dates. This record is what lets a new IT Manager, a new vendor account lead, or an internal audit reconstruct why the relationship is structured the way it is, without depending on institutional memory that walks out the door with personnel turnover on either side.

## Making the Final Call

A vendor steering committee that meets on schedule but doesn't catch problems while they're recoverable isn't providing governance — it's providing the appearance of governance. The structural fixes are specific and known: cadence tiered to actual risk, a RACI that puts real decision authority in the room, escalation paths that don't depend on vendor self-disclosure, and a KPI dashboard built partly from data the vendor doesn't control.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a vendor steering committee meet?
Cadence should be tiered to the vendor relationship's risk and criticality, not set uniformly — a monthly operational review paired with quarterly strategic sessions for high-risk or first-year engagements, and quarterly alone for mature, stable, lower-risk relationships. Applying monthly cadence to every vendor regardless of risk produces meeting fatigue that erodes the seriousness of sessions that actually matter.

### What is a RACI and why does it matter for vendor governance?
RACI defines who is Accountable, Responsible, Consulted, and Informed for the vendor relationship, and its value in governance is ensuring the people with actual decision or budget authority are the ones designated Accountable or Responsible. A committee full of informed-only attendees with no one able to approve a change order produces discussion but no action.

### Why do vendor status reports sometimes miss real delivery problems?
Vendor account teams have professional incentive to present status optimistically, especially ahead of a renewal conversation, and a reporting flow that depends entirely on vendor self-disclosure inherits that incentive. Defined objective status criteria and automatic escalation triggers — not based on subjective vendor judgment — close this gap.

### What should a vendor governance KPI dashboard include beyond vendor-reported metrics?
Independently verifiable data the client side controls directly — actual system uptime from client-side monitoring, ticket resolution times from the client's own systems, and user satisfaction data gathered independently of the vendor's account team. Relying solely on vendor-reported metrics means the client has no independent way to verify whether an SLA or delivery commitment was actually met.

### What should be documented after every steering committee meeting?
A specific decision record — not just minutes — capturing what was decided, who decided it, what alternatives were considered and rejected, and named owners with dates for resulting action items. This record is what lets the governance structure survive personnel turnover on either the client or vendor side.
