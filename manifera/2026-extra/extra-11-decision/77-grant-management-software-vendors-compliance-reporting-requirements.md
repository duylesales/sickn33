An organization spending $750,000 or more in federal awards in a fiscal year triggers a Single Audit under the Single Audit Act, requiring an independent auditor to test compliance with the specific requirements attached to each federal program, not just review financial statements generally. That audit pulls directly from whatever system tracked the grant — and a grant management platform that can produce a clean expenditure report but can't produce a defensible audit trail tying each dollar to its specific compliance requirement is a platform that will make that audit meaningfully harder and more expensive than it needs to be. Most grant management software vendor evaluations focus on tracking features — deadlines, application status, reporting calendars — and underweight the actual compliance-reporting architecture an audit will test.

## 2 CFR 200 (Uniform Guidance) and What It Actually Requires of a System

For any organization managing U.S. federal grants, 2 CFR 200 — the Uniform Administrative Requirements, Cost Principles, and Audit Requirements for Federal Awards, commonly called the Uniform Guidance — sets the compliance framework a grant management system needs to support, not just reference. This includes specific requirements around allowability of costs (can the system flag or restrict expenditures against categories the grant doesn't allow), time and effort reporting for personnel charged to federal awards, procurement standards for subawards and subcontracts funded by the grant, and record retention (generally a minimum of three years from the date of the final expenditure report, longer in specific circumstances).

The due diligence question for a compliance officer: does the vendor's platform structurally enforce these requirements — configurable budget categories tied to allowability rules, built-in time-and-effort tracking workflows, automated retention scheduling — or does it just provide generic fields you'd have to configure and police manually. A platform built generically for "nonprofit operations" without federal grant compliance as a design requirement will often have the data fields to record this information, but not the enforcement logic that actually prevents a compliance gap before it happens.

## The Single Audit Threshold and Sub-Recipient Monitoring

Organizations that pass federal funds through to sub-recipients — a common model for larger nonprofits and government agencies distributing grant funds to smaller local organizations — take on a specific compliance burden under Uniform Guidance: monitoring sub-recipient compliance, verifying sub-recipients are meeting the same audit requirements where applicable, and maintaining documentation of that monitoring. A grant management platform serving this use case needs sub-recipient tracking as a distinct data model from direct-expense tracking, with its own reporting workflows, risk assessment documentation, and monitoring visit or desk-review records. Vendors who treat sub-recipients as just another vendor or contact record, without a compliance-specific data structure, are underbuilt for organizations that pass through federal funds.

## EU Structural Fund Reporting: A Different System Entirely

Organizations managing EU grant funding — Horizon Europe, European Social Fund Plus (ESF+), European Regional Development Fund (ERDF) — report through entirely different mechanisms than U.S. federal grant reporting, most centrally the SFC2021 system (the European Commission's shared fund management IT platform for the current programming period). Reporting cadences, eligible cost categories, and audit trail requirements differ by fund and by managing authority, and a grant management platform built primarily around U.S. federal compliance patterns often has no meaningful support for EU structural fund reporting structures at all. If your organization manages both U.S. federal and EU grant portfolios, verify explicitly whether the platform genuinely supports both reporting frameworks as first-class configurations, or whether EU fund reporting would need to happen entirely outside the system in spreadsheets — which defeats much of the point of a centralized grant management platform.

## Audit Trail Depth: What "Reporting" Actually Needs to Preserve

A compliance-adequate audit trail isn't just a report showing final numbers — it needs to preserve who entered each transaction, when, under what approval, tied to which specific budget line and grant requirement, with the ability to reconstruct the full history of any given expenditure on demand. Ask vendors directly: if an auditor requests the complete history of a specific transaction two years after the fact — every edit, every approval, every category reassignment — can the system produce that without manual reconstruction from emails and spreadsheets. A platform where budget categories can be silently edited after the fact, without a preserved change history, is a platform that will generate exactly the kind of audit finding compliance officers are trying to avoid.

## Real-Time vs. Periodic Reporting, and Funder Expectations

Funders increasingly expect reporting visibility beyond the traditional quarterly or annual report cycle, particularly for larger grants or ones tied to specific milestone-based disbursement. Evaluate whether a candidate platform can produce funder-specific report formats on demand rather than only at scheduled intervals, and whether it supports the increasingly common requirement for outcome and impact metrics alongside financial compliance data — many funders now expect programmatic results reporting integrated with financial reporting, not delivered as a separate, disconnected narrative document. A platform that only handles the financial compliance side, with programmatic outcomes tracked entirely elsewhere, creates exactly the kind of reporting fragmentation that makes audit season and funder reporting season both harder than necessary.

## Red Flags in Grant Management Vendor Evaluation

Treat these as significant warning signs: a vendor that can't produce a sample audit trail report in a demo, on request, showing the full history of a transaction; a vendor whose retention scheduling is manual rather than automated against your organization's actual retention policy; a vendor with no distinct sub-recipient monitoring workflow if your organization passes through funds; and a vendor whose only answer to "does this support 2 CFR 200 cost allowability rules" is a generic "our system is flexible enough for that" rather than a specific description of configurable allowability logic.

## Making the Compliance Reporting Call

The vendors worth shortlisting for grant management are the ones who can demonstrate — in a live demo, not a feature list — that their platform enforces cost allowability, preserves a genuine audit trail, and supports sub-recipient monitoring and the specific reporting framework (federal Uniform Guidance, EU structural fund, or both) your organization actually operates under. A platform that looks capable in a sales demo but can't survive an auditor's actual document request two years later is a platform that will cost your organization far more in audit findings and remediation than it saved in licensing fees. ## The Compliance Reporting Checklist to Run in Every Vendor Demo

Don't accept a slide deck — require every shortlisted vendor to demonstrate these five things live against sample data before you score them: (1) create a sample expenditure that violates an allowability rule and confirm the system flags or blocks it, not just logs it after the fact; (2) pull the complete edit history of a single transaction, including every category reassignment and approval, timestamped and attributed to a specific user; (3) generate a sub-recipient risk assessment and monitoring record from a blank template, not a pre-built demo file; (4) export a report in a specific funder's required format — ideally one you actually report to — built from live data, not a static PDF sample; (5) show the retention schedule automatically calculating a three-year-minimum disposal date from the final expenditure report date, without manual entry. Score each vendor pass/fail on all five rather than averaging a subjective "looked capable" impression — a platform that fails even one of these live is a platform your auditor will find the same gap in eighteen months later, at a much higher cost than a failed demo.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What triggers a Single Audit requirement under the Single Audit Act?
An organization expending $750,000 or more in federal awards in a single fiscal year triggers the requirement for an independent Single Audit, which tests compliance with the specific requirements attached to each federal program, not just financial statement accuracy. A grant management system's audit trail depth directly determines how smoothly that audit proceeds.

### Do generic nonprofit CRM or operations platforms handle 2 CFR 200 compliance adequately?
Often not structurally. Many provide the data fields to record relevant information but lack the enforcement logic — configurable allowability rules, automated retention scheduling, time-and-effort workflows — that a Uniform Guidance-focused platform builds in by design. Verify enforcement, not just field availability, during evaluation.

### Can one platform handle both U.S. federal grant reporting and EU structural fund reporting?
Some can, but many grant management platforms are built primarily around one framework and treat the other as an afterthought or unsupported entirely. If your organization manages both, verify explicitly that each framework is supported as a first-class configuration rather than requiring manual work outside the system for one of them.

### What does an adequate audit trail need to preserve beyond final numbers?
It needs to preserve who entered or edited each transaction, when, under what approval, and tied to which specific budget line and grant requirement, with the ability to reconstruct a transaction's full history on demand. A system where budget categories can be edited without a preserved change history creates exactly the audit finding compliance officers are trying to avoid.

### What's different about sub-recipient monitoring compared to regular vendor or expense tracking?
Sub-recipient monitoring under Uniform Guidance requires its own compliance-specific data structure — risk assessments, monitoring visit or desk-review documentation, verification of the sub-recipient's own audit compliance where applicable — distinct from simply tracking them as a vendor or contact record. Platforms without this distinct workflow are underbuilt for organizations that pass through federal funds.

### (Scenario: auditor requests two-year-old transaction history and the current platform can't reconstruct it) How do we evaluate a replacement vendor's audit trail before we're caught in the same position again?
Require the vendor to demonstrate, in the demo itself, pulling the full edit history of a transaction from their own existing sample or demo data — not a description of the feature. If they can't produce it live in under a few minutes, treat that as a direct signal of how the feature will actually perform under real audit pressure, not just a demo limitation.

### (Scenario: compliance officer manages both a federal ERDF-adjacent program and a domestic Uniform Guidance award from the same office) Should we run two separate systems or insist on one platform covering both?
Insist on verifying both frameworks as first-class configurations in a single platform if at all possible — running two disconnected systems doubles the manual reconciliation work and creates exactly the kind of fragmented audit trail that produces findings. If no single vendor genuinely supports both well, prioritize the platform that handles your larger-dollar-volume program natively and treat the other as a documented manual process rather than pretending it's automated.

### (Scenario: vendor's demo environment looks clean because it was pre-configured by their sales engineer) How do we test allowability enforcement without being shown a rigged example?
Bring your own sample budget categories and a deliberately non-allowable expenditure type from your actual grant terms, and ask the vendor to configure the block live during the demo, not show you a pre-built one. A vendor's ability to configure — not just demonstrate — allowability rules in real time is the actual skill you're evaluating.

### (Scenario: sub-recipient network includes several small community organizations with limited technical capacity) What should we verify about how the platform handles sub-recipient-facing reporting?
Ask the vendor to show the actual interface a sub-recipient would use to submit expenditure reports and supporting documentation, not just the interface your compliance team sees. A monitoring workflow that's well-designed for your side but requires sub-recipients to email spreadsheets back to you defeats the purpose of centralized sub-recipient monitoring.
