Twenty-six weeks from RFP issue to signed contract, and when a procurement lead maps out where the time actually went, four weeks were spent waiting on legal redlines that sat in an inbox behind two other deals, six weeks were security review that could have run in parallel with commercial negotiation but didn't, and three weeks were a single VP's calendar availability for a fifteen-minute final approval. Of the twenty-six weeks, maybe eleven were genuinely necessary evaluation time — writing the RFP well, scoring responses rigorously, running real reference checks. The other fifteen were process debt: sequencing that didn't need to be sequential, approvals that didn't need to wait on a single person's calendar, and reviews that started late because nobody had a deadline attached to them. Understanding which category each delay falls into is the first step to actually shortening the timeline, rather than just accepting six months as the enterprise tax.

For a Procurement Lead accountable for both rigor and speed, the six-month RFP has become such a fixture of enterprise software buying that it's rarely interrogated — treated as an inherent cost of doing things properly rather than what it usually is: a set of avoidable sequencing and ownership gaps stacked on top of genuinely necessary evaluation work. This article breaks the timeline into its real components and identifies where compression is possible without cutting the diligence that protects the organization.

## Stage One: RFP Drafting — Where Ambiguity Gets Baked In

A poorly scoped RFP is the single biggest hidden cause of downstream delay, because ambiguous requirements produce vendor responses that don't map cleanly to each other, which then requires a clarification round, which then requires vendors to resubmit, adding two to four weeks that a tighter initial RFP would have avoided entirely. The fix isn't a longer drafting phase — it's a more disciplined one: a requirements workshop with every stakeholder track (technical, security, legal, business) contributing specific, testable requirements before drafting starts, rather than requirements getting added incrementally as each stakeholder reviews a draft in sequence.

Budget two to three weeks for RFP drafting with all stakeholder input gathered in parallel through structured requirements sessions, not a serial review-and-revise cycle where each function adds its section after seeing the last draft.

## Stage Two: Vendor Response Window — Longer Isn't Always More Rigorous

A standard 3-4 week vendor response window is reasonable for a complex enterprise RFP, but many organizations default to 6 weeks or more without a specific reason, simply because it feels appropriately serious for a large deal. Unless the RFP involves unusually complex technical requirements requiring vendors to build a working proof of concept, a well-scoped RFP with clear requirements doesn't need more than four weeks for qualified vendors to respond meaningfully — and a shorter, clearly bounded window also functions as an early signal of which vendors can actually mobilize responsively, which is itself useful evaluation data given that response speed correlates with delivery speed.

## Stage Three: Evaluation and Scoring — The Stage Worth Protecting

This is the stage that should not be compressed, because rigorous, structured scoring against a pre-agreed rubric is what makes the eventual decision defensible and what surfaces genuine differentiators between vendors rather than surface-level impressions from a slick demo. Budget genuine time here — typically 3-4 weeks for scoring, reference checks, and finalist demos — but protect it by making sure evaluators aren't simultaneously waiting on other stalled stages. A scoring committee that finishes technical evaluation in week three but can't finalize because legal hasn't started reviewing the finalists' contract paper yet is losing time not to evaluation rigor but to sequencing failure.

Reference checks specifically deserve real time and structure — not a courtesy call to a vendor-provided reference, but a structured conversation with 2-3 substantive questions about delivery reliability, change management, and what went wrong during the engagement and how the vendor handled it. A reference check that only asks "would you recommend them" produces almost no differentiating signal.

## Stage Four: Legal and Security Review — The Parallel Track That Rarely Runs in Parallel

This is where most of the avoidable six-month timeline actually lives. Legal contract review and security/InfoSec technical review are functionally independent — legal is assessing liability caps, IP terms, and data processing language; security is assessing architecture, certifications, and technical controls — and yet in most organizations these run sequentially, with security starting only after legal has cleared initial contract review, or vice versa, purely because the process was never explicitly designed to run them concurrently.

Running legal and security review in parallel from the point finalists are selected, with a shared target completion date and no dependency between the two tracks, is the single highest-leverage structural change most procurement organizations can make to their RFP timeline — routinely compressing six to eight weeks of sequential review into three to four weeks of parallel review with no reduction in the depth of either review.

## Stage Five: Final Approval — Where Individual Calendars Become the Bottleneck

The final sign-off stage is disproportionately where timelines stall on nothing more substantive than a senior approver's calendar availability. A framework that requires a specific individual's in-person or synchronous approval, rather than a defined asynchronous approval process with a hard deadline and a designated delegate, routinely adds one to three weeks waiting for someone's schedule to open up for what is often a fifteen-minute confirmation that the already-completed evaluation supports the recommendation.

Build a defined approval SLA into the framework — a maximum number of business days for final sign-off once the evaluation package is complete, with an explicit delegate authorized to approve in the primary approver's absence — and treat any breach of that SLA as a process failure worth escalating, not an unavoidable feature of enterprise decision-making. This connects directly to the broader [multi-stakeholder sign-off framework](https://www.manifera.com/blog/enterprise-software-vendor-selection-multi-stakeholder-sign-off-frameworks) an organization should already have in place — a framework with hard deadlines per stage is what prevents this exact bottleneck.

## Compressing the Timeline Without Cutting Diligence

The realistic target for a well-run enterprise RFP, from drafting through signature, is closer to twelve to sixteen weeks than twenty-six — achieved not by cutting any stage's actual rigor, but by running legal and security in parallel, holding firm response and approval windows, and front-loading requirements gathering so the RFP itself doesn't need a clarification round. None of this requires lowering the bar on evaluation depth; it requires removing the sequencing and ownership gaps that let genuinely necessary work stack up serially instead of running concurrently.

## Making the Final Call

A six-month RFP timeline is rarely six months of necessary diligence — it's usually eleven or twelve weeks of real evaluation work stretched across twenty-six weeks by sequential stages that could run in parallel and approval steps with no deadline attached. A Procurement Lead who maps their own organization's timeline against these five stages will almost always find the same pattern: rigorous evaluation, undermined by structural sequencing debt everywhere around it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's a realistic timeline for a well-run enterprise software RFP?
Twelve to sixteen weeks from drafting through signature is achievable without cutting diligence, versus the twenty-plus weeks many organizations default to. The compression comes from running legal and security review in parallel and holding firm windows at each stage, not from reducing evaluation rigor.

### Why do legal and security review usually run sequentially instead of in parallel?
Because most RFP processes were never explicitly designed to run them concurrently — legal reviews liability and IP terms while security reviews architecture and certifications, and these are functionally independent assessments that default to sequencing purely out of process inertia. Running them in parallel from finalist selection is typically the single biggest timeline compression available.

### How long should a vendor response window be for an enterprise RFP?
Three to four weeks is sufficient for a well-scoped RFP with clear requirements, unless the deal requires vendors to build a working proof of concept. Longer windows often just accommodate ambiguity in the RFP itself rather than genuinely needing more vendor response time.

### What causes the final approval stage to become a bottleneck?
Requiring a specific individual's synchronous or in-person sign-off rather than a defined asynchronous approval process with a hard deadline and designated delegate. This routinely adds one to three weeks waiting for a senior approver's calendar for what is often a brief confirmation of an already-completed evaluation.

### How can a Procurement Lead tell which RFP delays are necessary versus avoidable?
Necessary delay protects evaluation rigor — scoring, reference checks, and genuine technical or legal assessment. Avoidable delay is sequencing debt — stages waiting on each other with no real dependency, or approvals waiting on an individual's calendar rather than a defined SLA. Mapping the actual timeline against the five core RFP stages usually makes the split obvious.
