A dev shop offering to build your MVP for half its normal rate in exchange for 5% equity sounds, on the surface, like a smart way to stretch a thin pre-seed budget. It's also one of the most common cap table mistakes founders make before they've had a lawyer or an investor look at the deal — and it's a mistake that doesn't surface until months or years later, at the exact moment it's most expensive to fix: during a priced round, when new investors' counsel starts asking pointed questions about who's on the cap table and why.

## Why Vendors Offer Equity-for-Discount Deals

Vendors propose equity-for-services arrangements for a straightforward reason: it lets them win pre-seed and seed-stage clients who can't afford full cash rates, while betting on a portfolio-style return if even one or two of their equity clients eventually succeed. This isn't inherently predatory — some vendors genuinely operate this way as a business model and are transparent about it — but the arrangement's incentives are structurally different from a standard cash engagement, and founders frequently don't examine what a 3-10% equity stake actually costs them relative to the cash discount received. On a company that reaches even a modest exit or later-round valuation, 5% equity can be worth vastly more than the cash saved on the original engagement, and unlike a cash payment, that cost compounds with every future funding round instead of settling as a one-time expense.

## What the Equity Actually Costs You

Run the actual math before agreeing to any equity-for-services deal. A vendor taking 5% equity in exchange for a €30,000 discount on a build that would otherwise cost €60,000 is trading €30,000 in immediate savings for a stake that, if the company later raises a Series A at a €15 million post-money valuation, is worth €750,000 on paper before any further dilution — and that stake keeps diluting every subsequent investor's effective ownership, including yours, in every future round. The vendor's equity doesn't just cost you 5% once; it's baked into the cap table and gets diluted alongside everyone else at every subsequent raise, meaning the real founder-side cost is better thought of as a permanent tax on every future round, not a one-time trade.

## Cap Table Pollution and Investor Pushback

Institutional investors evaluating a seed or Series A deal review the cap table closely, and a vendor equity stake — especially an unusually large one, or one without standard vesting — routinely raises questions that slow down or complicate a round. Investors want to understand why a service provider holds equity, whether that equity came with any control rights or contractual entanglements, and whether the vendor relationship creates any ongoing dependency the new capital is meant to reduce. A vendor equity stake with no vesting schedule is a particular red flag, since it means the vendor holds full ownership regardless of whether the engagement was completed successfully or ended early — exactly the kind of unclean cap table entry that can require a costly cleanup negotiation before a round closes.

## The 409A and Valuation Complications

Equity issued to a vendor for services, rather than for cash, has to be accounted for correctly for tax and valuation purposes — issuing equity below fair market value without proper documentation can create tax liability issues for the recipient and complicate your company's 409A valuation (the independent appraisal used to price employee stock options fairly). Founders who handshake an equity-for-services deal without running it through proper legal and valuation processes routinely discover the paperwork gap during Series A due diligence, when investor counsel asks for documentation of every equity grant's basis and finds none for the vendor stake — a fixable problem, but one that costs legal fees and time during exactly the period when a founder has the least of either to spare.

## Safer Alternative Structures for Cash-Constrained Startups

If cash is genuinely the constraint, several structures address it without the permanent cap table cost of equity. A deferred payment structure — paying a portion of the vendor's fee 60-90 days after invoice, once initial runway pressure has eased or a bridge has closed — keeps 100% cash compensation without dilution. A milestone-discount structure, where the vendor accepts a modest cash discount (10-20%, not a full equity trade) in exchange for a longer, more predictable engagement commitment, aligns incentives without touching the cap table. And if equity genuinely makes sense — for a vendor providing sustained, deeply embedded technical leadership rather than a discrete project — structure it as a small, vested-over-time warrant or option grant with standard 4-year vesting and a 1-year cliff, exactly as you would for an employee, rather than an immediate, fully vested equity grant for a bounded project.

## What to Ask Before Signing Any Equity-for-Services Deal

Before agreeing to any equity component in a vendor contract, get an independent read on the actual cash-equivalent value of the equity being requested (not just the percentage), confirm the grant will be properly documented for 409A and tax purposes, insist on vesting tied to the engagement's actual deliverables rather than immediate full ownership, and — critically — get this reviewed by a startup lawyer before signing, not after. The cost of that legal review, typically a few hundred to low thousands of euros, is trivial next to the cost of an equity grant that complicates your next fundraise. ## Making the Contract Call

Equity-for-discount vendor deals feel like a clever way to stretch limited pre-seed or seed cash, but they routinely cost far more in diluted, compounding cap table value than the cash saved upfront, and they introduce due diligence friction at exactly the moments — fundraising rounds — when a clean cap table matters most. If cash is the real constraint, deferred payment terms or a modest cash discount for a longer commitment solve the problem without the permanent cost. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it ever a good idea to pay a software vendor in equity instead of cash?

Occasionally, for a vendor providing sustained, deeply embedded technical leadership over a long period — but even then, it should be structured as a small, vested-over-time grant with standard vesting, not an immediate full stake for a bounded project. For most discrete build engagements, deferred cash payment or a modest cash discount is the safer structure.

### How much does a 5% vendor equity stake actually cost a founder?

More than it appears. On a company that later raises at a €15 million post-money valuation, a 5% stake is worth €750,000 on paper — and unlike a one-time cash payment, it dilutes alongside every founder and investor at every subsequent funding round, making it a recurring cost rather than a one-time trade.

### Why do investors care about vendor equity on the cap table?

Investors review the cap table closely during due diligence and want to understand why a service provider holds equity, whether it came with any control rights, and whether it signals an ongoing dependency. An unusually large stake or one without standard vesting is a common source of due diligence friction that can slow down or complicate a round.

### What is a 409A valuation and why does vendor equity affect it?

A 409A valuation is the independent appraisal used to price employee stock options fairly for tax purposes. Equity issued to a vendor for services rather than cash needs to be properly documented and valued, and handshake deals without this documentation routinely surface as a gap during Series A due diligence.

### What's a safer alternative to equity for a cash-constrained startup?

Deferred payment terms — paying a portion of the fee 60-90 days after invoice — or a modest cash discount (10-20%) in exchange for a longer engagement commitment. Both address genuine cash constraints without the permanent, compounding cap table cost that equity introduces.
