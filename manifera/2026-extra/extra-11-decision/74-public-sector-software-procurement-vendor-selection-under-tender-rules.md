An open procedure tender for a mid-size software contract typically runs a minimum of 35 days from publication to submission deadline under EU procurement timelines — and that's before evaluation, the mandatory standstill period, and any challenge window. A restricted procedure can take longer once you add the pre-qualification stage. If you're a procurement lead who has only run commercial vendor selections before, the first thing that changes under public tender rules isn't the criteria — it's the calendar, and the fact that almost none of the calendar is compressible without legal risk.

The second thing that changes is that "we picked the best vendor" is not, on its own, a defensible outcome. It has to be the best vendor selected through the procedure that was legally required for the contract value, evaluated against criteria published before anyone saw a proposal, and documented well enough that a losing bidder's legal challenge fails. This article covers the procedural mechanics procurement leads actually need to get right, beyond just picking a strong vendor.

## Choosing the Right Procedure for the Contract Value and Complexity

Under the EU Public Procurement Directive (2014/24/EU), the procedure you're legally required to run depends on both the estimated contract value against published thresholds and the nature of what you're procuring. The open procedure — anyone can submit a proposal, no pre-qualification stage — is the default and generally fastest above-threshold route for straightforward requirements. The restricted procedure adds a pre-qualification stage that shortlists candidates before inviting full proposals, useful when you expect a high volume of unsuitable bidders and want to filter before investing evaluation time. The competitive procedure with negotiation, and competitive dialogue, are reserved for more complex procurements — typically where the technical solution can't be fully specified in advance, which describes a meaningful share of custom software projects — and they allow iterative refinement of proposals through structured dialogue rounds, at the cost of a longer, more resource-intensive process.

Getting this choice wrong has real consequences: running an open procedure for a requirement complex enough to have needed competitive dialogue often produces proposals that don't actually fit the need, forcing a restart. Running competitive dialogue for a requirement simple enough for an open procedure burns months you didn't need to spend.

## The ESPD: Self-Declaration Before Full Documentation

The European Single Procurement Document (ESPD) is a standardized self-declaration form bidders submit at the qualification stage, attesting to exclusion grounds (no convictions, no unpaid taxes, no professional misconduct) and selection criteria (financial standing, technical capacity) without requiring full supporting documentation upfront. Only the winning bidder — or bidders selected for the shortlist in a restricted procedure — typically needs to produce full certificates and evidence. This exists specifically to reduce the documentation burden on bidders who won't win, and a procurement lead should use it as designed: don't demand full supporting evidence from every bidder at submission, since it adds friction that discourages qualified vendors from bidding without changing the outcome.

## Setting Award Criteria: MEAT and the Weighting Decision You Can't Revisit

EU procurement law generally requires award on the basis of the "most economically advantageous tender" (MEAT), which explicitly permits — and in most modern procurement, expects — quality-weighted evaluation rather than lowest-price-wins. The weighting decision (a common structure is something like 60% quality/technical merit, 40% price, though the specific split is yours to set and should reflect what actually matters for the contract) has to be published in the tender documentation before proposals are evaluated, and it cannot be adjusted afterward to favor an outcome you've already decided you want. This is the single most consequential document you'll draft in the entire procurement, because a challenge to a public tender award most often targets exactly this: did the evaluation actually follow the published, weighted criteria, consistently, across every bidder.

Build in sub-criteria specificity too — "technical approach" as a single 40% line item is too vague to evaluate consistently; break it into named components (architecture approach, delivery methodology, team composition, prior comparable delivery) with individual weights, so your evaluators are scoring the same things the same way across every proposal.

## Framework Agreements and Dynamic Purchasing Systems

For organizations that procure software services repeatedly rather than once, a framework agreement — a pre-competed pool of qualified vendors you can call off from for individual work packages without running a full tender each time — reduces procurement overhead considerably. A Dynamic Purchasing System (DPS) is a related but distinct mechanism: unlike a closed framework, a DPS stays open to new qualified suppliers throughout its duration, which keeps competitive pressure alive over a multi-year term rather than locking in whichever vendors qualified at the start. If your organization anticipates recurring software development needs rather than a single project, evaluating whether a framework or DPS fits your procurement volume is worth doing before running yet another full open procedure for the next similar need.

## Abnormally Low Tenders and the Standstill Period

Two procedural mechanics trip up procurement leads more than any others. First: if a bid comes in suspiciously low relative to others, procurement law requires you to formally query the bidder on how they can deliver at that price before accepting or rejecting the tender — you can't simply take the lowest number at face value, nor can you disqualify it without giving the bidder a chance to explain. An unexplained abnormally low tender that gets awarded anyway is a common and often successful ground for challenge from a competing bidder.

Second: the standstill period (often called the Alcatel period, after the case that established it) is a mandatory pause — commonly around 10 calendar days — between notifying bidders of the award decision and actually signing the contract, during which unsuccessful bidders can request more detailed feedback on their score and, if warranted, lodge a formal challenge. Signing before the standstill period expires, or providing inadequate feedback that effectively denies bidders a real opportunity to assess whether to challenge, can render the contract void. Build the standstill period into your project timeline from day one — treating it as compressible is one of the more expensive assumptions a first-time public sector procurement lead can make.

## Making the Procurement Call

Public sector software procurement is not commercial vendor selection with extra paperwork — it's a different discipline with its own sequencing, its own irreversible decisions (published weighted criteria chief among them), and its own mandatory pauses that don't compress under schedule pressure. The procurement leads who run these well treat the procedural mechanics — procedure selection, ESPD, MEAT weighting, abnormally-low-tender queries, standstill — as fixed design constraints from the outset, not obstacles discovered midway through. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do we decide between the open procedure and the restricted procedure?
The open procedure is generally faster since it skips a separate pre-qualification stage, and works well for straightforward requirements where you don't expect an unmanageable volume of unsuitable bidders. The restricted procedure adds a shortlisting stage before full proposals, which is worth the extra time when you expect many bidders and want to filter for genuine capability before investing full evaluation effort.

### What happens if we receive a bid that looks abnormally low?
Procurement law requires you to formally query the bidder in writing on how they can deliver at that price before accepting or rejecting the tender — you cannot simply award it at face value or disqualify it without giving the bidder a chance to justify the price. Awarding an unexplained abnormally low tender is a common, often successful, ground for a losing bidder's challenge.

### Can we shorten the standstill period if our project timeline is tight?
No — the standstill period (commonly around 10 calendar days between award notification and contract signature) is a legal requirement, not a scheduling courtesy, and signing before it expires can render the contract void regardless of project urgency. Build it into your timeline from the start rather than treating it as compressible.

### Is a Dynamic Purchasing System different from a standard framework agreement?
Yes — a standard framework agreement is a closed pool of pre-qualified vendors fixed for its duration, while a Dynamic Purchasing System stays open to new qualified suppliers throughout its term, keeping competitive pressure alive over a multi-year period. It's worth considering specifically when you anticipate recurring procurement needs rather than a single project.

### What's the biggest documentation mistake in setting award criteria?
Publishing vague, single-line criteria like "technical approach: 40%" without breaking it into specific, individually weighted sub-criteria. This makes consistent scoring across bidders difficult to demonstrate and is a common target in procurement challenges, since evaluators without granular criteria are more easily accused of inconsistent or subjective scoring.
