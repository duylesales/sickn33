A non-technical founder with a validated idea and no technical co-founder faces a decision that gets framed far too narrowly, usually as "should I give up 15-25% equity to find a technical co-founder, or just hire a dev shop." Those aren't the only two options, and for a large share of founders, neither is the right first move. A fractional CTO — a senior technical leader engaged part-time, typically 10-20 hours a week, for a monthly retainer rather than equity or a full salary — sits between those two extremes and solves a different problem than either one does. Understanding what each option actually provides, and what it doesn't, is the decision that determines whether the next 12 months of technical execution go well.

## What Each Option Actually Provides

A development vendor builds software — you bring the product vision and scope, the vendor executes against it, and their incentive is typically tied to delivering the contracted work, not to the long-term technical strategy of the company. A technical co-founder brings equity-aligned, full-time ownership of technical decisions and typically writes code themselves in the early stages, but costs 15-30% of the company in founder equity and requires finding someone willing to commit years to your specific idea before it's proven anything. A fractional CTO provides technical leadership and decision-making — architecture choices, vendor evaluation, hiring decisions, technical risk assessment — without writing production code themselves in most engagements, at a cost of roughly €4,000-€10,000 per month depending on seniority and hours committed, with no equity dilution in the typical arrangement.

## The Gap a Fractional CTO Actually Fills

The specific gap a fractional CTO fills is judgment, not labor: a non-technical founder evaluating a development vendor's quote has no way to independently assess whether the proposed architecture makes sense, whether the timeline is realistic, or whether the vendor's technical claims hold up. A fractional CTO reviews vendor proposals, sits in on technical discussions the founder can't fully evaluate alone, and makes build-versus-buy and vendor-selection calls with actual technical grounding. This is distinct from — and usually a prerequisite for — a good dev vendor relationship, because a founder without any technical oversight is negotiating and managing a vendor contract blind, which is exactly the condition that leads to scope creep, quality issues, and IP problems going unnoticed until they're expensive to fix.

## Cost Comparison Across a 12-Month Horizon

Run the numbers over a realistic first year. A technical co-founder taking 20% equity on a company that later raises a seed round at a €6 million post-money valuation has taken roughly €1.2 million in value for that year of work, deferred but real. A fractional CTO at €6,000/month for 20 hours a week costs €72,000 over the same year, with zero dilution, and can be scaled down or ended without a co-founder breakup dynamic if the fit isn't right. A dev vendor without any fractional technical oversight might cost less directly — say €50,000-€100,000 for an MVP build — but carries the highest execution risk, since there's no one on the founder's side of the table evaluating whether that spend is being used well. Most founders underestimate how much a fractional CTO's oversight can reduce vendor-side waste, often paying for itself through better-scoped, better-vetted vendor engagements alone.

## When a Fractional CTO Plus Vendor Is the Right Combination

The strongest configuration for many non-technical founders, especially at pre-seed and seed stage, combines both: a fractional CTO providing technical strategy, vendor oversight, and architecture decisions, paired with a [dedicated development team](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) or vendor doing the actual implementation. This gives the founder technical judgment they don't have to hire full-time for, and execution capacity without the fixed cost and hiring risk of a full internal engineering team. It works especially well when the fractional CTO is involved in vendor selection from the start — sitting in on vendor interviews, reviewing proposed contracts and architecture, and setting up the reporting cadence that keeps the vendor accountable — rather than being brought in after a vendor relationship is already underway and already troubled.

## When a Full Technical Co-Founder Is Actually Worth It

A full-time, equity-aligned technical co-founder is the right call when the product's core value proposition is a genuinely hard technical problem that needs sustained, deep ownership — not a standard CRUD application or marketplace, but something where the technical execution itself is the differentiator (novel ML infrastructure, a hard real-time systems problem, deep domain-specific technical complexity). It's also right when the founder genuinely wants a technical partner for company-building decisions beyond just engineering — hiring, culture, product strategy — not just technical execution. If neither of those applies, a fractional CTO plus a strong vendor typically gets equivalent technical outcomes at a fraction of the long-term cost.

## Evaluating and Structuring a Fractional CTO Engagement

Vet a fractional CTO candidate the way you'd vet any senior hire: ask for references specifically from founders they've advised through a vendor selection and an MVP build, not just general engineering leadership experience. Structure the engagement with a clear scope of hours and deliverables — architecture review, vendor management, hiring support — rather than an open-ended "advisory" retainer with no concrete accountability. And set an explicit review point at three months to assess fit, since a fractional CTO relationship that isn't working is far easier and cheaper to end than an equity-based co-founder relationship that isn't.

## Making the Call

For most non-technical founders without a validated, deeply technical core product, the choice isn't fractional CTO versus dev vendor — it's fractional CTO plus vendor versus vendor alone, with the fractional CTO providing the technical judgment that makes the vendor relationship actually work well. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a fractional CTO actually do if they don't write code?

A fractional CTO provides technical leadership and judgment: reviewing and negotiating vendor proposals, making architecture and build-versus-buy decisions, assessing technical risk, and supporting technical hiring — the oversight a non-technical founder can't provide for themselves when evaluating a development vendor.

### How much does a fractional CTO typically cost compared to a technical co-founder?

A fractional CTO typically runs €4,000-€10,000 per month for 10-20 hours a week with no equity dilution. A technical co-founder usually takes 15-30% equity, which on a company that later raises at a multi-million-euro valuation can represent far more total value than a year or two of fractional CTO fees — though it also brings full-time, equity-aligned commitment a fractional arrangement doesn't.

### Can a fractional CTO and a development vendor work together?

Yes, and it's often the strongest configuration for non-technical founders — the fractional CTO handles technical strategy, vendor oversight, and architecture decisions, while the vendor executes the actual build. This works best when the fractional CTO is involved from vendor selection onward, not brought in after problems surface.

### When is a full technical co-founder worth the equity cost?

When the product's core differentiation is a genuinely hard, sustained technical problem — not a standard application build — or when the founder wants a full company-building partner beyond engineering execution. For most standard product builds, a fractional CTO plus a strong vendor achieves comparable technical outcomes without the equity cost.

### How should a founder structure a fractional CTO engagement?

With a clear scope of hours and deliverables — architecture review, vendor management, hiring support — rather than an open-ended advisory retainer, references from founders they've guided through a vendor selection specifically, and an explicit three-month review point to assess fit before committing longer term.
