Six weeks after closing a €320,000 pre-seed round on a SAFE, one founder we spoke with had already burned through 40% of her runway — not on salaries or ads, but on a freelance developer she found through a job board who delivered a login screen and a half-built dashboard before going silent. No company entity, no written IP assignment, no source code handed over on the platform he'd been paid to build on. She had to start the build over with five months of cash left instead of eight. This is the single most common way pre-seed startups die before they ever get a chance to fail on the merits of their idea: not a bad product decision, but a bad vendor decision made under time pressure with no technical co-founder to catch it.

At pre-seed, the stakes of a vendor decision are different in kind from every later stage. There's no VP of Engineering to audit the code, no board pushing back on scope, and often no technical co-founder at all — just a founder, a limited budget, and a clock that starts ticking the day the round closes. Getting this one decision wrong doesn't just cost money; it costs the runway needed to reach the traction that makes the next round possible at all.

## The Real Constraint Is Runway, Not Price

Founders shopping for a pre-seed dev vendor almost always optimize for the wrong variable first: the lowest hourly rate. A €18/hour offshore freelancer looks cheaper on paper than a €55/hour boutique agency team, but the comparison only holds if the cheaper option actually ships a working product. Typical pre-seed rounds in Europe now run €250,000 to €1,000,000, with 18-36 months of intended runway once salaries, tooling, and legal costs are subtracted — and most founders allocate somewhere between 15% and 30% of that round to the initial build. On a €400,000 round, that's realistically €60,000-€120,000 for an MVP, which needs to survive scrutiny at the next fundraise, not just look finished in a demo video. The right question is never "what's the hourly rate" — it's "what's the cost per week of runway if this vendor takes twice as long as promised or has to be replaced entirely." A vendor that's 40% more expensive but delivers in 10 weeks instead of 20 is nearly always the cheaper choice once burn rate is priced in.

## Fixed-Price vs. Time-and-Materials at Pre-Seed

Pre-seed founders are drawn to fixed-price quotes because they feel like budget certainty, but a fixed price only protects you if the scope is genuinely fixed — and pre-seed products almost never have a genuinely fixed scope, because you're still learning what the product needs to be. A fixed-price contract with an underspecified scope becomes a negotiation over every change request, with the vendor incentivized to interpret ambiguity in their favor and charge for "out of scope" work that any reasonable person would consider core to the MVP. Time-and-materials with a hard budget cap and weekly delivery checkpoints is usually the better structure at this stage: it keeps the vendor's incentives aligned with shipping working software quickly rather than defending a spec, and it gives you a natural exit point — after week three, if velocity or communication quality is poor, you've lost three weeks, not a locked-in €40,000 fixed engagement. Milestone-based fixed price works better once you've validated the spec with a smaller vendor engagement first, not as the very first contract you sign.

## The IP Assignment Clause That Actually Matters

Every pre-seed vendor contract needs an explicit, unambiguous work-for-hire or IP assignment clause stating that all code, designs, and related IP transfer to your company on payment — not on project completion, not at the vendor's discretion, but on payment for the specific deliverable. Without this clause in writing, you are legally exposed even if the vendor never intends to cause trouble: ambiguous IP ownership is exactly the kind of finding that stalls a Series A term sheet, because investors' counsel will flag it during technical and legal due diligence. This matters more, not less, when the vendor is a solo freelancer rather than a registered company, because there's no corporate entity standing behind the work if a dispute arises later. Get the clause reviewed by a lawyer familiar with startup contracts before signing — the cost of an hour of legal review is trivial next to the cost of an unclear chain of IP title discovered during due diligence eighteen months from now.

## Red Flags in a Pre-Seed Vendor Pitch

A few patterns show up disproportionately in vendor relationships that go wrong at this stage. A vendor who quotes a full MVP price without asking a single clarifying question about your users, your core workflow, or what "done" looks like is pattern-matching your project to a template, not scoping your actual product. A vendor who wants 100% payment upfront, rather than staged payments tied to delivered, working increments, is asking you to absorb all the delivery risk yourself. A vendor who can't show you a comparable previous project's actual running product — not just a portfolio screenshot — hasn't necessarily done the work they're claiming. And a vendor operating as an unregistered individual with no company entity, no standard contract template, and payment only via personal bank transfer or crypto is a legal and continuity risk regardless of how talented the individual developer might be personally.

## What a Pre-Seed-Appropriate Engagement Actually Looks Like

The right engagement size at pre-seed is small enough to de-risk cheaply and structured enough to protect your IP and your timeline. That typically means: a written scope document covering the 3-5 core user flows that constitute your actual MVP (not every feature on your roadmap), weekly demos of working software rather than status reports, a contract with an explicit IP assignment and a termination clause you can exercise on short notice, and code that lives in a repository you own from day one — not the vendor's private repo that gets "transferred" at the end. ## Evaluating Technical Credibility Without a Technical Co-Founder

Most pre-seed founders evaluating a vendor aren't equipped to review code quality directly, and that's fine — it's not the skill you need to have built yet. What you can do is verify proxies: ask for references from founders at a similar stage who can speak to whether the vendor hit dates and communicated proactively about problems, not just whether the final product worked. Ask specifically how the vendor handles scope disagreements — a good answer describes a change-request process; a bad answer insists disagreements "haven't come up." And if you can afford even a few hours of a fractional technical advisor's time before signing, use it for a single purpose: having someone technical review the proposed contract's scope, IP clause, and architecture approach before you commit €60,000 and two months of runway to it.

## Making the Vendor Call

The pre-seed vendor decision isn't really about finding the cheapest builder — it's about protecting the two things you can't get back once they're spent: runway and time. A vendor that costs 20% more but ships a working, well-documented MVP in ten weeks with clean IP transfer is worth more than a bargain vendor who might take twice as long and leave your cap table clean but your codebase a liability. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much of a pre-seed round should go toward the initial software build?

Most pre-seed founders allocate 15-30% of the round to the MVP build, which on a typical €250,000-€1,000,000 European pre-seed round works out to roughly €40,000-€150,000, depending on scope and the vendor's rate structure. The right figure depends on how much of the remaining round needs to fund runway to the next milestone rather than the build itself.

### Should a pre-seed startup use a freelancer, a boutique agency, or a dedicated offshore team?

It depends on scope stability and risk tolerance. A solo freelancer is cheapest but carries the highest continuity and IP risk if they become unavailable mid-project. A boutique agency or dedicated team costs more but provides contractual continuity, a company entity to hold accountable, and typically a documented handoff process — worth the premium for most first-time non-technical founders.

### What IP clause should be in every pre-seed vendor contract?

An explicit clause stating all code, designs, and related work product transfer to your company upon payment for the specific deliverable, not upon project completion or at the vendor's discretion. This should be reviewed by a lawyer before signing, and it matters even more with solo freelancers than with registered companies, since there's no corporate entity to hold accountable later.

### What's the biggest red flag in a pre-seed vendor pitch?

A quote for a full MVP price with no clarifying questions about your actual users or workflows — it signals a templated approach rather than real scoping. Close behind that: demanding full payment upfront rather than staged, milestone-tied payments, which shifts all delivery risk onto the founder.

### How do I evaluate vendor technical credibility without a technical co-founder?

Ask for references from founders at a comparable stage who can speak to whether the vendor hit dates and communicated proactively about problems. Ask how the vendor handles scope disagreements — a concrete process is a good sign, a claim that disagreements "haven't come up" is not. If possible, get a few hours of a fractional technical advisor's time to review the contract's scope and IP terms before signing.
