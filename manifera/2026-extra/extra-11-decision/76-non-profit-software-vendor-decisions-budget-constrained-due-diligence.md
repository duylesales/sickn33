A funder that caps overhead and administrative costs at 15% of a grant doesn't care that your organization needs custom case management software to actually deliver the program the grant funds — from the funder's accounting perspective, software is often bucketed as overhead unless you've structured the grant budget to classify it as a direct program cost. This single classification decision, made months before you ever talk to a vendor, can determine whether a $40,000 platform build is fundable at all. Nonprofit software vendor selection isn't commercial vendor selection with a smaller number attached — the budget constraint changes which questions matter, in what order.

## TechSoup, Nonprofit Tiers, and What the Discount Doesn't Cover

Most nonprofit founders' first instinct, correctly, is to check whether a vendor offers a nonprofit discount or donation program — TechSoup-distributed licenses, Google Ad Grants, Microsoft's nonprofit tier, AWS and Google Cloud nonprofit credits. These are genuinely valuable and worth pursuing systematically before evaluating any paid vendor. But they cover software licensing and cloud infrastructure costs, not the custom development, integration, and ongoing maintenance work most organizations actually need once off-the-shelf tools stop fitting their program model. A nonprofit that's secured free Salesforce Nonprofit Cloud licenses through TechSoup still needs someone to configure it, integrate it with a donor database, and maintain it — and that implementation cost is frequently larger than the licensing cost the discount program covered, which is a gap founders routinely underestimate when budgeting a "free" platform.

## Total Cost of Ownership, Not Sticker Price

The vendor quote you get is rarely the number that matters most. A cheaper build with weak documentation, no handoff plan, and a codebase only one contractor understands costs more over three years than a slightly pricier build designed for a small internal team or a different, more sustainable vendor to maintain later. The specific questions that surface real TCO: what happens when the original developer becomes unavailable — is there documentation and a clean codebase a different vendor could pick up, or is the software effectively hostage to one relationship; what's the actual hosting and maintenance cost once the build phase ends, since many nonprofits budget the build and forget the run; and does the vendor's proposed technology stack use common, well-supported tools your organization could realistically find a second vendor for, versus an obscure or proprietary stack that locks you into the original vendor indefinitely.

Ask every vendor directly: if we needed to switch to a different team in two years, how hard would that be, and what would we need from you to make it possible. A vendor confident in the quality and portability of their own work answers this directly; a vendor who gets evasive is telling you something about how replaceable they intend to be.

## Restricted vs. Unrestricted Funding and What It Means for Vendor Structure

Grant funding frequently comes restricted to a specific program or purpose, which has direct implications for how you can structure a vendor engagement. A platform that serves multiple programs but was purchased using funds restricted to one program creates an allocation problem your auditor will eventually ask about — you generally need to allocate the cost across programs proportionally to actual use, documented at the time of purchase, not retroactively justified. This is worth resolving before signing a vendor contract, not after: structure the engagement (and the invoice, and the internal cost allocation memo) to match how the funding is actually restricted, so the expense is defensible when a funder or auditor reviews it.

Unrestricted funds and reserves give you more structural flexibility to build something that serves the whole organization rather than being scoped narrowly to satisfy one grant's restrictions — if you have discretionary funds available, it's often worth using them specifically for the shared infrastructure pieces (a unified database, core integrations) that don't map cleanly to one program, and reserving restricted program funds for the program-specific features that clearly do.

## Scoping for Sustainability, Not Just Launch

The most common budget failure in nonprofit software projects isn't overspending on the initial build — it's underbudgeting for what happens after launch. A platform that requires a developer on retainer to keep running, with no line item for that in next year's budget, is a platform that degrades or breaks within a year or two, quietly, until a funder-facing report reveals data that hasn't updated in months. Push any vendor proposal to include an honest estimate of ongoing cost — hosting, security patching, minor feature maintenance — as a distinct line from the build cost, and treat a vendor who can't or won't estimate this as a vendor who hasn't thought past delivery day.

A related structural option worth exploring directly with a vendor: phased, module-based builds that let you launch a minimum viable version within budget now and add functionality as future grants or unrestricted reserves allow, rather than either overbuilding beyond current budget or underbuilding something that can't scale with the organization later. A vendor experienced with nonprofit budget cycles will propose this kind of phasing without you having to ask; one who only offers an all-or-nothing full-scope proposal may not be used to working within grant-cycle constraints.

## Red Flags Specific to Budget-Constrained Buyers

A few patterns are worth treating with particular skepticism in a nonprofit context: a vendor who won't discuss phased scope or module-based pricing at all; a vendor whose maintenance/support pricing escalates sharply after an initial discounted first year (common enough to be worth asking about explicitly upfront); and a vendor who proposes a fully custom, proprietary architecture for a need that off-the-shelf or lightly customized open-source tools could serve at a fraction of the cost — nonprofit budgets rarely justify a fully bespoke build when a well-configured existing platform would meet 80% of the need for 20% of the cost. Custom development earns its cost when the program's actual workflow genuinely doesn't fit any existing tool, not as a default starting point.

## Making the Budget-Constrained Call

The founders who get this right treat the budget constraint as a scoping discipline rather than a reason to accept a lower-quality vendor relationship — they push every vendor on total cost of ownership, structure engagements to match how funding is actually restricted, and insist on phased, sustainable scope over an impressive but unaffordable full build. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do nonprofit software discount programs like TechSoup cover custom development costs?
No — these programs typically cover licensing and cloud infrastructure costs, not the custom development, integration, or ongoing maintenance work most organizations need once off-the-shelf tools stop fitting their program. Budget for implementation and maintenance separately, since this cost is frequently larger than the discounted licensing cost itself.

### Can we use grant funds restricted to one program to pay for software that serves multiple programs?
Generally you need to allocate the cost proportionally across programs based on actual use, documented at the time of purchase rather than justified retroactively. Resolve this allocation approach before signing a vendor contract so the expense is defensible if a funder or auditor reviews it later.

### How do we know if a vendor's technology choices will lock us in?
Ask directly whether the proposed stack uses common, well-supported tools a different vendor could realistically pick up, and ask what documentation and handoff support you'd get if you needed to switch teams in two years. A vendor confident in the portability of their own work will answer specifically; evasiveness on this question is itself informative.

### Should a nonprofit always choose the cheapest vendor quote?
No — the cheapest build quote often excludes ongoing maintenance, documentation, and portability, which can make it more expensive over two or three years than a slightly higher quote structured for sustainability. Compare total cost of ownership, not just the initial build number.

### What's a reasonable way to phase a software build within a limited nonprofit budget?
Scope a minimum viable version that covers the highest-priority workflow within current budget, then add functionality in later phases as future grants or unrestricted reserves allow. A vendor experienced with nonprofit budget cycles should propose this kind of phasing without being asked, rather than presenting only a full-scope, all-or-nothing proposal.
