A 45-person distribution company still runs its order fulfillment through a shared Excel spreadsheet, a printer, and a fax machine that one employee insists is faster than the alternatives. The owner knows this is unsustainable — a warehouse error last quarter shipped the wrong pallet to a client twice, costing a contract — but every quote from a software vendor arrives full of terms like "API-first architecture" and "microservices," and there is nobody on staff who can tell whether that is the right approach or an expensive way to solve a problem that needed a simpler fix. This is the position most SME owners are actually in when they start a digital transformation: convinced they need to change something, with no internal technical function to translate vendor pitches into decisions they can trust.

Roughly nine in ten SMEs operate without a dedicated IT department, according to patterns consistently observed across European small-business surveys — technology decisions typically fall to the owner, an operations manager, or whoever happens to be least intimidated by computers. That structural reality changes what "vendor due diligence" has to mean. A framework built for a CTO evaluating architecture diagrams is useless to an owner who needs to know, in plain terms, whether a vendor will actually deliver something that runs their business better and doesn't leave them stranded a year later.

## The Vendor Becomes Your IT Department — Say So in Writing

The single most important reframe for an SME without internal IT is this: the vendor is not just building software, they are functionally becoming your IT department for as long as the relationship lasts. That means the scope of what you are buying needs to explicitly include the things an internal IT team would normally handle — user support when something breaks, security patching, backups, uptime monitoring — not just the initial build. SME owners frequently discover, months after a project "finishes," that none of this was actually included, because the SOW was scoped like a one-time construction project rather than an ongoing operational dependency.

Before signing anything, get explicit written answers to three questions: who do we call when something breaks at 6pm on a Friday, what is the guaranteed response time, and what exactly is covered under the retainer or support contract versus billed separately as a new project. A vendor unwilling to put these answers in writing is telling you, indirectly, that the relationship ends the moment the invoice is paid — which is precisely the opposite of what an SME without internal IT needs.

## Managed Services vs. Project-Based: Picking the Right Structure

SME digital transformation vendors typically offer two structures, and picking the wrong one is a common, expensive mistake. Project-based engagements deliver a defined piece of work — a new inventory system, a customer portal — and then the relationship ends or shifts to a much lighter support tier. Managed services engagements keep the vendor responsible for the ongoing health of the system: monitoring, updates, minor feature requests, and support, usually for a predictable monthly fee.

An SME with a single, well-defined problem (replace the spreadsheet-based order system with proper software) and enough in-house capability to handle day-to-day questions is often well served by project-based work with a defined support-and-warranty period afterward. An SME undertaking a broader transformation — multiple interconnected systems, ongoing new feature needs, no internal capacity to even file a clear bug report — is usually better served by a managed services structure, even though the monthly cost feels higher on paper, because the alternative is discovering a critical failure with nobody contractually obligated to respond quickly. ## Running Technical Due Diligence Without a Technical Background

You do not need to understand code to evaluate a vendor competently — you need a small number of proxy signals that correlate strongly with vendor quality even to a non-technical evaluator. Ask for two or three reference clients of a similar size and industry, and actually call them — not just read a testimonial on the vendor's website, but ask the reference directly what went wrong during the project and how the vendor handled it. Every real vendor relationship has friction somewhere; a reference who claims there were zero problems is either being polite or was not asked a specific enough question.

Ask to see a live demo of a system the vendor built for another client in a comparable situation, not a slide deck. Ask what happens to your data and your source code if you ever want to leave — a vendor who gets uncomfortable at this question is signaling a lock-in strategy, not a partnership. And bring in a one-time paid technical advisor — an independent consultant, not affiliated with any vendor you're evaluating — for a few hours to review the top two or three proposals before you sign; the cost is trivial compared to the cost of a wrong multi-year commitment, and this specific verification approach is covered in more depth in our companion piece on the [trust verification framework for non-technical founders](https://www.manifera.com/blog/choosing-a-vendor-as-a-non-technical-founder-the-trust-verification-framework).

## Documentation and Handover: What "Done" Should Actually Include

Because there is no internal IT function to absorb institutional knowledge as a project progresses, documentation is not optional polish for an SME engagement — it is the mechanism by which the business avoids permanent dependency on this one vendor. Insist that the contract specifies deliverables beyond the working software: a plain-language system overview (what does this actually do, in terms a new employee could understand), an admin guide for whoever will operate the system day to day, and a list of every third-party account, API key, and credential associated with the project, registered under the business's own accounts, not the vendor's.

This documentation requirement should be treated the same way an SME treats a fire inspection or a lease agreement — a basic operational safeguard, not an optional nice-to-have you cut to save budget. ## Red Flags Specific to No-IT-Department Buyers

Vendors sometimes specifically target SMEs without technical staff because they correctly calculate that scope creep, vague deliverables, and unnecessary complexity are harder to catch without an internal technical evaluator. Watch for proposals padded with technology choices that serve the vendor's convenience more than your business need — an unnecessarily complex architecture for a problem that a simpler, off-the-shelf tool could solve at a fraction of the cost. Watch for pricing that bundles everything into one large upfront number with no milestone structure, which removes your ability to stop or redirect the engagement if early work misses the mark. And watch for a sales process that never once asks detailed questions about your actual day-to-day operations — a vendor who proposes a solution without understanding your specific workflow is guessing, and an SME cannot afford to be the training ground for that guess.

## Making the Transformation Call

Digital transformation without an internal IT department is not a disadvantage you have to apologize for — it is simply a constraint that should shape how you structure the vendor relationship: clear ongoing support obligations in writing, documentation as a required deliverable, reference calls that ask specific questions, and, where the budget allows, an independent technical advisor to sanity-check the biggest decisions. Get the structure right, and the absence of internal IT stops being a liability the moment you have a vendor who is genuinely accountable for filling that gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I evaluate a software vendor if I don't understand the technical details?
Focus on proxy signals instead of technical claims: call reference clients and ask what went wrong, request a live demo rather than a slide deck, ask what happens to your data if you leave, and consider paying an independent technical advisor for a few hours to review the top proposals before you commit.

### Should an SME choose managed services or a one-time project engagement?
A well-defined, single-system project with enough internal capacity to handle day-to-day questions can work with project-based pricing and a defined support period afterward. A broader transformation with multiple systems and no internal technical capacity is usually better served by managed services, where the vendor stays contractually responsible for ongoing health and support.

### What should be included in a vendor contract if we have no internal IT department?
The contract should explicitly cover post-launch support response times, who to contact when something breaks, what's included under the ongoing fee versus billed as new work, and documentation deliverables including an admin guide and a full list of accounts and credentials registered under the business's own name.

### How much should an SME budget for an independent technical advisor during vendor selection?
A few hours of an independent consultant's time to review the top two or three vendor proposals typically costs a small fraction of one month's software spend, and it is inexpensive relative to the risk of committing to a multi-year vendor relationship built on unverifiable claims.

### What is the biggest mistake SMEs make when choosing a digital transformation vendor?
Treating the engagement as a one-time construction project rather than an ongoing operational dependency. Without an internal IT department to catch the gap, SMEs frequently discover months later that critical needs like support, patching, and backups were never actually included in the original scope.
