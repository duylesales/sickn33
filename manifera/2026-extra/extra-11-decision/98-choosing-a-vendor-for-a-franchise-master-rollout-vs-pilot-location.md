A franchisor signs a vendor contract for a new ordering and loyalty system, pilots it at three flagship locations, calls the pilot a success, and commits to a full 60-location rollout — only to discover, four locations into the full rollout, that the vendor's onboarding process, which worked fine with hands-on attention for three pilot sites, cannot scale to onboard a new location every week without weeks of delay stacking up. The pilot proved the software worked. It never tested whether the vendor's operational capacity could actually deliver at franchise scale. This gap — testing the product without testing the delivery mechanism — is the single most common failure in franchise technology rollouts, and it is entirely preventable with the right contract structure from the start.

A pilot and a full rollout are not the same purchase at different volumes. They test different things, and a vendor decision that treats them as one continuous commitment — sign once, scale automatically — removes the franchisor's ability to catch a vendor whose product is good but whose delivery capacity is not, before that gap becomes expensive at 60-location scale.

## What a Pilot Is Actually Supposed to Prove

A pilot location should be explicitly scoped to answer specific questions, defined before the pilot begins, not evaluated loosely afterward on a general "did it go well" basis. Does the system handle your actual transaction volume and complexity without performance issues? Does it integrate cleanly with your existing point-of-sale, payment processing, and reporting infrastructure? Do franchisee staff at the pilot location actually adopt it, or do workarounds start appearing within the first month? And critically — does the vendor's support and onboarding process, even at small scale, meet the response-time and quality bar you will need replicated dozens of times over?

Define success criteria for each of these questions in writing before the pilot starts, with specific, measurable thresholds — not "the system should work well" but "order processing time should not increase by more than 10% compared to the legacy system" or "support tickets should be resolved within four business hours." A pilot without predefined success criteria tends to get evaluated retrospectively through whatever lens makes the decision-maker feel good about a commitment they have already started making politically, which defeats the entire purpose of piloting in the first place.

## Choosing the Right Pilot Location Deliberately

The instinct to pilot at your best-performing, most cooperative, most tech-forward location is understandable and almost always a mistake. A flagship location with an engaged manager and modern infrastructure will make nearly any reasonably competent vendor look successful, telling you very little about how the system performs under more typical, or more difficult, real-world conditions. A better pilot location is representative of the network's actual diversity — median transaction volume, a manager with average (not exceptional) tech comfort, and infrastructure conditions similar to most of your locations, not your newest flagship.

Where possible, run the pilot at two locations simultaneously rather than one — a straightforward site and a genuinely harder one (older infrastructure, higher transaction complexity, a historically change-resistant manager). A vendor who succeeds at both gives you real confidence about scaling; a vendor who succeeds only at the easy site is telling you something important about where the rollout will hit friction later.

## Structuring the Vendor Contract to Separate Pilot From Rollout

The contract structure matters as much as the pilot design itself. Avoid signing a single contract that commits to full network rollout contingent only on vague pilot "success" — this removes your negotiating leverage and your ability to walk away cleanly if the pilot reveals real but fixable problems. Instead, structure the engagement as two distinct phases with a genuine decision gate between them: a pilot contract with defined scope, cost, and success criteria, followed by a separate rollout contract negotiated after the pilot results are in, informed by what the pilot actually revealed rather than what was assumed beforehand.

This structure gives the franchisor two forms of leverage. First, pricing leverage — rollout-phase pricing negotiated with real pilot performance data in hand is a fundamentally different negotiation than pricing agreed to before either party has evidence of what the engagement actually requires. Second, scope leverage — issues surfaced during the pilot (an integration that needs rework, an onboarding process that needs to be redesigned for scale) can be built into the rollout contract's requirements explicitly, rather than discovered as expensive surprises mid-rollout. Contract structures with a genuine decision gate, similar to the [outcome-based versus project-based structuring](https://www.manifera.com/services/custom-software-development/) approach used in other complex engagements, protect the franchisor from being contractually committed before the real risk factors are known.

## Testing Vendor Delivery Capacity, Not Just Product Quality

The failure described in this article's opening — a vendor whose product works but whose onboarding process cannot scale — is specifically a delivery capacity failure, and it needs to be tested deliberately during the pilot phase, not assumed. Ask the vendor directly, and get it in writing: what does your onboarding process look like at 5 locations per month versus 1, do you have dedicated implementation staff beyond whoever ran the pilot, and can you provide a reference from a franchise network that scaled with you from a comparable pilot size to a comparable full rollout size.

A vendor with genuine franchise-scale experience will have a documented, repeatable onboarding playbook they can show you — not just describe verbally — because they have had to build one to survive their own past rollouts. A vendor without one, however good their product demo looked, is likely to hit the same capacity wall this article opened with, and the pilot is your last, cheapest opportunity to catch that gap before it costs you weeks of delay across dozens of locations.

## Sequencing the Full Rollout After a Successful Pilot

Once the pilot validates both the product and the vendor's delivery capacity, sequence the full rollout in waves rather than attempting a single simultaneous cutover across the entire network — a rollout structure covered in more depth in our piece on [franchise software vendor selection across multiple locations](https://www.manifera.com/blog/franchise-software-vendor-selection-one-system-across-multiple-locations). Each wave should include a mix of location types (urban and rural, high and low volume, tech-comfortable and resistant managers) so lessons from wave one genuinely inform wave two, rather than each wave being a homogeneous group that hides problems until they appear at scale.

## Making the Rollout Call

A pilot is only useful if it is designed to fail informatively — structured with clear success criteria, a deliberately representative (not flattering) location, and an explicit test of the vendor's delivery capacity, not just their product. Pair that with a contract structure that keeps the full rollout as a genuinely separate decision, negotiated with real evidence in hand, and a franchisor avoids the expensive trap of discovering at location 15 that the vendor who impressed everyone at location 1 cannot actually deliver at scale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many locations should a franchise pilot include?
Two is often better than one — a representative, typical location and a deliberately harder one (older infrastructure, higher complexity, or a change-resistant manager). Piloting only at your best, most cooperative location tells you little about how the system will perform across the full diversity of your network.

### Should the pilot and full rollout be in the same contract?
No. Structure them as two distinct phases with a genuine decision gate between them — a pilot contract with defined success criteria, followed by a separately negotiated rollout contract informed by actual pilot results. A single combined contract removes your leverage to walk away or renegotiate scope based on what the pilot reveals.

### What's the most common reason a successful pilot fails to scale into a full rollout?
The vendor's delivery and onboarding capacity, not the product itself. A vendor can provide excellent hands-on support for a small pilot and still lack the repeatable, staffed onboarding process needed to bring dozens of locations online on schedule.

### What success criteria should we define before starting a franchise pilot?
Specific, measurable thresholds set in writing before the pilot begins — for example, order processing time not increasing by more than a defined percentage, support tickets resolved within a defined response window, and staff adoption without workarounds appearing within the first month.

### How should a full franchise rollout be sequenced after a successful pilot?
In waves rather than a single simultaneous cutover, with each wave including a representative mix of location types so lessons from earlier waves genuinely inform later ones instead of problems only surfacing once the rollout has already reached full scale.
