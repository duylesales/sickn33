A founder pitching investors with "we can build this in six weeks for €25,000" is usually pitching a number that came from a competitor's marketing page, not from an actual scoped estimate. Seed-stage founders consistently underbudget the software build by 40-60% against what it actually costs to reach a product credible enough to drive the retention and usage metrics a Series A requires — not because vendors are dishonest, but because founders anchor on the cheapest quote they've seen rather than the realistic one, and then discover the gap only after signing. This article works through what an MVP build actually costs at seed stage, by region and by scope, so the number in your budget spreadsheet matches the number a vendor will actually deliver against.

## What a Seed Round Actually Funds

European seed rounds in 2026 typically land between €1.5 million and €5 million, intended to fund 18-24 months of runway to a Series A-ready set of metrics. Of that, software development — whether building the MVP from scratch or hardening a pre-seed prototype into something that can carry real user load — commonly consumes 20-35% of total spend across the seed period once you include ongoing iteration, not just the initial build. On a €2.5 million round, that's €500,000-€875,000 across the runway, front-loaded heavily in the first six to nine months. Founders who budget only for the initial MVP sprint and not for the three to six months of iteration that follows first launch consistently run into a funding gap exactly when usage data starts telling them what to fix.

## Rate Benchmarks: What You're Actually Comparing

Vendor rates vary enormously by region, and comparing quotes without normalizing for this is the single most common seed-stage budgeting mistake. US-based agencies typically bill $150-$250 per hour for senior engineering talent. Western European agencies run €90-€160 per hour. Eastern European teams (Poland, Romania, Ukraine) run €45-€75 per hour. Vietnam and broader Southeast Asian dedicated teams run €25-€45 per hour for comparable senior-level output, with English-language project management and structured delivery processes increasingly standard among established firms. A full-stack MVP requiring roughly 800-1,400 development hours — a realistic range for a genuinely functional product with authentication, core workflows, a basic admin surface, and payment integration — costs €120,000-€350,000 at Western European rates and €20,000-€63,000 at Vietnam-based dedicated team rates for materially the same scope. The gap isn't quality by default; it's cost of living and market rate, which is exactly why offshore and nearshore models have become standard practice for seed-stage teams rather than a compromise.

## The Timeline Reality Behind "MVP in Six Weeks"

A six-week MVP timeline is achievable only for a genuinely minimal scope — a single core workflow with no integrations, no admin tooling, and placeholder-quality UI. The moment a product needs payment processing, a third-party API integration, role-based permissions, or anything beyond the single happy path, realistic timelines move to 10-16 weeks for a team working full-time on the build. Vendors who quote six weeks for a full-featured MVP are either underscoping what "MVP" means (and will hit you with change orders once real requirements surface) or padding the estimate with unstated assumptions that don't match your actual product. Ask any vendor to walk through their week-by-week delivery plan against your specific feature list before accepting a timeline — a vendor unwilling or unable to do this hasn't actually scoped your project.

## Fixed Scope vs. Iterative Budget Allocation

The biggest single budgeting error at seed stage is treating the MVP as a one-time, fixed-cost project rather than the first phase of an ongoing build. Seed-stage products change fast in the first two quarters after launch as usage data reveals what users actually do versus what the founding team assumed — and a vendor engagement structured as a single fixed-price contract with no built-in capacity for iteration leaves you either re-negotiating a new contract under time pressure or stuck with a static product while competitors iterate faster. Budget the seed round software spend as two components: an initial build phase (typically 40-50% of total software budget) and an ongoing iteration capacity, ideally structured as a [dedicated team](https://www.manifera.com/services/offshore-software-development/) retained month to month rather than re-scoped project by project.

## Where Seed-Stage Founders Overspend

The most common seed-stage overspend is custom-building infrastructure that off-the-shelf tools already solve well — authentication, payments, email delivery, basic analytics. A vendor proposing to custom-build a payments system rather than integrating Stripe, or custom-building user authentication rather than using an established identity provider, is either inexperienced or padding the scope. The second common overspend is premature scalability engineering: building for 100,000 concurrent users when the product needs to prove it can retain 100 real ones first. A seed-stage MVP should be architected to not require a rewrite at 10x usage, but engineering effort spent on infrastructure that only matters past Series A scale is runway spent on a problem you don't have yet.

## Evaluating a Vendor's Budget Credibility

A credible seed-stage vendor quote breaks down hours or cost by feature area, not just a single lump sum — you should be able to see roughly what authentication, the core workflow, the admin panel, and payments integration each cost, so you can make informed trade-offs if the budget needs to shrink. Ask what happens to the estimate if a feature turns out to be more complex than scoped — a credible vendor has a defined change process, not a vague assurance that "we'll figure it out." And ask for a reference from a comparable seed-stage client, specifically about whether the delivered cost matched the quoted cost, since a vendor's win rate on staying within their own estimates says more about their planning discipline than any portfolio piece.

## Making the Budget Call

The right seed-stage software budget isn't the cheapest quote you can find — it's the number that gets you a product real enough to generate the usage and retention data a Series A requires, without leaving you unable to iterate on what that data tells you. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What percentage of a seed round should go to software development?

Most European seed-stage founders allocate 20-35% of total round size to software development across the full runway period, including both the initial build and post-launch iteration — not just the first sprint. On a €2.5 million round, that's roughly €500,000-€875,000 spread across 18-24 months.

### Why do MVP cost quotes vary so much between vendors?

Almost entirely due to regional rate differences and scope assumptions. US and Western European senior rates run €90-€250 per hour, while Eastern European and Southeast Asian dedicated teams run €25-€75 per hour for comparable output. Always normalize quotes by hours estimated, not just total price, before comparing.

### Is a six-week MVP timeline realistic?

Only for a genuinely minimal single-workflow scope with no integrations or admin tooling. Once payments, third-party APIs, or role-based access enter the picture, realistic timelines run 10-16 weeks for a full-time team. Treat any six-week quote for a full-featured product as under-scoped.

### Should the MVP budget be a single fixed-price project or ongoing capacity?

Structure it as both: an initial build phase covering roughly 40-50% of total software budget, followed by ongoing dedicated-team iteration capacity. A single fixed-price contract with no room for post-launch iteration routinely forces a stressful re-negotiation right when usage data starts revealing what to fix.

### What's the biggest way seed-stage founders overspend on their MVP?

Custom-building infrastructure that established tools already solve — payments, authentication, email delivery — and over-engineering for scale the product hasn't earned yet. Both consume budget that should go toward proving the core product works for real users first.
