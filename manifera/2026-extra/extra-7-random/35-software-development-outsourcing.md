The standard software development outsourcing cost comparison — an outsourced hourly rate against an internal engineer's salary — compares an outsourced provider's fully-loaded rate against a fraction of an internal hire's actual fully-loaded cost, and a CFO who runs the comparison this way is comparing two numbers that aren't actually comparable.

**The Pain:** A CFO evaluating software development outsourcing against internal hiring typically receives a comparison built around the outsourced hourly rate against the internal candidate's base salary — a comparison that feels straightforward and looks unfavorable to outsourcing at first glance, without accounting for the substantial costs an internal hire carries beyond base salary that the outsourced rate has already absorbed.

**The Agitation:** A CFO who approves internal hiring over outsourcing based on an apples-to-oranges cost comparison commits the company to a fully-loaded internal cost that, once benefits, overhead, recruiting, management time, and turnover risk are properly counted, frequently exceeds the outsourced alternative's fully-loaded cost by a meaningful margin — a miscalculation that compounds every year the internal hire remains on payroll, and one that's specifically caused by comparing an unloaded internal number against a fully-loaded outsourced one.

## What the Honest Comparison Actually Requires

A genuine software development outsourcing cost comparison requires loading both sides of the comparison equally — the full cost of an internal hire, not just base salary, against the full cost of the outsourcing arrangement, not just the quoted hourly rate — and a CFO who insists on this symmetry gets a comparison that actually reflects the decision.

The internal side of a properly loaded comparison includes several categories beyond base salary that a CFO should insist on quantifying explicitly. Benefits and employer-side taxes typically add 20-35% on top of base salary depending on jurisdiction. Recruiting cost — agency fees, internal recruiting time, and the opportunity cost of the position remaining unfilled during the search — adds a meaningful one-time cost that's easy to omit because it doesn't show up as an ongoing line item. Management overhead — the time a manager spends on a direct report's performance management, career development, and day-to-day supervision — is a real cost that's rarely quantified but genuinely consumes management capacity that has other valuable uses. Turnover risk — the statistical likelihood of needing to repeat the recruiting cost within a few years, weighted by the company's actual historical turnover rate for similar roles — is a genuine expected cost that a single-hire cost comparison ignores entirely.

The outsourcing side of a properly loaded comparison should include the management overhead of overseeing the outsourced arrangement, which is real but typically lower than direct-employee management overhead, and any transition or onboarding cost specific to standing up the outsourcing relationship, which is usually lower than an internal hire's recruiting cost but not zero.

Once both sides are loaded consistently, the comparison a CFO ends up with is frequently much closer than the naive base-salary-versus-hourly-rate comparison suggested, and the actual right answer, in a meaningful share of cases, favors outsourcing on pure cost grounds even before factoring in speed to start and flexibility to scale up or down — advantages the naive comparison doesn't even attempt to quantify but that carry real value a CFO should weigh alongside the corrected cost numbers.
