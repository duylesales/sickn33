Most build-versus-buy software decisions aren't actually decisions — they're justifications for a preference someone already had before the analysis started, and a CFO who runs the numbers honestly after the fact usually discovers the analysis was reverse-engineered to support a conclusion, not to reach one.

**The Pain:** A CFO is asked to weigh in on a build-versus-buy software decision, and the "analysis" arrives already leaning heavily toward whichever option the requesting team preferred going in — engineering usually prefers to build, operations usually prefers to buy — with the supporting numbers assembled to make that preference look like the objectively correct answer rather than genuinely weighed against the alternative.

**The Agitation:** A build-versus-buy decision made to justify a pre-existing preference, rather than genuinely weighed, produces the wrong answer often enough to be expensive at scale — a company that builds when it should have bought spends multiples of the cost of an off-the-shelf solution maintaining something that never needed to be custom, while a company that buys when it should have built ends up constrained by a tool that can't do what the business genuinely needs, paying an ongoing cost in workarounds and lost capability that a build would have avoided.

## Running the Analysis Honestly, Not Backwards

A genuine build-versus-buy software analysis starts from a small number of variables applied consistently, regardless of which answer the requesting team walked in preferring, and a CFO who insists on this structure gets a decision that's actually defensible.

The first variable is genuine differentiation, addressed earlier in the context of custom software broadly: does this specific capability reflect something the business does uniquely well, or is it a commodity function every company in the category needs similarly. Genuine differentiation favors building; commodity functionality favors buying, and a CFO should push back hard on any analysis that skips this question or treats it as self-evident without actually examining it.

The second variable is total cost of ownership over a realistic multi-year horizon, not just the initial acquisition cost. Buying software has a visible, predictable subscription cost; building software has a build cost that's usually visible and a maintenance cost that's usually underestimated or omitted entirely. A fair comparison requires estimating both paths' full multi-year cost, including the maintenance burden building creates and the potential for a buy option's subscription costs to scale unfavorably as usage grows.

The third variable is speed to value — how quickly each path actually delivers working capability, since buying typically wins decisively here for anything not requiring deep customization, and a build option's timeline should be estimated realistically, including the discovery, development, and testing phases that a "we'll just build it" instinct tends to compress in the initial pitch.

The fourth variable is switching cost and lock-in risk in both directions — buying creates dependency on a vendor's roadmap and pricing decisions, which carries real risk if that vendor's priorities diverge from the company's needs over time; building creates dependency on the company's own ongoing capacity to maintain what it built, which carries real risk if that capacity isn't reliably available going forward. Neither path is risk-free, and a genuine analysis weighs both risks explicitly rather than only examining the risk of the option the team doesn't prefer.

A CFO who requires all four variables addressed explicitly, for both options, before accepting a build-versus-buy recommendation converts what's usually a preference dressed up as analysis into an actual decision, and that discipline alone measurably improves the accuracy of these calls over time.

## A Scoring Rubric for the Four-Variable Analysis

A CFO who wants the four-variable framework to produce a consistent, comparable answer across multiple decisions — not just a one-off analysis — can score each variable 1-5 for both build and buy, then weight the total. Differentiation and switching-cost/lock-in risk should each carry a 30% weight, since they represent the longest-horizon strategic exposure; total cost of ownership carries 25%; speed to value carries 15%, reflecting that most decisions can tolerate a few extra months more easily than they can tolerate the wrong multi-year commitment. A capability scoring 4-5 on differentiation should require a build score at least 1.5 points above buy on the weighted total before buying is even considered, because genuine differentiation rarely survives being outsourced to a commodity tool regardless of near-term cost savings.

In practice, this rubric surfaces a pattern most CFOs recognize once they see it: engineering-proposed build cases tend to score speed to value optimistically (compressing discovery and testing by 30-40% versus realistic estimates), while operations-proposed buy cases tend to under-score lock-in risk by ignoring vendor pricing-escalation history. Requiring the rubric filled out by someone outside the requesting team — not the engineering lead pitching to build, not the ops lead pitching to buy — removes most of the bias a narrative-only analysis carries.

Check whether all four variables — differentiation, total cost of ownership, speed to value, and lock-in risk — were applied consistently to both options, or whether the analysis only closely examined the option the team already preferred.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to properly compare build and buy total costs) Why does total cost of ownership matter more than initial acquisition cost in a build-versus-buy decision?

Because building software creates an ongoing maintenance cost that's frequently underestimated or omitted, and a fair comparison requires estimating both paths' full multi-year cost.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to assess lock-in risk for both build and buy options) Does buying software eliminate lock-in risk compared to building?

No, buying creates dependency on a vendor's roadmap and pricing, while building creates dependency on the company's own ongoing maintenance capacity — both carry real risk that should be weighed explicitly.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to determine whether a capability is genuinely differentiating) What's the first question to ask in a genuine build-versus-buy analysis?

Whether the capability reflects something the business does uniquely well, or is a commodity function every company in the category needs similarly.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to estimate the cost of a wrong build-versus-buy decision) What does it typically cost when a company builds software it should have bought?

Multiples of the cost of an off-the-shelf solution, spent maintaining custom software that never needed to be custom in the first place.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to weight the four build-versus-buy variables consistently across multiple decisions) How should a CFO weight differentiation, cost, speed, and lock-in risk when scoring a build-versus-buy decision?

Weight differentiation and lock-in risk at roughly 30% each, given their longest-horizon strategic exposure, total cost of ownership at 25%, and speed to value at 15%, since a timeline slip is usually more recoverable than the wrong multi-year commitment.

### (Scenario: CFO worried that engineering and operations teams score their own build-versus-buy proposals with bias) Who should own scoring a build-versus-buy analysis to avoid team bias?

Someone outside the requesting team — engineering pitching to build tends to compress speed-to-value estimates by 30-40%, and operations pitching to buy tends to under-score vendor lock-in risk, so a neutral scorer removes most of that systematic bias.
