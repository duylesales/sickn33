"Software services" is a label broad enough to cover a two-hour consulting call, a twelve-month product build, and a five-year managed platform relationship — and a CEO who searches for a software services provider without knowing which of these they actually need will end up evaluating proposals that aren't really comparable to each other.

**The Pain:** A CEO knows the business needs "software services" of some kind — a new system, an upgrade, ongoing support for something already built — but hasn't translated that general need into a specific category of engagement, and every provider conversation ends up talking past the actual need because the CEO and the vendor are implicitly assuming different definitions of what's being discussed.

**The Agitation:** A CEO who engages a software services provider without first clarifying which category of engagement is actually needed frequently ends up with a mismatch that surfaces expensively — hiring a project-based team for what was actually an ongoing operational need, or committing to a long-term managed relationship for what was actually a one-time build, either of which produces a contract structure poorly suited to the real requirement, typically costing 20-40% more than a correctly matched engagement would have.

## The Real Categories Behind "Software Services"

Software services, stripped of the marketing language, breaks into a small number of genuinely distinct categories, and a CEO who identifies which one actually applies before talking to vendors has a dramatically more productive set of conversations.

The first category is advisory and consulting — expert guidance on a specific technical decision or strategy question, without hands-on building. This fits a company that has its own engineering capability and needs outside expertise on a specific question: which architecture pattern fits a scaling challenge, how to structure a technology roadmap, whether a proposed vendor's technical claims hold up. Engaging a full development team for what's actually an advisory need wastes money on capacity that isn't the actual bottleneck.

The second category is project-based development — a defined build with a start and an end, delivering a specific piece of software against an agreed scope. This fits a genuinely bounded need: a new product, a specific integration, a defined feature set. The critical requirement for this category to work well is that the scope actually can be defined upfront with reasonable confidence — project-based engagements struggle precisely when the underlying need turns out to be more open-ended than initially assumed.

The third category is dedicated team augmentation — ongoing engineering capacity embedded with or alongside an existing team, without a fixed end date, for genuinely continuous product development. This fits a company with an evolving product that needs sustained capacity, not a one-time deliverable — and it's the category most often mismatched with project-based contracts, producing an artificial "end date" for work that was never actually going to be finished, just handed off awkwardly to whoever's available next.

The fourth category is managed operations and maintenance — ongoing responsibility for keeping an existing system running, secure, and updated, without necessarily building significant new functionality. This fits a company with a stable system that needs reliable upkeep more than active development, and engaging a full project-based build team for what's actually a maintenance need means paying for development capacity that has nothing to build.

A CEO evaluating software services should start by honestly answering which of these four actually describes the need, because that answer, more than any specific vendor's marketing, determines what contract structure, pricing model, and team composition will actually fit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CEO trying to figure out what kind of software services the business actually needs) How do I know which category of software services my business actually needs?

Ask whether the need has a genuinely definable end state (project-based), is ongoing and evolving (dedicated team), is a specific expert question (advisory), or is about keeping an existing system running (managed maintenance).

### (Scenario: CEO who engaged a project-based contract for what turned out to be ongoing work) Why does a project-based contract sometimes lead to repeated scope renegotiation?

Because the underlying need was actually open-ended and continuously evolving, not genuinely bounded, so the artificial project end date keeps getting pushed as the real, ongoing need becomes apparent.

### (Scenario: CEO trying to avoid overpaying for the wrong type of engagement) What's the financial risk of choosing the wrong category of software services engagement?

Typically a 20-40% cost premium through repeated renegotiation, mismatched team composition, or paying for development capacity that isn't actually the bottleneck.

### (Scenario: CEO deciding between advisory services and a full development team) When does advisory-only engagement make more sense than a full development team?

When the company already has its own engineering capability and needs outside expertise on a specific decision or strategy question, not hands-on building capacity.

### (Scenario: CEO trying to determine if a system needs active development or maintenance) How do we know if we need active development versus managed maintenance?

If the system is stable and the priority is reliability, security, and uptime rather than significant new functionality, managed maintenance fits better than a full development engagement.
