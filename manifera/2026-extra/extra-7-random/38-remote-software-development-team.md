A co-located team gets a substantial amount of genuinely valuable coordination for free, through informal hallway conversations, overheard context, and incidental awareness of what colleagues are working on — none of which a remote software development team gets automatically, and a VP of Engineering who doesn't deliberately rebuild a substitute loses real coordination value without noticing the loss's specific source.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering running a remote software development team typically focuses deliberate process design on the formal, scheduled parts of team coordination — standups, planning meetings, documented specs — because those are the visible, easy-to-design parts of the process, while the informal, incidental coordination a co-located team gets automatically has no obvious remote equivalent and often just doesn't get replaced at all.

**The Agitation:** A remote software development team missing a substitute for informal coordination experiences a specific, diffuse cost — duplicated work because two engineers didn't happen to know they were both approaching a related problem, missed opportunities for a quick clarifying question that would have been trivial in a hallway but feels like it requires scheduling a meeting when remote, and slower propagation of useful context that a co-located team would have picked up through simple proximity — costs that accumulate steadily and are hard to attribute to any single missing process, because the thing missing isn't a process, it's an entire informal communication layer.

## Deliberately Rebuilding the Informal Layer

A remote software development team needs specific, deliberately designed substitutes for the informal coordination a co-located team gets automatically, and a VP of Engineering who treats this as a real design problem, not an unavoidable cost of remote work, can recover most of the value a co-located team gets for free.

The first substitute is a lightweight, low-friction channel for exactly the kind of small, quick questions that would have been a hallway conversation — a dedicated space, culturally normalized as appropriate for informal, low-stakes questions, distinct from formal project channels, where asking something quick doesn't carry the implicit weight of "starting a documented thread" that makes people hesitate to ask small things remotely. The specific cultural norm that needs deliberate reinforcement is that asking something small and informal here is genuinely encouraged, not a minor imposition — because remote workers without that explicit reassurance tend to under-ask relative to what they'd have asked in a hallway, precisely because the remote channel feels more formal by default.

The second substitute is deliberate, lightweight visibility into what colleagues are actively working on — not a heavyweight status-reporting process, but something closer to ambient awareness: brief, low-effort updates in a shared space that let team members notice, without deliberately searching, when their own work overlaps with something a colleague is doing. This substitutes for the incidental awareness co-located teams get by overhearing conversations or seeing what's on someone's screen, and its absence is specifically what causes duplicated effort that isn't caught until later.

The third substitute is intentionally scheduled unstructured time — not a formal meeting with an agenda, but time explicitly set aside without a specific task, framed as space for the kind of tangential, exploratory conversation that happens spontaneously in person and simply doesn't happen at all on a remote team unless it's deliberately scheduled, because remote default settings make every interaction feel like it needs a purpose.

A VP of Engineering who deliberately designs these three substitutes treats the informal communication layer as a real design problem worth genuine attention, rather than accepting its absence as an unavoidable cost of being remote — and the difference shows up as measurably less duplicated work, faster propagation of useful context, and a team that asks more of the small clarifying questions that prevent larger misunderstandings downstream.
