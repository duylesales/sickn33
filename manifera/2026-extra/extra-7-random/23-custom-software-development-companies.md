Every custom software development company nails the sales process — that's what a sales process is for. The signal that actually predicts how the engagement will go shows up earlier and more quietly than the sales pitch, in how the company handles the discovery conversation before any contract exists.

**The Pain:** A CTO evaluating custom software development companies is naturally focused on portfolio quality, technical credentials, and reference checks — the standard due diligence — while a smaller, easy-to-miss signal from the earliest conversations often predicts the engagement's trajectory more reliably than any of that standard diligence, because it's harder for a company to fake under the specific conditions where it shows up.

**The Agitation:** A CTO who evaluates only the standard signals — portfolio, references, credentials — selects among companies that have all, by definition, cleared that bar, which means the standard signals don't actually differentiate a company that will genuinely serve the CTO's specific interests from one that will prioritize its own convenience the moment those two things diverge, a divergence that shows up specifically during discovery, before any commitment locks either side in.

## The Signal That Shows Up Before the Contract Exists

The single most predictive signal in evaluating custom software development companies is how a prospective vendor behaves during the discovery conversation, specifically whether they push back on a stated requirement that's technically questionable, or simply agree with everything the CTO says to keep the sales process moving smoothly.

A company genuinely invested in a good outcome will, during discovery, identify at least one place where the CTO's initial framing of the problem is incomplete or where a stated requirement would create a technical problem down the line — not to be difficult, but because a company that's actually thinking carefully about the project, rather than just trying to close the deal, notices these things and says so, even though raising a concern during a sales conversation carries some risk of seeming difficult or slowing down the close.

A company optimizing purely for closing the deal agrees readily with whatever the CTO proposes, asks clarifying questions but never substantive challenging ones, and produces a discovery conversation that feels smooth and easy — which feels good in the moment and is actually a warning sign, because the same instinct to avoid friction during a sales conversation, when it's a company-level pattern rather than an individual salesperson's style, tends to persist into the engagement itself, producing a team that implements requirements literally rather than pushing back on ones that deserve pushback, right up until a costly problem ships that a moment of friction during discovery would have prevented.

The practical test a CTO can run is simple: during the discovery conversation with any custom software development company, present at least one requirement or assumption that's genuinely questionable — not a trick, just something a careful technical partner should reasonably flag — and see whether the company catches it. A company that does is demonstrating, under real conditions rather than a rehearsed answer, the kind of technical engagement a CTO actually wants for the life of the project. A company that doesn't is showing exactly the pattern that will make problems harder to catch once real development starts and the stakes of unflagged issues are considerably higher.

This test costs a CTO nothing beyond deliberately including a testable assumption in the discovery conversation, and it produces a genuinely differentiating signal among companies whose portfolios and references all clear the standard bar equally.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Ghent Insurer's Too-Easy Discovery Call

Verzekeringsplatform Gent NV, a Ghent-based insurance-technology company, had selected a custom software development company whose discovery conversation had agreed readily with every requirement presented, including a data-retention assumption that turned out to conflict with the company's actual regulatory obligations — a conflict the vendor never flagged, discovered only when an internal compliance review caught it three months into development, forcing a costly mid-project redesign.

Manifera's subsequent discovery process for the company's next project deliberately probed several stated requirements, catching two genuine issues — including a data-handling assumption similar to the one that had caused the earlier problem — before any development began, at zero cost to catch versus the substantial redesign cost of catching it later.
