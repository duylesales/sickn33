A VP of Engineering running three outsourced development vendors typically manages all three the same way — a weekly status call, a shared ticket queue, an annual contract review — even though one vendor delivers a peripheral internal tool, another owns 40% of the company's core platform capacity, and the third is a single contractor with no bench behind them. Vendor management applied uniformly across a genuinely uneven risk portfolio protects the company from exactly none of the risks that actually matter.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering managing multiple outsourced development vendors tends to treat vendor management as an administrative layer — status meetings, invoice approval, a yearly satisfaction survey — bolted onto whatever process already governs internal teams, rather than as a distinct discipline with its own mechanisms: contractual escalation paths, knowledge continuity requirements, and independent output verification that doesn't rely on the vendor's own self-reporting.

**The Agitation:** A vendor providing a significant share of a company's engineering capacity, managed with no formal escalation path beyond a single account manager and no visibility into who on the vendor's side actually holds the critical knowledge, is one personnel change or one contract dispute away from a multi-week capability gap — and companies that discover this the hard way commonly report six to twelve weeks of degraded delivery while a replacement vendor or internal team rebuilds context that was never documented anywhere the client could access independently.

## Vendor Management Is a Portfolio Problem, Not a Relationship Problem

**Segment vendors by criticality tier, not by spend.** The natural instinct is to give the most management attention to the vendor with the largest invoice, but the right axis is criticality — how much of the roadmap depends on this vendor, and how quickly a failure would become visible to customers. A vendor building an internal admin tool and a vendor owning the core transaction pipeline should never receive the same governance cadence, escalation depth, or continuity requirements, even if their invoices are similar.

**Define escalation paths before you need them.** Most vendor relationships have an escalation path that exists only in theory — a vague understanding that "if something goes wrong, we'll talk to our account manager." A functioning escalation path names specific individuals on both sides at each severity level, states response-time commitments in the contract rather than in a slide deck, and has been tested at least once before a real incident forces it to be tested for the first time under pressure.

**Guard against key-person dependency at the vendor, not just internally.** Engineering leaders routinely audit their own teams for bus-factor risk but rarely ask the equivalent question about a vendor: if the two engineers who actually understand this codebase left the vendor tomorrow, would the client even know until delivery slipped? Contractual requirements for documented knowledge transfer, shared access to architecture decisions, and periodic knowledge-continuity checks close this blind spot.

**Verify output, not attendance.** A vendor that shows up to every status call and closes every sprint's tickets on time can still be shipping code that accumulates technical debt, skips edge-case handling, or drifts from architectural standards — none of which shows up in a status meeting. Independent code review, automated quality gates the vendor doesn't control, and periodic architecture audits are the actual verification mechanism; attendance and ticket velocity are proxies that a vendor under schedule pressure can game without anyone noticing for months.

**Build a transition plan for every vendor relationship from day one, not after the relationship sours.** The vendors that are hardest to exit are the ones where no one thought about exit at the start — no documentation standard was enforced, no code or infrastructure access was retained independently, and no second vendor or internal team was ever kept warm enough to absorb the work quickly. A transition plan drafted at onboarding, and revisited annually, turns a potential crisis into a manageable, if unwelcome, project.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering managing several outsourced vendors with no differentiated governance) Should every outsourced development vendor be managed with the same cadence and escalation depth?

No. Vendor management should be segmented by criticality — how much of the roadmap depends on the vendor and how quickly a failure becomes customer-visible — not by contract size or relationship comfort.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about losing a vendor's key engineers) How does a company guard against key-person dependency at a vendor it doesn't directly manage?

By contractually requiring documented knowledge transfer, shared visibility into architecture decisions, and periodic knowledge-continuity checks that don't rely on any single engineer at the vendor remaining available.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering relying on status calls to judge vendor performance) Why isn't a vendor's on-time ticket closure a reliable signal of quality?

Because ticket velocity and meeting attendance are proxies a vendor under schedule pressure can maintain while quietly accumulating technical debt, skipping edge cases, or drifting from architecture standards — none of which shows up in a status update.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering who has never tested an escalation path) When should an escalation path with a vendor actually be tested?

Before a real incident forces it — an escalation path that has never been exercised is a theory, not a functioning process, and the first real test should not be the first time it's tried.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering who has never planned an exit from a vendor relationship) Why should a transition plan be built at the start of a vendor relationship rather than when it ends badly?

Because the practices that make an exit manageable — enforced documentation, independent access to code and infrastructure, and a warm secondary option — are far cheaper to establish from day one than to retrofit once a relationship is already deteriorating.
