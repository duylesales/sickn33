Three mobile app development company quotes, three different numbers, and a CFO who picks based on the number alone has no way of knowing whether the lowest quote reflects genuine efficiency or a scope gap that surfaces as a change order the moment development actually starts.

**The Pain:** A CFO comparing quotes from mobile app development companies is working from documents that all describe roughly the same feature list at meaningfully different prices, with no reliable way to tell whether the price difference reflects real efficiency, a lower-cost talent pool, or a scope gap where something genuinely necessary — platform-specific testing, post-launch support, app-store compliance — simply wasn't included in the cheaper number.

**The Agitation:** A CFO who selects a mobile app development company on quoted price alone routinely discovers the real total cost only after signing, when a change order arrives for something that should have been in the original scope, or when the delivered app fails app-store review over a compliance issue the quote never accounted for. That gap between quoted price and actual total cost typically runs 20-40% for engagements where the original comparison wasn't apples-to-apples.

## What a Fair Comparison Between Quotes Actually Requires

Comparing mobile app development company quotes fairly requires breaking each one into specific components, because a single headline number can hide meaningfully different scope underneath an apparently similar price.

The first component to verify is platform coverage specificity — does the quote cover genuine native development for both iOS and Android, or does it assume a cross-platform framework that may not deliver the same performance or platform-specific feature access, and is that assumption made explicit or buried in fine print. A quote that looks cheaper because it defaults to a lighter-weight technical approach isn't necessarily wrong, but a CFO needs to know that's the tradeoff being made, not discover it after the fact.

The second component is testing and QA scope, specifically whether the quote includes testing against a realistic range of devices and OS versions, or assumes testing on whatever device happens to be in the developer's hand. A mobile app development company that quotes a lower number partly by minimizing testing scope is quoting a lower risk tolerance for post-launch defects, and a CFO should know that tradeoff is being made before signing, not discover it through a wave of one-star reviews after launch.

The third component is app-store submission and compliance handling — whether the quote includes navigating App Store and Play Store review guidelines, handling a potential rejection and resubmission, and setting up the app-store accounts under client ownership, or whether this is treated as the client's problem to figure out separately. A quote that excludes this is missing a step that, done wrong, can add weeks to a launch timeline the client didn't budget for.

The fourth component, and the one most CFOs forget to ask about explicitly, is what happens after launch — whether the quote includes any post-launch support window for the inevitable early bugs and OS-update compatibility issues, or whether that's a separate, unbudgeted engagement the client will need to negotiate from scratch once the original team has moved on. A quote that's silent on this isn't necessarily hiding something, but a CFO comparing quotes needs to normalize for it explicitly, because a cheaper quote with zero post-launch support isn't actually cheaper once that gap surfaces.
