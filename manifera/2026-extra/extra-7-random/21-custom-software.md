Custom software is either the smartest investment a company makes that year, or an expensive way to rebuild something that already exists, cheaper and better-tested, off the shelf — and the difference between the two comes down to one honest question most companies skip.

**The Pain:** A CEO is considering custom software for a business process, and the instinct to build something tailored feels reasonable — the existing off-the-shelf options don't quite fit, and a custom solution promises to match the business exactly. What's less examined is whether the mismatch with off-the-shelf tools reflects something genuinely differentiating about the business, or just a workflow quirk that could be adjusted more cheaply than building custom software to accommodate it.

**The Agitation:** A company that builds custom software for a process that isn't actually differentiating pays the full cost of custom development — design, build, ongoing maintenance, the opportunity cost of engineering time — for the same functional outcome an off-the-shelf tool would have provided at a fraction of the cost and with none of the maintenance burden, typically overpaying by €80,000-€200,000 over the system's useful life once total cost of ownership is properly counted.

## The One Question That Actually Determines the Right Call

Custom software is justified specifically when a business process is genuinely differentiating — when the way the company does something is part of its actual competitive advantage, not an accident of how things have always been done — and a CEO who applies this test honestly avoids the most common and most expensive custom-software mistake.

The test itself is simple to state: would a competitor copying this specific process exactly still struggle to replicate the advantage, because the value comes from institutional knowledge, proprietary data, or a genuinely unique workflow that can't be bought off the shelf? If yes, custom software is very likely worth its cost, because an off-the-shelf tool structurally can't capture what makes the process valuable. If no — if the process is fundamentally similar to what every other company in the category does, just with minor workflow preferences layered on top — an off-the-shelf tool, possibly with light configuration, usually delivers the same functional outcome for a fraction of the cost.

The mistake most companies make is conflating "this doesn't perfectly match our current workflow" with "this is differentiating." Nearly every off-the-shelf tool requires some adaptation of how a company currently works, and that friction feels like evidence the tool doesn't fit, when it's often just the normal cost of adopting any new system. A CEO evaluating custom software should ask specifically whether the workflow being protected is genuinely valuable to preserve, or whether it's simply familiar, and familiar isn't the same as valuable.

The second consideration, once genuine differentiation is confirmed, is scope discipline — building custom software specifically for the differentiating part of the process, while using off-the-shelf tools or simpler custom components for the surrounding, non-differentiating parts. A company that decides one process genuinely needs custom software often over-extends that decision to the entire surrounding system, building custom solutions for parts that were never actually the differentiating element, multiplying cost for capability that didn't need to be bespoke.

The economics genuinely favor custom software when the differentiation test passes clearly — the cost of building and maintaining a tailored system is worth paying because no off-the-shelf alternative could deliver the same competitive value. The economics favor off-the-shelf when the test fails, and a CEO who builds custom software anyway is paying a premium for a preference, not a genuine business need.
