Nearly every company that sets out to build an MVP ends up building something considerably bigger than minimum, because "minimum" is the hardest word in "minimum viable product" to actually hold the line on, and scope creep during MVP software development is so common it should be treated as the default outcome to plan against, not an occasional risk.

**The Pain:** A CEO commissioning MVP software development genuinely intends to build something minimal — a focused first version to test a core hypothesis with real users — but the specific features that get added during the build each feel individually reasonable and low-cost, and the cumulative effect of many individually-reasonable additions is an MVP that's no longer minimum by the time it ships.

**The Agitation:** An MVP that scope-creeps into something considerably larger than minimum loses the two things that make an MVP valuable in the first place — speed to market, since a bloated MVP takes considerably longer to build than a genuinely minimal one, and clarity of learning, since a larger, more feature-rich MVP makes it harder to isolate which specific element of the product is actually driving user behavior, defeating the entire diagnostic purpose of building an MVP rather than a full product.

## Why Scope Creep Happens and How to Actually Prevent It

MVP scope creep happens for a specific, predictable reason: each individual feature addition, considered in isolation, seems to have an obviously positive cost-benefit ratio — a small additional build cost for a feature that seems like it would clearly improve the product — and a team evaluating additions one at a time, rather than against the MVP's original defined boundary, approves each one on its own individually-reasonable merits without noticing the cumulative scope has drifted well past minimum.

The practical defense against this isn't willpower or a general commitment to staying lean — those consistently fail against the steady drip of individually-reasonable requests — it's a specific, written definition of the MVP's boundary, established before the build begins, defining exactly which single core hypothesis the MVP exists to test and which single core user action constitutes success. Any feature addition proposed during the build gets evaluated against this specific written boundary, not against its own individual merit: does this feature contribute directly to testing the core hypothesis, or does it improve the product in some other way that, however genuinely valuable, isn't the specific thing this MVP exists to learn.

A feature that doesn't serve the core hypothesis test gets deferred to a backlog for after the MVP ships and the hypothesis is validated, not rejected outright — many of these additions are genuinely good ideas for the eventual full product, just not necessary for what the MVP specifically needs to prove. This reframing — "not now" rather than "not ever" — makes the discipline considerably easier to hold, since the team isn't losing good ideas, just correctly sequencing them after the point where they'd interfere with getting a clean answer to the MVP's core question.

A CEO's specific role in this process is holding the written boundary against pressure to expand it mid-build — pressure that inevitably arrives, from stakeholders wanting their specific feature included, from the team's own instinct to build a more complete-feeling product — and the CEO who holds that line consistently gets a genuinely fast, genuinely diagnostic MVP; the CEO who doesn't gets a slower, more expensive product that answers a blurrier question than a real MVP was supposed to answer.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Bergen Startup's Scope-Crept MVP

Digitale Løsninger Bergen AS, a Bergen-based startup, had commissioned an MVP intended to test whether small fishing-fleet operators would adopt a digital maintenance-scheduling tool, only to watch the build absorb a steady stream of individually-reasonable feature requests from stakeholders until the "MVP" had grown into a nine-month build testing no single clear hypothesis.

Manifera's subsequent MVP for the company's next hypothesis established a specific written boundary upfront — testing exclusively whether operators would log a single maintenance event through the tool weekly — and deferred fourteen individually-reasonable feature requests to a documented post-MVP backlog during the six-week build. The MVP shipped in six weeks instead of nine months and delivered a clear, unambiguous answer to the specific hypothesis it was built to test.

> *"The first MVP took nine months and we still couldn't say clearly what we'd learned, because it had become too many things at once. The second one took six weeks and gave us an answer we could actually act on, because we said no to fourteen good ideas that weren't the point of that build."*
> — **CEO, Digitale Løsninger Bergen AS, Norway**
