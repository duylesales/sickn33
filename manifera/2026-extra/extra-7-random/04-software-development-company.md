Every software development company's homepage promises the same three things — senior talent, agile process, transparent communication — and a CEO evaluating five proposals that all say exactly this has learned precisely nothing useful about which one will actually deliver.

**The Pain:** A CEO without a technical co-founder or in-house CTO needs to choose a software development company to build a product the business depends on, and every proposal reads persuasively, uses the same reassuring language, and shows a portfolio of plausible-looking past work — leaving no obvious way to distinguish the partner who will actually deliver from the one who will produce eight months of expensive disappointment.

**The Agitation:** A CEO who picks the wrong software development company doesn't find out for months, because early sprints in almost any engagement look fine — the real test comes when complexity increases, requirements shift, or a senior person leaves, and that's precisely when an unprepared partner's cracks show. Recovering from a bad choice at that point typically costs a company €80,000-€180,000 in wasted spend and a restart with a different vendor, on top of the months of runway already lost.

## The Questions That Actually Separate Good From Bad

Every software development company can answer the generic evaluation questions well, because those questions have become table stakes in every sales conversation. A CEO needs a different set of questions — ones a mediocre company either can't answer specifically or answers in a way that reveals the gap.

The first real question is what happens to the relationship when the assigned team's most senior person leaves or gets reassigned. A strong software development company has a documented continuity plan — cross-trained team members, knowledge transfer practices, a defined backfill process. A weak one has never had to think about this, because it doesn't structurally protect against it, and the CEO finds out the hard way when it happens mid-project.

The second is how the company handles a fundamental disagreement about scope or approach. Ask directly: describe a real situation where you pushed back on a client's request because it was technically wrong, and what happened. A company with genuine technical integrity has a specific story. A company that just executes whatever's asked, regardless of merit, will either dodge the question or describe compliance as a virtue.

The third is what independent verification exists between the people building the software and the people telling the CEO it's going well. A software development company where the same team both delivers and self-reports has a structural conflict of interest — there's no independent check catching a problem before the CEO discovers it in a demo that doesn't work as promised. A company with genuine governance separation can describe, specifically, who verifies quality independently of the delivery team.

The fourth is what a real, verifiable reference says about the company's behavior under pressure, not just its output quality. Ask a reference specifically: was there ever a moment things went wrong, and how did the company handle it? A company's character under strain, not its performance when everything's going smoothly, is what actually predicts whether a CEO's own engagement will survive its inevitable difficult moment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CEO comparing multiple software development company proposals that all sound similar) Why do most software development company proposals sound nearly identical?

Because generic reassurances — senior talent, agile process, transparent communication — have become table stakes in every sales conversation, and answering them well doesn't distinguish a genuinely strong partner from a mediocre one.

### (Scenario: CEO trying to test a vendor's continuity planning before signing) How do we find out if a software development company can survive losing a key team member mid-project?

Ask directly about their continuity plan — cross-training, knowledge transfer practices, a defined backfill process — and specifically whether they've had to execute it before.

### (Scenario: CEO worried a vendor will just say yes to everything) How do we know if a software development company will push back when we're technically wrong?

Ask for a specific real example of them pushing back on a client request, and what happened — a company with genuine technical integrity has a concrete story, not a vague assurance.

### (Scenario: CEO trying to verify a vendor's self-reported status updates) How do we know a vendor's "everything is on track" status update is accurate?

Ask whether anyone independent of the delivery team reviews quality and progress, since a vendor grading its own work has a structural incentive to report favorably regardless of reality.
