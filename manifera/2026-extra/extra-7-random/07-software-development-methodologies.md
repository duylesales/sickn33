A team running two-week sprints, calling every meeting a "ceremony," and using the word "agile" in every sentence isn't necessarily running an agile methodology — it might just be running waterfall with better vocabulary, one two-week chunk at a time.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering inherited a team using agile terminology throughout, but the actual pattern of work looks suspiciously like a fixed, sequential plan broken into two-week increments — requirements were locked months ago, the roadmap doesn't meaningfully change sprint to sprint, and the "agile ceremonies" are really just status meetings wearing scrum vocabulary.

**The Agitation:** Choosing a methodology label without actually adopting its underlying discipline produces the worst of both worlds — the overhead of agile ceremonies (constant planning, constant re-estimation) without agile's actual benefit (genuine responsiveness to changing information), or the appearance of waterfall's predictability without its actual discipline of getting requirements right upfront before committing to a fixed plan. Teams stuck in this mismatch typically waste 15-20% of their capacity on process overhead that isn't earning its keep in either direction.

## Matching the Methodology to What the Project Actually Needs

The methodology question isn't "which one is best" in the abstract — it's which one matches the actual uncertainty profile of the specific project, and a VP of Engineering who skips this diagnosis and defaults to whatever's currently fashionable sets the team up for exactly the mismatch that wastes capacity.

Genuine agile methodology fits projects where requirements are expected to change as the team learns more — a new product where user behavior will reveal what's actually needed, a system responding to a market that's still shifting. The core discipline agile requires isn't the ceremonies, it's genuine responsiveness: the roadmap for two sprints from now should be allowed to look meaningfully different based on what was learned in the current sprint, and a team running "agile" where that never actually happens is running the ceremonies without the substance.

Genuine waterfall, or a more structured phased approach, fits projects where requirements are genuinely knowable upfront and the cost of getting something wrong late is severe — a regulated system where a specification has to be locked and approved before development starts, a hardware-adjacent project where changing course mid-build is prohibitively expensive. The discipline waterfall requires is rigor in the planning phase proportional to how expensive a late change would be — and a team running waterfall without that upfront rigor gets the downside (inflexibility) without the upside (a genuinely well-specified plan).

A hybrid approach — phased at the macro level, iterative within each phase — fits the more common real-world case: a project with some genuinely fixed constraints (a regulatory deadline, a hard integration requirement) and some genuinely uncertain areas (user-facing features where real feedback will change the plan). The discipline this requires is explicitly separating which parts of the project are fixed and which are genuinely iterative, rather than applying one methodology uniformly to a project that actually has both characteristics simultaneously.

The diagnostic question a VP of Engineering should ask before choosing, or before auditing an inherited choice, is simple: for this specific project, what's the actual cost of being wrong about a requirement discovered late, and how likely is that discovery? High cost and high likelihood argues for upfront rigor. Low cost and high likelihood argues for genuine iteration. The methodology label matters far less than whether the team's actual practice matches that honest assessment.
