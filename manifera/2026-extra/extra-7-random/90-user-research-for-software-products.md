Most engineering teams don't skip user research because they don't value it — they skip it because the version of research they've seen is a six-week study with a formal report that arrives after the sprint it was supposed to inform has already shipped, and a VP of Engineering under delivery pressure will rationally choose to ship on the team's best guess rather than wait on a process that moves slower than the codebase does.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering is routinely asked to build features based on a product manager's interpretation of a handful of sales calls or a single vocal customer, with no direct evidence that the interpretation is correct, and by the time engineering discovers the assumption was wrong — usually from post-launch usage data — the feature is built, shipped, and now sitting in the backlog as a rework item competing with new priorities.

**The Agitation:** Industry data consistently shows that a large share of shipped features, commonly cited around a third to a half, see low or negligible adoption after launch, and for a mid-sized engineering team that translates directly into months of sprint capacity spent building things that get quietly deprioritized rather than removed, a cost that compounds every quarter research is treated as optional rather than a normal input to the backlog.

## What Lightweight, Engineering-Compatible Research Actually Looks Like

**Discovery research before a feature is scoped, not after it's built.** The highest-leverage research happens before a single ticket is written — five to eight structured conversations with real users about a specific problem area, run in a single week, aimed at validating or killing an assumption before engineering time is committed to it.

**Usability testing on a working prototype, not a finished product.** Testing a clickable prototype or an early build with five to seven users reliably surfaces the majority of significant usability problems, and doing it before the feature is fully built means fixes are a design change rather than a re-engineering effort.

**A standing research cadence, not a one-off project.** Teams that treat research as a single big study repeat the mistake of the six-week report; teams that build a lightweight, recurring cadence — a few user conversations most sprints, tied directly to whatever's about to be scoped — get continuous evidence without ever blocking a release.

**Research findings routed directly into ticket-writing, not a separate deck.** Research that produces a report nobody reads changes nothing; research that produces two or three specific, falsified-or-confirmed assumptions handed directly to whoever is writing the next sprint's tickets changes what gets built.

**Quantitative usage data validating what qualitative research suggested.** Interviews and usability sessions generate hypotheses about what will work; production usage data and funnel analysis after launch confirm or contradict them, and a mature research practice closes that loop instead of treating launch as the end of the research process.

A VP of Engineering doesn't need to build a dedicated research team to get these benefits — a lightweight practice run in parallel with sprint planning, staffed by even a fractional researcher or a trained product person, catches the majority of costly wrong bets before they consume engineering capacity, which is the actual return on investment that matters to an engineering leader being asked to justify the practice.
