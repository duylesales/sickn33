## What Actually Drives Mobile App Development Cost

Mobile app development cost is driven by a specific, identifiable set of variables, and a CFO who understands these can build a realistic budget range before ever talking to a vendor, rather than anchoring on a generic industry number that doesn't reflect the actual project.

The first cost driver is platform scope — building for one platform (iOS or Android) costs meaningfully less than building genuinely native for both, and a cross-platform framework sits in between, trading some platform-specific capability for shared codebase efficiency. A CFO's first real budget decision is which of these three paths fits the actual audience and requirements, because this single choice moves the cost estimate more than almost any other variable.

The second cost driver is backend complexity, which is frequently underweighted in cost conversations that focus on the visible mobile app itself. An app that's mostly a interface on top of a simple data store costs considerably less than one requiring real-time synchronization, complex business logic, or integration with multiple external systems — the backend work is often comparable to or larger than the mobile app work itself, and a budget that only accounts for "the app" while ignoring the backend it depends on is missing a major cost component.

The third cost driver is the depth of platform-specific features required — camera integration, offline functionality, push notifications, biometric authentication, hardware sensor access — each of which adds real development and testing time beyond a basic CRUD interface. A CFO should list these explicitly against the actual product requirements, because "the app needs offline mode" sounds like a small feature request and is actually one of the more expensive capabilities to build correctly.

The fourth cost driver, and the one most consistently missing from initial budgets, is the ongoing cost after launch — maintenance, OS-update compatibility work, and iterative improvement based on real usage data, which for a genuinely successful app typically runs 15-25% of the initial build cost annually. A CFO who budgets only for the initial build and treats the app as "done" at launch is planning for a cost trajectory that doesn't match how mobile apps actually need to be maintained to stay functional and competitive.

## By The Numbers: Where the Mobile App Development Cost Actually Goes

A CFO reviewing a mobile app development cost quote line by line should expect roughly this allocation: discovery and UX architecture 10-15%, UI design 8-10%, engineering (iOS and Android combined) 45-55%, QA and device testing 12-15%, and project management 8-10%. Engineering dominates, but QA is the line CFOs most often try to compress — and the line where compression most reliably produces a post-launch bug bill larger than the savings.

On rate, the arbitrage is concrete, not vague: a Dutch or Western European senior mobile engineer bills €90-140/hour, while an equivalent Vietnam-based engineer inside a governed pod bills €35-55/hour — a 55-65% reduction on the largest line item, without the quality variance that comes from unmanaged freelance outsourcing. On absolute totals, a functional MVP (one platform, 4-6 core screens, a simple backend) typically lands between €35,000-€75,000; a full cross-platform product with real-time features and complex integrations runs €150,000-€400,000+. A CFO comparing two quotes that differ by more than 20% for what's described as the same scope should ask which of these five allocation categories the lower quote actually cut, because it's rarely engineering hours — it's almost always QA or post-launch support.

### (Scenario: CFO whose budget only accounts for the visible mobile app) Why does backend complexity matter so much to mobile app development cost?

Because the backend work supporting the app — data synchronization, business logic, integrations — is often comparable to or larger than the mobile app interface itself, and a budget that ignores it is missing a major cost component.

### (Scenario: CFO evaluating which platform-specific features to include) Why do features like offline mode or biometric authentication significantly affect cost?

Because each platform-specific capability adds real development and testing time beyond a basic interface, making features that sound small in a requirements list some of the more expensive capabilities to build correctly.

### (Scenario: CFO who treated the app as "done" once it launched) Does mobile app development cost end at launch?

No, ongoing maintenance, OS-update compatibility, and iterative improvement typically run 15-25% of the initial build cost annually for a genuinely successful app.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to avoid a mid-project budget renegotiation) How do we avoid discovering the real mobile app development cost only after committing to a budget?

Build a realistic cost model against the actual project's platform scope, backend complexity, and feature depth before any budget commitment is presented to stakeholders.

### (Scenario: CFO comparing a fixed-price quote against a time-and-materials quote) Should mobile app development cost be structured as fixed-price or time-and-materials?

Fixed-price works when requirements are stable and well-scoped, since it caps CFO risk; time-and-materials fits when the product will evolve during build, since a fixed-price contract on shifting scope simply moves the renegotiation to change orders instead of eliminating it.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding between native and cross-platform to control app development cost) Is a cross-platform framework like React Native or Flutter actually cheaper than building native iOS and Android apps separately?

Usually yes for the initial build, often saving 25-35% by sharing one codebase across platforms, but a CFO should confirm the specific feature set doesn't rely on deep hardware or platform APIs where cross-platform performance trade-offs erase the savings.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding whether to launch with a minimum viable product) How much does launching with an MVP reduce mobile app development cost compared to a full-featured build?

An MVP scoped to core screens and one platform typically costs 50-70% less than a full-featured launch, and the real value isn't just the lower number — it's validating demand before committing the larger budget to features nobody's confirmed users want.
