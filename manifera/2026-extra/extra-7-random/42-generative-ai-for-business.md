Most companies exploring generative AI for business prioritize use cases based on which capability seems most technically impressive, rather than which use case actually delivers the most business value — a prioritization inversion that routinely leads to genuinely valuable, if less flashy, applications getting deprioritized behind impressive-seeming ones that never justify their investment.

**The Pain:** A CEO exploring generative AI for business is naturally drawn toward use cases that showcase the technology's most visibly impressive capabilities — a sophisticated conversational interface, an eye-catching content-generation feature — because these demonstrate AI's capability clearly and make for a compelling internal pitch, while the specific question of which use case delivers the most actual business value, measured in cost savings, revenue impact, or genuine operational improvement, gets less rigorous scrutiny than the impressiveness question.

**The Agitation:** A CEO who prioritizes generative AI initiatives by technical impressiveness rather than business value routinely invests in flashy applications that struggle to demonstrate genuine ROI once the initial novelty wears off, while quieter, less visually impressive use cases — automating a genuinely tedious internal process, improving the accuracy of an existing analytical task — that would have delivered clear, measurable value get deprioritized because they don't generate the same internal excitement during initial planning.

## Prioritizing by Value, Not Visibility

Generative AI for business should be prioritized using the same rigorous value-assessment discipline a CEO would apply to any other significant investment — quantified expected value against quantified cost — rather than a softer, less rigorous assessment of which use case seems most technically exciting.

The practical framework starts with cataloging candidate use cases against two dimensions independently: the genuine business value a successful implementation would deliver, quantified as specifically as possible in terms of cost savings, time saved, revenue impact, or risk reduction; and the genuine implementation difficulty, including the specific engineering effort covered elsewhere in the context of moving from demo to production, not just the difficulty of an initial proof of concept. A CEO who insists on quantifying both dimensions explicitly, for every candidate use case, converts what's often an implicit, excitement-driven prioritization into an actual value-based ranking.

The use cases that typically rank highest under honest value-based prioritization are often unglamorous by the standards of internal excitement — automating a specific, well-defined, currently manual and tedious task that consumes meaningful staff time; improving the speed or consistency of an existing analytical or classification process that already has a clear, measurable baseline to improve against; or reducing errors in a process where errors carry a quantifiable cost. These use cases tend to have clearer, more measurable value and lower implementation risk than ambitious, novel customer-facing applications, precisely because they're narrower and better-defined.

The use cases that generate the most internal excitement — sophisticated new customer-facing AI features, ambitious automation of complex, judgment-heavy processes — often carry both higher implementation difficulty and less certain value, because their value depends on genuine adoption and genuine quality at a bar that's harder to hit reliably, and a CEO evaluating them honestly against the same value-and-difficulty framework often finds their actual expected value, discounted for implementation risk, is lower than the initial excitement suggested.

A CEO who runs this honest, explicit prioritization exercise before committing budget to specific generative AI initiatives ends up allocating investment toward the use cases genuinely most likely to deliver measurable return, rather than the use cases most likely to generate an impressive internal demo — and the difference in realized value between these two prioritization approaches, at scale across a portfolio of AI initiatives, tends to be substantial.
