Ask a team to describe their software development process and they'll recite the same words every team recites — sprints, standups, retros, code review. Watch how the team actually works for a week and the described process and the real one are often only loosely related.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering inherited or built a team that says it follows a standard agile software development process — two-week sprints, daily standups, sprint planning, retrospectives — and on paper, the process looks textbook. In practice, sprint commitments are missed more often than they're hit, standups run long without resolving anything, and retrospectives produce the same action items meeting after meeting because nothing from the last one actually got implemented.

**The Agitation:** A software development process that exists on a wiki page but not in daily practice is worse than having no formal process at all, because it creates the illusion of predictability that leadership plans around — a roadmap commitment made assuming sprint estimates are reliable, when in practice they never have been, sets up a very public failure to deliver that damages the team's credibility far more than an honest "we don't have a formal process yet" would have.

## Where the Gap Between Process and Practice Actually Lives

The gap between a documented software development process and the reality of how a team works almost always concentrates in a small number of specific places, and a VP of Engineering can find them by checking specific, verifiable signals rather than asking the team to self-assess.

The first gap is in sprint planning accuracy — whether story point estimates are calibrated against the team's own historical velocity, or asserted from a gut feeling that's never actually checked against outcomes. A team with a real process tracks estimate accuracy over time and adjusts; a team with process theater estimates the same way every sprint regardless of how consistently wrong those estimates have been.

The second gap is in what code review actually catches. A documented process that includes "all code is reviewed before merge" sounds rigorous until you check whether reviews are substantive — genuine questions, requested changes, pushback on approach — or a rubber stamp that approves within two minutes regardless of change size. The artifact that reveals the truth is the pull request comment history itself, not the policy document describing what's supposed to happen.

The third gap is in whether retrospectives actually produce change. A team with a functioning process treats retro action items as real commitments, tracked and revisited at the next retro to confirm they happened. A team with process theater generates the same list of complaints every two weeks because nothing from the list ever gets addressed — the retrospective becomes a ritual venting session rather than an improvement mechanism.

The fourth gap, and the one that most directly explains missed sprint commitments, is whether the definition of done is genuinely enforced or quietly negotiated away under deadline pressure. A team with a real process has a consistent bar — tested, documented, reviewed — that a ticket has to clear before it's called complete. A team with process theater has a definition of done that shrinks whenever the sprint is running behind, which produces the appearance of hitting commitments while actually accumulating hidden, unfinished work that surfaces later as a bug or a surprise.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Copenhagen Insurtech's Process Theater

Forsikringsteknik Danmark A/S, a Copenhagen-based insurtech, had a documented agile process that looked exemplary on paper, but sprint commitments were missed roughly 60% of the time, and the VP of Engineering had no clear explanation why, since the team was clearly working hard and the process documentation looked correct.

Manifera's audit found the definition of done had quietly eroded — "tested" had come to mean "the developer clicked through it once," not automated coverage — and retro action items from the prior six months had a near-zero implementation rate. Manifera reset the definition of done to a genuinely enforced standard and instituted retro follow-through tracking. Sprint commitment accuracy improved from roughly 40% to over 85% within three sprints.

> *"Our process looked perfect in the wiki. It just wasn't the process we were actually running, and nobody had checked the difference until someone finally looked at the actual pull requests instead of the policy document."*
> — **VP of Engineering, Forsikringsteknik Danmark A/S, Denmark**
