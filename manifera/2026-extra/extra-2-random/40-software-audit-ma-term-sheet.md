The financial model behind most acquisition term sheets assumes the target's codebase is an asset — a technical audit exists specifically to find out whether that assumption is true.

**The Pain:** A CFO leading acquisition diligence on a promising software target has a clean set of financials, a compelling growth story, and a term sheet ready to sign, but no independent technical assessment of the codebase underpinning the entire revenue model — the assumption has simply been that "the product works, so the code must be fine."

**The Agitation:** Technical due diligence findings — undocumented architecture, unmanaged technical debt, unresolved IP assignment gaps, or a codebase that can't scale to the acquirer's growth projections — surface in an estimated 60-70% of software M&A technical audits, and when found post-signature rather than pre-signature, they routinely force a valuation renegotiation of 10-20% or an escrow holdback that can represent millions of euros the CFO didn't budget for in the deal structure.

## The Architectural Mandate

A technical software audit before an M&A term sheet is signed is the codebase equivalent of a structural survey before buying a building — it exists to answer questions the financial statements cannot: is the technology actually capable of supporting the growth the deal thesis assumes, does the company legally and cleanly own the intellectual property it's selling, and what liabilities are embedded in the architecture that won't show up until the acquirer tries to scale, integrate, or extend the product post-close.

The audit's financial value comes from converting invisible technical risk into a quantifiable, negotiable deal term before signature, when negotiating leverage still exists — not after close, when the buyer owns the problem outright with no recourse beyond whatever warranties and indemnities happened to be drafted into the purchase agreement. A rigorous audit examines several categories of risk with direct financial translation: technical debt (what does remediation cost, and does it change the post-close product roadmap timeline), architecture scalability (can the system handle the acquirer's projected growth without a costly rebuild), IP assignment chain of title (does the target actually own the code it's selling, all the way down to individual contributor assignment), security posture (what breach or compliance liability is embedded and unaddressed), and dependency risk (how much of the system relies on unsupported, deprecated, or single-vendor components that create operational fragility).

Custom software development companies being evaluated as acquisition targets vary enormously in how disciplined their engineering governance has been, and a target's financial performance is a poor proxy for its technical health — a company can generate strong revenue on top of a fragile, undocumented, or IP-compromised codebase for years before the underlying risk becomes financially visible, typically right after an acquirer starts trying to build on top of it. The custom software development market includes many otherwise-attractive targets carrying exactly this kind of latent technical liability, invisible in a standard financial and legal due diligence process that doesn't include a hands-on code and architecture review.

The mandate for a CFO leading any acquisition involving meaningful software assets is to commission an independent technical audit as a standard, non-negotiable component of due diligence, timed early enough in the process that findings can still inform term sheet negotiation, valuation adjustment, or deal structure — not discovered during post-close integration planning when the only remaining options are expensive remediation or an indemnity claim against a seller who may no longer be fully solvent or cooperative.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO leading acquisition diligence on a software target) What does a technical M&A audit actually examine?

A thorough audit covers technical debt and remediation cost, architecture scalability against the acquirer's growth projections, IP assignment chain of title, security posture, and dependency risk from unsupported or single-vendor components. Each category is translated into a quantified financial risk, not just a technical description.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding whether the audit timeline fits a compressed deal process) How long does a technical audit take, and will it fit our deal timeline?

A focused audit for a mid-sized target typically takes one to three weeks depending on codebase size and complexity, and can be structured to run in parallel with financial and legal diligence rather than extending the overall timeline.

### (Scenario: CFO wondering how common technical issues are in acquisition targets) How often do technical audits actually find something significant?

Findings of real financial consequence, meaning issues that would reasonably affect valuation or deal structure, surface in an estimated 60-70% of technical audits on software acquisition targets, making the audit a high-value step rather than a formality.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to justify the audit cost to deal stakeholders) Is the cost of a technical audit justified for a smaller acquisition?

Yes, proportionally. Audit cost typically scales with codebase size and complexity, and even for smaller deals, the cost represents a small fraction of the potential valuation impact of an undiscovered technical liability, making it justified across most deal sizes involving meaningful software assets.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting to know what happens if the audit finds a problem) What do we actually do if the audit finds a serious technical issue?

Findings become negotiating leverage: they can support a valuation adjustment, a specific escrow holdback tied to remediation, or in rare cases a decision to walk away from the deal entirely. The key value is having the information before signature, when all of these options are still available.
