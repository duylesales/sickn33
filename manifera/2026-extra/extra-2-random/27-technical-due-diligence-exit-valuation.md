The acquirer's technical due-diligence team doesn't care how fast you shipped features for the last three years — they care about exactly the things nobody was tracking while you were shipping fast, and they will find them.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a company mid-acquisition just got the technical due-diligence report from the buyer's engineering team: undocumented IP ownership on code written by a rotating cast of offshore contractors, no clean chain of custody on who wrote what, a security posture that's never been formally audited, and a bus factor of one on the core revenue system. The deal is still open, but the number on the table just became negotiable.

**The Agitation:** Technical debt discovered during due diligence doesn't get fixed before closing, it gets priced into the deal, and it's priced against the seller. Acquirers routinely apply a 10-25% valuation discount, or an escrow holdback of similar size, when due diligence surfaces IP ownership gaps or undocumented architecture — on a €15M exit, that's €1.5M-€3.75M that a VP of Engineering could have protected months earlier by getting the technical house in order before the data room opened, not after.

## The Architectural Mandate

Technical due diligence evaluates four things an acquirer's engineering team will check methodically, and a VP of Engineering preparing for an exit needs to treat each one as a pre-closing deliverable, not a hope.

The first is IP chain of custody: every line of production code needs a clear, documented ownership trail back to an individual or entity with an enforceable IP assignment. This is where offshore engagements structured as informal staff augmentation or freelance arrangements without individual-engineer NDAs and assignment clauses become existential — an acquirer's counsel will flag any code written by a contractor without airtight IP transfer as a liability, and in the worst case, as a reason to walk. A custom software development company partner used throughout the company's history needs to have had this structure from day one, because retroactively fixing IP assignment on old code is difficult, expensive, and sometimes impossible if the original contractor can't be located.

The second is architecture and technical debt legibility. A due-diligence team will ask for architecture diagrams, dependency maps, and a technical debt inventory with severity ratings. A company that can produce this in a day looks like a well-run engineering organization; a company that needs three weeks to reverse-engineer its own system looks like a company hiding something, even when it isn't — and due-diligence timelines don't wait for that reconstruction, they just discount the price while it happens.

The third is security and compliance posture, evaluated against whatever regulatory frame applies — GDPR for European operations, SOC 2 or ISO 27001 as an implicit baseline for enterprise-serving SaaS. Gaps here aren't just a valuation discount, they're a potential deal-breaker if the acquirer's own compliance obligations would be inherited alongside an unaudited codebase.

The fourth is bus factor and team continuity risk specifically framed for an acquisition: will the engineering capability the acquirer is paying for still exist six months after close, or does critical knowledge live in one or two people who may not survive an acquisition-driven reorg? Acquirers price this risk explicitly, sometimes through earnout structures that make payment contingent on key engineers staying — which only works in the seller's favor if the underlying knowledge was already distributed before the deal, not concentrated in people whose retention isn't guaranteed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering anticipating an acquisition conversation) How early should we start preparing for technical due diligence?

Ideally six to twelve months before any serious buyer conversation, since IP remediation and bus-factor redistribution take real time and can't be compressed into a deal timeline without raising more flags than they resolve. Even a preliminary interest signal is a reasonable trigger to start.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about historical contractor code) What happens if we can't locate a former contractor to get proper IP assignment?

In some cases the affected module needs to be rewritten rather than merely re-documented, since a buyer's counsel will treat unassignable IP as a real liability, not a technicality. This is expensive after the fact, which is why individual-engineer IP assignment from day one is the far cheaper path.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering estimating the financial stakes) How much can a technical diligence finding actually move the deal price?

Acquirers commonly apply a 10-25% valuation discount or an equivalent escrow holdback when diligence surfaces material IP or architecture risk, which on a mid-market exit can represent several million euros depending on deal size.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering concerned about key-person dependency) Does bus factor actually affect deal terms, or is it just a technical concern?

It affects deal terms directly, often through earnout or retention-contingent payment structures when an acquirer believes critical knowledge is concentrated in people who might leave. Distributing that knowledge before diligence reduces the acquirer's leverage to impose those structures.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering wanting a confidential pre-diligence check) Can Manifera run a readiness audit without the wider organization knowing an exit is being considered?

Yes, a technical readiness audit can be scoped and run discreetly as a standard engineering-quality engagement, without signaling to the broader team that an acquisition conversation is underway.
