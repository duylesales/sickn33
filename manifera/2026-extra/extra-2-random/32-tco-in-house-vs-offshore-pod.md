Most build-vs-buy spreadsheets comparing in-house hiring to offshore development compare a fully-loaded in-house salary to a bare offshore hourly rate — which is not a comparison, it's a mistake dressed up as analysis.

**The Pain:** A CFO evaluating whether to hire three senior engineers in-house or engage an offshore dedicated pod has been handed two numbers by two different stakeholders: an in-house recruiter's salary benchmark and a vendor's day rate. Neither number includes the same cost categories, and the board wants a recommendation by next week.

**The Agitation:** Comparing an unloaded offshore rate to a loaded in-house salary routinely understates the true cost gap by 35-45%, and a CFO who presents that flawed comparison to the board risks approving a hiring plan that turns out to cost €120,000-€180,000 more per year than modeled once recruiting fees, benefits, tooling, office overhead, and ramp-time-to-productivity are honestly counted on both sides.

## The Architectural Mandate

A defensible total-cost-of-ownership model requires pricing every cost category identically on both sides of the comparison, and most models fail at exactly this step. In-house hiring carries direct costs that never appear on a job offer: recruiting fees (typically 15-25% of first-year salary for a senior engineer), employer social contributions, benefits, equipment, office allocation, management overhead, and the six-to-nine month ramp period during which a new hire is paid full salary while producing partial output. None of this is optional cost — it is structural cost that a headline salary figure conceals.

Offshore dedicated pod pricing needs the same honest accounting in the other direction. A day rate that looks attractive on a vendor comparison sheet can hide markup layers, change-order fees for scope drift, and — critically — the management overhead a client organization absorbs when the vendor doesn't provide its own technical leadership. Custom software development pricing that excludes a documented ramp timeline, a named tech lead, and a fixed scope-change process isn't cheaper, it's just cheaper to quote.

The mandate for a CFO building this model is a like-for-like cost stack: base compensation or day rate, management overhead, ramp-to-productivity time, attrition risk, and IP/legal protection cost, applied consistently across both options. When priced this way, the gap between in-house and a well-structured offshore dedicated team narrows in some categories (management overhead is lower with a self-managing pod) and widens in others (a pod's blended day rate for equivalent seniority is typically 45-60% below Dutch or German in-house loaded cost). The honest model doesn't pre-determine the answer — it makes the answer defensible in front of a board that will ask hard questions either way.

Attrition risk deserves its own line item and is almost always omitted. Senior engineering attrition in competitive EU markets runs 15-20% annually, and every departure resets the ramp clock and adds a recruiting-fee cost that a TCO model built once at the start of the year will never capture unless it's built as a multi-year projection, not a single hiring-event snapshot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO comparing an in-house hiring plan against a vendor quote) What cost categories does an in-house salary benchmark usually leave out?

Recruiting fees, employer social contributions, benefits, equipment, office overhead, management time, and six-to-nine months of ramp-to-productivity are typically excluded from a headline salary figure. A defensible TCO model prices all of these explicitly rather than treating the base salary as the full cost.

### (Scenario: CFO reviewing a vendor's day-rate quote before signing) What hidden costs should we look for in an offshore vendor's day rate?

Watch for change-order markup on scope drift, lack of a documented ramp timeline, and whether the client is expected to provide technical management the vendor should be supplying. A rate that excludes these is not lower cost, it's an incomplete quote.

### (Scenario: CFO building a multi-year cost projection for the board) Why does attrition risk matter for a total-cost-of-ownership model?

Senior engineering attrition in competitive EU markets runs 15-20% annually, and every departure resets the ramp clock and triggers new recruiting costs. A one-time hiring-cost snapshot misses this compounding effect entirely, while a multi-year TCO model captures it.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding between full in-house build and full offshore pod) Is a hybrid model ever more cost-effective than choosing one option entirely?

Often, yes. Retaining a small in-house team for product strategy and IP-sensitive decisions while using a dedicated offshore pod for execution-heavy work frequently produces a lower blended TCO than either extreme, while preserving institutional knowledge where it matters most.

### (Scenario: CFO asked by the board to justify the offshore pricing model) How do we know a dedicated pod's rate isn't artificially cheap in a way that creates delivery risk later?

Ask for the itemized cost stack: base rate, ramp timeline, management structure, and continuity terms. A vendor unwilling to itemize this is a signal the low headline rate is compensating for costs it plans to pass to you later as change orders or delivery delays.
