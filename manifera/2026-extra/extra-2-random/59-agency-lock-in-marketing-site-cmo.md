The quote to add a single new landing-page template just came back at €18,000 and six weeks, from the only agency that has ever touched the codebase, and everyone in the room quietly understands this is less a quote than a ransom note.

**The Pain:** A CMO inherited a marketing site built years ago by an agency that never documented the codebase, never handed over clean repository access, and built the CMS integration with enough custom, undocumented logic that no other vendor will confidently quote work on it. Every change, however small, routes through the same agency, at whatever price and timeline they choose to offer, because switching means rebuilding institutional knowledge that was never written down in the first place.

**The Agitation:** Vendor lock-in on a marketing site isn't a mild inconvenience — it's a recurring tax on every single change, and a CMO stuck with a single-vendor hostage codebase typically pays 40-70% above competitive market rates for routine development work, which on a marketing site requiring even modest ongoing development can mean €80,000-€150,000 a year in vendor premium that a properly structured, portable codebase would never have required in the first place.

## The Architectural Mandate

Lock-in isn't a contract problem — contracts can always be terminated. It's an architecture and knowledge-transfer problem, and it's created deliberately or carelessly at build time, long before the CMO who inherits it ever gets a vote. The mandate is code and infrastructure ownership structured explicitly around portability from day one: the client organization owns the source code repository outright (not the agency), hosting and infrastructure accounts are registered under the client's name, and every third-party integration is documented with credentials and configuration the client controls directly, not buried in an agency's internal password manager.

The second mandate is architectural legibility — a codebase built on widely-adopted frameworks and conventional patterns that any competent development team can read and safely modify, rather than bespoke, undocumented custom logic that only the original builders understand. This doesn't mean sacrificing sophistication; it means the sophistication is documented and follows patterns a new team can onboard against, verified through a technical audit any vendor should be able to pass before being trusted with the account.

The third mandate is a mandatory technical handover package as a contractual deliverable, not a courtesy: architecture documentation, environment setup instructions, a record of every third-party service and API key, and a clean git history with meaningful commit messages. Any custom software development company worth the name should be willing to build this as a matter of course, because a vendor confident in the quality of their own work has no reason to make it illegible to the next team.

The fourth mandate is a periodic third-party code audit — an independent technical review, run annually or with any major vendor transition, that assesses whether the current codebase remains portable, whether documentation has kept pace with changes, and whether the organization could, in practice, switch vendors within a reasonable timeline if it needed to. Treating portability as something to verify periodically, rather than something assumed to be true because it was true once, is what actually prevents lock-in from quietly re-accumulating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CMO defending the martech budget at a QBR) How do we know if we're actually locked into our current agency or just have a good working relationship?

The clearest test is asking a competing vendor to quote a piece of work on your existing codebase. If every competitor declines or demands an expensive discovery phase first, that's lock-in, not loyalty, regardless of how the relationship feels day to day.

### (Scenario: CMO worried about the risk of switching vendors) Isn't switching vendors on a lock-in codebase riskier than just staying with the current one?

It can be in the short term, which is exactly why an independent technical audit before any switch matters, it identifies what needs documenting or untangling first, so the transition is planned rather than a scramble.

### (Scenario: CMO negotiating a new contract and wanting to prevent future lock-in) What should be in a contract to prevent lock-in from happening again?

Explicit clauses assigning source code and infrastructure ownership to your organization from day one, a mandatory technical documentation deliverable, and hosting/third-party accounts registered under your name rather than the vendor's.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to estimate the financial impact of a lock-in situation) How much is vendor lock-in actually costing us if we've never had competitive bids?

Without a comparison point it's hard to know precisely, but lock-in premiums of 40-70% above competitive market rates are common, meaning a routine audit that enables competitive bidding often pays for itself within the first project.

### (Scenario: CMO planning a vendor transition after years with one agency) How long does it typically take to untangle and document a previously opaque codebase?

For a typical marketing site, a full audit, documentation, and account-migration process usually takes four to eight weeks, depending on how much custom, undocumented logic the original build accumulated.
