The investor's technical advisor asked for a current architecture diagram, a summary of the security posture, and the test coverage percentage — three questions that took the engineering team four days to answer, not because the answers were bad, but because nobody had ever written any of it down.

**The Pain:** A CEO is raising a funding round with strong product traction and revenue growth, and the term sheet is contingent on technical due diligence — a standard process where an investor's technical advisor reviews the codebase, architecture, security posture, and engineering practices. The engineering team is genuinely competent and the system works well in production, but nothing about the architecture, the security controls, the testing approach, or the technical roadmap has ever been documented in a form presentable to an outside reviewer, because documentation has always lost the priority fight against shipping features.

**The Agitation:** Technical due diligence delays are one of the most common, most avoidable ways a fundraising timeline slips, because the underlying engineering quality is frequently fine — the actual problem is the absence of documentation that lets a time-constrained external reviewer verify that quality efficiently. An investor's technical advisor working through a data room with no architecture documentation, no security summary, and no clear answer to basic questions about scalability or technical risk either has to spend significantly more time extracting that information through calls and follow-ups, or forms an impression of engineering immaturity that has nothing to do with the actual quality of the system, purely because nothing was prepared for them to review.

## The Technical Due Diligence Readiness Mandate

The first mandate is producing a current, accurate architecture diagram and written system overview before diligence begins, not assembled reactively once a data room request arrives — this single document, done well, answers a disproportionate share of the questions a technical reviewer will ask in the first pass.

The second mandate is a documented security posture summary — how data is encrypted, how access is controlled, what security testing has been performed, what compliance certifications exist or are in progress — since security questions are near-universal in technical diligence and a company with genuinely solid practices but no summary of them looks weaker on paper than one with a clear, if less impressive, documented posture.

The third mandate is quantified engineering health metrics prepared in advance — test coverage, deployment frequency, incident history and resolution time, key technical debt areas and remediation plans — presented honestly rather than either hidden or oversold, since an experienced technical reviewer trusts a company that's transparent about known limitations far more than one that presents a suspiciously flawless picture.

The fourth mandate is running an internal mock diligence review well before the actual fundraising process starts, using the same kind of questions a real technical advisor would ask, specifically to surface documentation gaps and genuine technical risks while there's still time to address or at least prepare a thoughtful answer for both.
