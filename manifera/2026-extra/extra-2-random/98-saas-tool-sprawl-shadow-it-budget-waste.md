The finance team ran the first-ever comprehensive SaaS subscription audit and found forty-seven active tools, three different project management platforms running simultaneously across departments that had never coordinated, and eleven subscriptions nobody currently at the company could explain, justify, or remembered signing up for in the first place.

**The Pain:** A CFO's company has accumulated software subscriptions organically over years, as individual teams signed up for tools that solved an immediate problem — a design team's collaboration tool, a sales team's prospecting platform, an engineering team's monitoring service — often on a company card without a centralized procurement or approval process, and without anyone maintaining a comprehensive, current inventory of what's actually being paid for, by whom, and whether it's still being used. Individually, each subscription looked like a reasonable, small decision at the time; collectively, they represent an unmanaged and largely unexamined line item in the budget.

**The Agitation:** SaaS sprawl is a specific, quantifiable form of budget waste that persists because no single line item is large enough to individually attract scrutiny — a €49-a-month tool that stopped being used eight months ago doesn't trigger a review the way a major line item would, and the cumulative effect of dozens of these small, unexamined subscriptions routinely adds up to a genuinely material annual cost. Beyond the direct financial waste, unmanaged shadow IT also represents a security and data governance risk, since tools adopted outside a formal procurement process often haven't gone through any security review and may hold company or customer data nobody in IT or security is even aware exists in that system.

## The SaaS Governance Mandate

The first mandate is a comprehensive, current inventory of every active software subscription across the company, built through a combination of expense report analysis, credit card statement review, and single sign-on log review, since relying purely on what department heads remember paying for reliably misses a meaningful share of the actual sprawl.

The second mandate is usage verification for every identified subscription, not just cost verification — a tool that's being paid for and actively used by a genuinely engaged team is a different situation than a tool that's being paid for and hasn't had a login in six months, and the audit needs to distinguish between the two rather than treating cost alone as the decision criterion.

The third mandate is consolidation of genuinely redundant tools serving the same function across different departments — multiple project management platforms, multiple design collaboration tools, multiple analytics platforms — since redundancy across departments that never coordinated their tool selection is one of the most common and highest-value consolidation opportunities a SaaS audit typically finds.

The fourth mandate is an ongoing procurement governance process going forward, not just a one-time cleanup, since sprawl reaccumulates naturally without a lightweight but real approval and inventory-tracking process for any new subscription a team wants to adopt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO suspecting software spend has grown faster than justified) How do we know if our company actually has meaningful SaaS sprawl worth auditing?

Compare software subscription spend growth against headcount and revenue growth over the past two to three years — spend growing meaningfully faster than either is a strong indicator of accumulated sprawl worth a comprehensive audit.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to build a complete inventory of current subscriptions) How do we build a truly comprehensive inventory when department heads may not remember everything their teams pay for?

Combine expense report analysis, corporate credit card statement review, and single sign-on log review, since relying purely on what people remember reliably misses a meaningful share of actual subscriptions, particularly ones purchased on individual cards.

### (Scenario: CFO trying to decide which tools to eliminate) Should we eliminate a subscription just because it's expensive, or is usage the better criterion?

Usage is the better primary criterion — a genuinely engaged, actively used tool serving a real team need is a different situation than an unused subscription regardless of cost, and the audit should distinguish clearly between the two before making elimination decisions.
