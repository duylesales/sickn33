The CMO has known for four months that step three of the signup flow loses 22% of new users to a form validation bug that only appears on Safari. Engineering has known too. It's still not fixed, because it's never anyone's sprint priority.

**The Pain:** A CMO has spent significant budget driving qualified traffic to a signup flow, and the funnel analytics clearly show a steep drop at a specific step — a slow-loading verification screen, a confusing form field, a broken validation state on one browser — that engineering has acknowledged but never actually scheduled, because it competes against roadmap features with a product owner and a deadline, while onboarding conversion has no dedicated owner on the engineering side at all.

**The Agitation:** Every month the onboarding flow stays broken, the CMO is paying full acquisition cost for traffic that converts at a needlessly suppressed rate, which means the effective cost per activated user is quietly inflated by whatever percentage the broken flow is losing. A five-point conversion drop at the top of the funnel doesn't show up as an engineering bug in anyone's dashboard — it shows up as a marketing efficiency problem the CMO gets asked about in the next board meeting, with no clean way to point at the actual root cause sitting in a backlog nobody prioritized.

## The Onboarding-as-Revenue-Infrastructure Mandate

The first mandate is treating the onboarding funnel as revenue infrastructure with the same engineering priority as a payment flow, not as a design artifact that ships once and gets left alone. Every percentage point of conversion lost at signup is a direct, calculable multiplier on acquisition spend, and framing onboarding fixes in those terms — not as a "nice to have" UX polish item — is what gets them scheduled against feature work.

The second mandate is instrumenting the funnel with step-by-step conversion tracking granular enough to identify exactly where users drop, on which browser, on which device, rather than relying on aggregate signup-to-activation numbers that hide the specific failure point inside a vague overall percentage.

The third mandate is a dedicated, ring-fenced engineering capacity for conversion-critical paths — onboarding, checkout, activation — separate from the roadmap-feature backlog, so a Safari validation bug isn't competing against a new dashboard feature for the same sprint slot and consistently losing.

The fourth mandate is closing the loop between marketing and engineering with a shared, jointly-owned metric — activated users per marketing dollar spent — so a broken onboarding step is visible to both teams as the same problem, rather than a marketing metric on one side and an unprioritized bug on the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CMO trying to get engineering to prioritize a known onboarding bug) Why does a known conversion-killing bug so often stay unfixed for months?

Because it typically has no dedicated owner on the engineering side and competes against roadmap features that have both a product owner and a deadline — without a framework that treats conversion fixes as revenue-critical, they consistently lose that priority contest.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to quantify the cost of a funnel leak) How do we calculate the real cost of a broken onboarding step?

Multiply the conversion drop percentage at the affected step by total qualified traffic reaching that step, then apply your cost-per-acquisition — the result is the effective acquisition spend being wasted on traffic that a fixed funnel would have converted.

### (Scenario: CMO wondering whether a separate conversion team is really necessary) Isn't a separate conversion-focused engineering pod redundant if we already have a product engineering team?

Not if the existing team's roadmap consistently deprioritizes conversion fixes, which is the pattern that causes the leak in the first place. A ring-fenced pod exists specifically to prevent conversion-critical work from losing to feature work indefinitely.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to build a shared metric with engineering) What's a good shared metric to align marketing and engineering priorities on the funnel?

Activated users per marketing dollar spent, tracked jointly, makes a broken funnel step visible to engineering in the same terms marketing already uses to justify budget, closing the gap between "a bug" and "a revenue problem."

### (Scenario: CMO trying to estimate how quickly a dedicated pod could fix known issues) How fast can a dedicated pod typically resolve a known, long-standing conversion bug?

Often within the first sprint, since the fix itself is frequently small — the delay was prioritization, not complexity, which is exactly what dedicated, ring-fenced capacity is designed to solve.
