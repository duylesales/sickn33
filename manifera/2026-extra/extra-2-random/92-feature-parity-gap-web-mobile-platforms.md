The marketing email announced the new reporting dashboard to the entire customer base. Support spent the next three days explaining, one ticket at a time, that the feature everyone had just been told about didn't actually exist yet in the mobile app, and nobody had told marketing that before the campaign went out.

**The Pain:** A CMO's company builds and ships features on the web platform first, with mobile app parity treated as a follow-up that happens "eventually," on a timeline the mobile team sets independently based on its own backlog priorities rather than any coordination with when marketing plans to announce the feature broadly. Over time, a growing list of features exists on one platform but not the other, and customers — a meaningful share of whom are mobile-primary — experience the product as inconsistent and, from a marketing perspective, every feature announcement now carries hidden risk of promoting something a significant chunk of the customer base literally cannot access yet.

**The Agitation:** Platform parity gaps are a specific, recurring source of customer frustration and support burden that's largely invisible to leadership until it's pointed out explicitly, because each individual gap looks like a minor, temporary state rather than a systemic pattern — but a customer who discovers a promoted feature doesn't work on their primary platform experiences that as a broken promise, regardless of the internal explanation that mobile "is coming soon." A CMO who keeps promoting features without visibility into actual cross-platform availability is running a marketing program with a structural credibility risk baked into every campaign.

## The Cross-Platform Parity Governance Mandate

The first mandate is a shared, visible feature-parity tracker that both engineering and marketing actually reference — a single source of truth showing exactly which features exist on which platform, so a marketing campaign is never planned around a feature's web availability without someone checking mobile status first.

The second mandate is an explicit product decision, made deliberately rather than by default, about which features genuinely need day-one parity across platforms versus which can reasonably launch platform-by-platform with a clear, publicly communicated timeline — the problem isn't sequential rollout itself, it's sequential rollout that isn't planned or communicated.

The third mandate is involving marketing in feature-launch sequencing decisions before a campaign is built, not after, so that marketing can either wait for parity, explicitly scope a campaign to the platform where the feature actually exists, or set customer expectations honestly about a phased rollout — any of which is better than promoting a feature broadly and letting customers discover the gap themselves.

The fourth mandate is prioritizing parity work for any feature that's about to be, or already has been, prominently marketed, since a feature the business is actively promoting is precisely the one where a platform gap does the most reputational damage, deserving priority over parity gaps in less visible parts of the product.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CMO who has promoted a feature that doesn't yet exist on a major platform) How do we avoid promoting a feature that isn't actually available on every major platform yet?

Establish a shared feature-parity tracker that marketing checks before building any feature-based campaign, and require a sign-off checkpoint confirming platform availability before the campaign launches.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to understand whether sequential platform rollout is inherently a problem) Is it always wrong to launch a feature on one platform before another?

No, sequential rollout is often reasonable — the problem is sequential rollout that isn't a deliberate decision or isn't communicated honestly, versus a planned rollout where marketing scopes the campaign appropriately or sets clear expectations about timing.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to prioritize parity work with engineering) How do we get engineering to prioritize closing a specific platform-parity gap?

Connect the request explicitly to marketing visibility — a feature that's about to be, or already has been, prominently promoted deserves priority over parity gaps in less customer-visible parts of the product, and framing it that way helps it compete for engineering priority.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to recover from an announcement that outpaced actual availability) What should we do if we've already promoted a feature that isn't available everywhere yet?

Communicate the gap honestly and proactively rather than waiting for customers to discover and complain about it, and prioritize closing the parity gap for that specific feature as quickly as possible given the visibility it already has.

### (Scenario: CMO trying to estimate the cost of building better cross-platform coordination) What does it cost to build the coordination process and close a high-visibility parity gap?

The coordination process itself is largely a process investment with modest direct cost, while closing a specific, actively-marketed parity gap typically costs €15,000-€35,000 depending on the feature's complexity.
