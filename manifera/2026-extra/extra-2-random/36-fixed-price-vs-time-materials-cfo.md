CFOs default to fixed-price contracts because the number on the page looks like certainty — and then watch that certainty evaporate the moment the first change order lands.

**The Pain:** A CFO signs a fixed-price contract for a custom platform build specifically because it promises budget predictability, only to receive a change-order request in month three that adds 40% to the original price because a requirement "wasn't in scope" — a requirement that any experienced engineering team would have flagged as obviously necessary during discovery.

**The Agitation:** Fixed-price contracts with under-scoped discovery phases routinely accumulate change-order costs of 25-50% above the original quote, meaning a project quoted at €300,000 frequently lands at €400,000-€450,000 once the change orders are counted — with the CFO having reported the original €300,000 figure to the board as the budget, and now needing to explain a variance that undermines confidence in every future estimate from the same function.

## The Architectural Mandate

The choice between fixed-price and time-and-materials contracts is not a choice between risk and no risk — it's a choice about which party absorbs scope uncertainty, and most CFOs pick the model without understanding where that risk actually lands. Fixed-price contracts transfer scope risk to the vendor only if the scope was defined precisely enough at signature to be enforceable; if it wasn't, the vendor simply recovers the risk through change orders, and the client ends up bearing the cost anyway, just relabeled and delivered later with less negotiating leverage than they had before signing.

Custom software development pricing structured as fixed-price only produces genuine budget certainty when preceded by a paid discovery phase detailed enough to produce a specification a vendor can actually be held to — user stories, acceptance criteria, technical architecture decisions, and explicit exclusions. Skipping or under-investing in discovery to save a few weeks and a modest fee is the single most common cause of fixed-price contracts inflating well beyond their quoted number, because the ambiguity that discovery would have resolved gets resolved instead through change orders, on the vendor's terms, mid-project, when the client has the least leverage to push back.

Time-and-materials contracts invert the risk allocation: the client bears scope uncertainty directly, but gains the ability to adapt as requirements evolve without a change-order negotiation for every adjustment. This model is frequently the financially superior choice for projects with genuine requirement uncertainty — new product development, unclear market fit, evolving technical architecture — precisely because the flexibility it buys is worth more than the certainty a fixed-price contract falsely promises when the scope wasn't knowable in the first place. The mandate for a CFO is matching the model to the actual uncertainty profile of the project, not defaulting to fixed-price because the invoice looks smaller in the board deck.

A hybrid structure — a fixed-price discovery phase producing a detailed specification, followed by either a fixed-price build phase against that specification or a capped time-and-materials phase with a not-to-exceed ceiling — captures the advantages of both models. It gives the CFO a real number to report after discovery, not before it, and it gives the vendor an enforceable scope boundary that makes change orders the exception rather than the default mechanism for delivering the actual requirements.

## The Economics

A fixed-price contract without a proper discovery phase is not actually fixed-price — it's a deferred time-and-materials contract with worse negotiating leverage, and the 25-50% change-order inflation that results is effectively unbudgeted spend a CFO reported to the board as controlled cost. On a €300,000 project, that's €75,000-€150,000 of variance that erodes confidence in every future estimate from the same relationship, while a properly structured discovery-then-price model, costing perhaps 5-8% of total project value upfront, routinely prevents the bulk of that overrun. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO choosing a pricing model for an upcoming project) How do we decide between fixed-price and time-and-materials for a new project?

Match the model to how well-understood the requirements are. A project with a clear, stable scope favors fixed-price after proper discovery; a project with genuine requirement uncertainty, such as new product development, is usually better served by time-and-materials with a capped ceiling.

### (Scenario: CFO who has been burned by change orders before) How do we prevent a fixed-price contract from inflating through change orders?

Invest in a paid discovery phase that produces a detailed specification before pricing the build, and require any change order above a defined threshold to go through CFO-level sign-off. This converts change orders from the default delivery mechanism back into a genuine exception.

### (Scenario: CFO reporting a project budget to the board before discovery is complete) Should we report a fixed-price quote to the board before discovery is finished?

No. A quote given before detailed discovery is a rough estimate, not a commitment, and reporting it as a firm budget sets up a variance conversation later. Report the re-quoted number after discovery as the actual budget commitment.

### (Scenario: CFO worried time-and-materials contracts offer no cost control) Does time-and-materials mean we have no control over total spend?

No, a properly structured time-and-materials contract includes a not-to-exceed ceiling and regular budget-burn reporting, giving cost control comparable to fixed-price while preserving the flexibility to adapt scope as requirements clarify.

### (Scenario: CFO evaluating whether a discovery phase is worth the extra time and fee) Is a paid discovery phase worth delaying the project start?

Yes, in almost every case. A two-to-four week discovery investment, typically 5-8% of total project value, routinely prevents change-order inflation that would otherwise cost several times that amount, while also giving the CFO a defensible number to report.
