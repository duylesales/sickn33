Every headcount request that lands on a CFO's desk assumes hiring is the only way to add engineering capacity — but the fully-loaded cost of one full-time hire could fund an entire dedicated pod, and almost nobody runs that comparison before approving the requisition.

**The Pain:** A CFO is reviewing a headcount request from engineering: three new full-time senior developers to clear a growing backlog, each requiring a six-figure salary, benefits, equipment, office overhead, and recruiting fees before they write a single line of code. The request is framed as the only option, because nobody on the finance side has a comparable model for what a dedicated team service actually costs.

**The Agitation:** The fully-loaded cost of one senior engineer in a Western European market runs €95,000-€130,000 a year once salary, benefits, payroll tax, recruiting cost, and ramp-up time are counted — and that's before the six-to-nine month period where a new hire is only partially productive. Approve three of those requisitions and the company has committed close to €350,000 in fixed annual cost before a single backlog item ships, with recruiting risk, attrition risk, and management overhead all sitting on the CFO's forecast as unpriced variables.

## The Architectural Mandate

The mandate for any CFO evaluating an engineering headcount request is to force a side-by-side comparison between full-time-equivalent hiring and dedicated team services before approving the requisition — not after the recruiting process has already consumed months and budget. This isn't an argument against hiring; it's an argument for making the comparison explicit, because the two models have fundamentally different cost structures and risk profiles that a headcount request rarely surfaces.

An FTE hire is a fixed, long-term commitment: salary, benefits, and overhead persist whether or not the workload does, and downsizing carries severance cost and morale impact. A dedicated pod under a software development outsourcing model is a variable-capacity commitment — engineers, a tech lead, and QA assigned to your roadmap for as long as the engagement requires, scalable up or down between projects without the fixed-cost tail of a full-time headcount. For a CFO managing engineering budget against unpredictable product demand, that flexibility alone is worth modeling explicitly, separate from the day-rate comparison.

The day-rate comparison itself typically favors the pod model by a wide margin once fully-loaded costs are counted honestly. A dedicated offshore pod delivers comparable or greater output at 40-60% of the fully-loaded cost of equivalent Western European FTEs, without the six-to-nine month ramp period, because a properly structured pod arrives with its delivery process, technical leadership, and code standards already established. The mandate is to stop comparing a pod's day rate against a developer's base salary — the only honest comparison is fully-loaded FTE cost, including recruiting, benefits, overhead, and ramp-time, against a pod's all-in engagement cost.

There's also a strategic dimension a CFO should weigh alongside the pure cost math: headcount is a permanent line on the org chart that survives past the project that justified it, while a pod scales with the roadmap. A director of software development managing a mixed team of core FTEs and dedicated pods gets the stability of permanent ownership over core systems and the elasticity to absorb roadmap spikes without over-hiring — a structure that materially changes how a CFO can forecast engineering spend against revenue growth rather than against headcount commitments made a year in advance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO reviewing a headcount request from engineering) How do we fairly compare the cost of a pod against hiring FTEs?

Compare fully-loaded FTE cost — salary, benefits, payroll tax, recruiting, and ramp-up time — against the pod's all-in engagement cost, not against a pod's day rate versus a base salary alone. Manifera's Amsterdam team builds this comparison as part of the initial proposal.

### (Scenario: CFO worried about losing institutional knowledge without permanent hires) Does using a pod mean we lose long-term ownership of our codebase?

No, if the engagement is structured correctly. A dedicated pod can be retained long-term just like an FTE team, with documentation and code ownership assigned to your organization from day one, giving you continuity without the fixed-headcount commitment.

### (Scenario: CFO managing a hiring freeze while facing a deadline) Can a pod be approved outside a frozen headcount budget?

Often yes, because a pod engagement is typically structured as an operating or project expense rather than a permanent headcount line, which can fall outside a board-mandated hiring freeze. This is exactly the structure that let Delacroix Assurance Digital hit a compliance deadline during a freeze.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding how much of engineering should be pods versus in-house) What's the right mix of in-house FTEs versus dedicated pods?

Core, long-term systems that require deep institutional ownership are usually best served by a stable in-house team, while roadmap spikes, new product lines, or time-boxed initiatives are well suited to pods. Most director-of-engineering-led organizations run a blended model rather than an all-or-nothing approach.

### (Scenario: CFO evaluating whether pod engineers are as capable as FTE hires) Are pod engineers lower quality than the senior FTEs we'd otherwise hire?

Not when the pod is properly structured — Manifera's pods are staffed with senior engineers and a dedicated tech lead who've delivered inside regulated European industries, and the day-rate savings come from cost-of-living differences in Vietnam, not from a lower talent bar.
