A localization manager or product lead evaluating machine translation — for client-facing regulated content, high-volume routine documentation, or general product localization — often approaches the technology with assumptions shaped by machine translation's visible progress in casual and conversational use cases, assumptions that don't fully account for the specific accuracy, cost, and legal considerations regulated localization content actually carries. Several of these assumptions deserve direct correction before they shape a localization workflow platform's build.

## Myth 1: "Machine Translation Output Can Simply Replace Professional Human Translation at Similar Quality for Client-Facing Regulated Content"

Machine translation systems have genuinely improved at producing fluent, plausible-sounding output across many language pairs, and it's reasonable to extrapolate from strong individual samples toward an assumption that machine translation can substitute for professional human translation broadly, including for regulated content like medical device manuals or legal contracts. What this underweights is the difference between fluent, plausible-sounding output and translation that holds up under the specific scrutiny regulated, client-facing content actually receives: terminology precision verified against domain-specific glossaries, legal and regulatory phrasing requiring direct human judgment, and the kind of contextual accuracy (correctly disambiguating a term with genuinely different meanings across contexts) that current machine translation approaches, however impressive at surface-level fluency, don't reliably substitute for at the level of verified accuracy regulated content actually requires.

## Myth 2: "Machine Translation Reduces Localization Cost Roughly Proportionally to Word Volume"

A localization manager reasonably expects that if machine translation can process a large volume of source text quickly, the cost savings should scale proportionally with that volume. What this underweights is that machine-translated output, particularly for anything beyond simple, low-stakes internal content, typically requires a genuine human post-editing and review pass to reach publishable quality — verifying terminology, correcting contextual mistranslations, and adjusting phrasing the machine model rendered fluently but incorrectly. This post-editing cost doesn't scale down proportionally with source volume the way raw machine-translation processing cost might, meaning the actual cost savings from machine-translation-assisted localization are often considerably more modest than a naive volume-based cost projection would suggest, particularly for content categories where terminology precision and regulatory accuracy matter most to the client's actual liability exposure.

## Myth 3: "Legal Liability for a Mistranslated Regulated Document Produced With Heavy Machine-Translation Reliance Is a Settled, Low-Risk Question"

The legal landscape around liability for a mistranslated regulated document — a medical device instruction, a pharmaceutical label, a legal contract clause — that relied heavily on machine translation with insufficient human review remains genuinely unsettled in multiple jurisdictions relevant to regulated localization, with liability allocation between the localization vendor, the client, and the machine translation provider itself frequently contested rather than resolved by a stable, low-risk legal consensus a vendor can confidently build a production workflow around without ongoing attention. A localization manager treating this as a solved, low-risk legal question, rather than an actively evolving area requiring ongoing legal monitoring specific to the vendor's actual machine translation tools and review practices, risks building meaningful commercial and contractual exposure on a legal foundation that could shift in ways affecting the vendor's actual liability for a client's mistranslated regulated content.

## Why These Myths Deserve Direct Correction Before Production Decisions

These assumptions aren't unreasonable — machine translation's genuine, visible progress naturally creates optimism about its broader applicability, and it's a reasonable instinct to explore cost and speed advantages a mature technology appears to offer. What makes regulated localization content specifically different from some other machine-translation use cases is the combination of genuinely high client and regulatory scrutiny over terminology and contextual accuracy (unlike a single impressive translated sample, regulated documentation needs to hold up to domain-specific verification), a real, non-proportional post-editing cost that limits how directly source volume translates into production savings, and a genuinely unsettled legal landscape specifically relevant to a vendor's liability exposure for its regulated-content clients.

## What This Means for Scoping a Machine-Translation-Assisted Localization Workflow Correctly

- **Position machine translation as an acceleration tool within a human-reviewed pipeline, not a wholesale replacement for professional translation**, particularly for content categories where terminology precision and regulatory accuracy matter most.
- **Budget realistic post-editing and review cost alongside machine-translation processing cost**, rather than projecting cost savings that scale proportionally with raw source volume without accounting for the genuine human verification pass most regulated content actually requires.
- **Maintain active legal monitoring specific to machine-translation liability allocation and the specific tools used in production**, treating this as an ongoing risk management responsibility rather than a settled question resolved once at the start of a client engagement.
- **Reserve machine translation for content categories where its actual strengths align well with the use case**, like high-volume internal documentation within a human-reviewed framework, rather than applying it uniformly across content categories with genuinely different accuracy and liability requirements.

## Why Client Trust Adds a Real Commercial Dimension Beyond Production Cost

A specific, additional consideration worth naming directly: beyond the production cost and legal considerations already discussed, client trust and sentiment toward machine-translation reliance specifically has become a genuinely active commercial factor for localization vendors, with regulated-content clients expressing real skepticism or contractual caution toward vendors perceived as leaning heavily on machine translation in place of qualified human translators for their most sensitive content. A vendor evaluating a machine-translation-assisted workflow benefits from weighing this commercial and trust dimension explicitly, not purely as a production cost or legal question, since a technically successful and legally sound workflow can still face real commercial headwinds if it generates client skepticism around perceived over-reliance on machine translation for content the client considers genuinely high-stakes.

This is a specific reason transparency about how and where machine translation is actually used within a specific client's project, and specifically pairing machine translation with genuine, visible human post-editing and review disclosure, tends to be a commercially safer positioning than either avoiding disclosure or overstating the technology's role, since both extremes risk a credibility or trust problem with clients that, for regulated content specifically, care genuinely and specifically about this question independent of the actual output's objective quality.
