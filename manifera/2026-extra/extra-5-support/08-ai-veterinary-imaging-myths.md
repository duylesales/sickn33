A practice owner or medical director at a veterinary group evaluating AI-assisted diagnostic imaging — automated radiograph analysis, AI-flagged abnormality detection, and triage prioritization for review — often approaches the technology with assumptions shaped by AI's visible progress in human medical imaging and other diagnostic domains, assumptions that don't fully account for the specific reliability, cost, and liability considerations veterinary diagnostic imaging carries. Several of these assumptions deserve direct correction before they shape a diagnostic workflow decision.

## Myth 1: "AI Image Analysis Can Simply Replace a Veterinarian's or Radiologist's Diagnostic Read at Similar Reliability"

AI-assisted imaging tools have genuinely improved at flagging visually apparent abnormalities in radiographs and other diagnostic imaging, and it's reasonable to extrapolate from strong performance on well-defined detection tasks toward an assumption that AI analysis can substitute broadly for a veterinarian's or a veterinary radiologist's diagnostic read. What this underweights is the difference between flagging a visually apparent pattern and producing a reliable diagnostic read across the genuine diversity of species, breeds, imaging equipment, and presentation variation a real veterinary practice actually encounters: AI models trained on a specific dataset frequently underperform on cases meaningfully different from their training distribution, and the kind of clinical judgment that integrates imaging findings with patient history, physical exam findings, and species-specific presentation nuance is not something current AI-assisted imaging tools reliably substitute for at the level of diagnostic reliability real veterinary medicine actually requires.

## Myth 2: "AI-Assisted Imaging Tools Cut Diagnostic Cost Roughly Proportionally to Volume"

A practice owner reasonably expects that if an AI tool can analyze a large volume of radiographs quickly, the cost savings should scale proportionally with that volume. What this underweights is that AI-flagged findings, particularly anything indicating a potential abnormality, still require a genuine veterinarian review and curation pass before a diagnosis is finalized and communicated to a pet owner — confirming the flagged finding, ruling out false positives, and integrating the imaging result with the broader clinical picture. This verification cost doesn't scale down proportionally with imaging volume the way raw AI-analysis cost might, meaning the actual cost savings from AI-assisted imaging are often considerably more modest than a naive volume-based cost projection would suggest, particularly for imaging categories where diagnostic accuracy carries real clinical and legal stakes for the animal's treatment outcome.

## Myth 3: "Liability for an AI-Assisted Misdiagnosis Is a Settled, Low-Risk Legal Question"

The legal and professional liability framework governing veterinary malpractice — generally rooted in state veterinary practice acts and standard-of-care principles enforced by state veterinary boards — was not written with AI-assisted diagnostic tools specifically in mind, and how liability apportions between a treating veterinarian and an AI tool's flagged (or missed) finding remains a genuinely unsettled question in most jurisdictions relevant to veterinary practice. A practice treating AI-assisted imaging as a low-risk, essentially administrative addition to the diagnostic workflow, rather than a tool that introduces real, still-evolving liability questions around how much a veterinarian can reasonably rely on an AI-flagged or AI-cleared finding, risks a genuine liability event — a missed diagnosis where an AI tool didn't flag a genuine abnormality is exactly the kind of incident that exposes a practice to real malpractice and reputational consequences, regardless of the diagnostic process's automated component.

## Why These Myths Deserve Direct Correction Before Production Decisions

These assumptions aren't unreasonable — AI-assisted imaging's genuine, visible progress in human medicine and other domains naturally creates optimism about its broader applicability, and it's a reasonable instinct to explore cost and speed advantages a maturing technology appears to offer for a diagnostic task as time-intensive as radiograph review. What makes veterinary diagnostic imaging specifically different from some other AI-assistance use cases is the combination of genuine reliability variation across the species and presentation diversity real veterinary practice encounters, a real, non-proportional veterinarian-review cost that limits how directly analysis volume translates into production savings, and a genuinely unsettled malpractice liability framework specifically relevant to how a practice can safely rely on AI-assisted findings.

## What This Means for Scoping an AI-Assisted Imaging Workflow Correctly

- **Position AI analysis as a triage and prioritization aid within a veterinarian-reviewed workflow, not a wholesale replacement for a diagnostic read**, particularly for cases involving species, presentations, or equipment meaningfully outside the tool's known training coverage.
- **Budget realistic veterinarian review and curation cost alongside AI-analysis cost**, rather than projecting cost savings that scale proportionally with raw imaging volume without accounting for the genuine confirmation pass most flagged findings actually require.
- **Maintain active legal and malpractice-liability monitoring specific to AI-assisted diagnostic tools**, treating this as an ongoing risk management responsibility rather than a settled question resolved once at the start of adoption.
- **Reserve AI analysis for imaging categories where its actual strengths align well with the use case**, like flagging cases for prioritized review within a veterinarian-confirmed framework, rather than applying it uniformly across imaging categories with genuinely different reliability and liability profiles.

## Why Client Trust Adds a Real Commercial Dimension Beyond Diagnostic Cost

A specific, additional consideration worth naming directly: beyond the diagnostic cost and liability considerations already discussed, pet-owner trust and sentiment toward AI-assisted diagnosis specifically has become a genuinely active commercial factor for veterinary practices, with a meaningful share of pet owners expressing real skepticism or concern upon learning that a diagnostic finding involved an AI tool rather than a veterinarian's direct, unassisted read. A practice evaluating an AI-assisted imaging strategy benefits from weighing this commercial and trust dimension explicitly, not purely as a diagnostic cost or legal question, since a technically reliable and legally sound imaging workflow can still face real commercial headwinds if pet owners feel their animal's diagnosis leaned too heavily on automation rather than direct veterinary judgment.

This is a specific reason transparency about how and where AI-assisted analysis is actually used within a specific practice's diagnostic workflow, paired with genuine, visible veterinarian confirmation of any AI-flagged finding communicated to a pet owner, tends to be a commercially safer positioning than either avoiding disclosure or overstating the tool's autonomy, since both extremes risk a trust problem with a client base that, for many practices, cares genuinely and specifically about this question independent of the diagnosis's objective accuracy.
