A CFO walks into a board meeting with a well-researched offshore software engineering proposal, confident the cost case is airtight — and gets stopped cold by a single question nobody on the deal team prepared for: "who's accountable if this goes wrong?"

**The Pain:** A CFO presenting an offshore software engineering budget to the board has usually done the cost analysis thoroughly — hourly rates, headcount, projected savings versus a Netherlands hire. What the board actually interrogates is rarely the cost line; it's the risk line, and a CFO who's only prepared the cost case gets sent back to redo the proposal, losing a full board cycle, typically six to eight weeks, before the engagement can even start.

**The Agitation:** A board that approves an offshore engineering budget without a satisfactory answer on governance, IP protection, and delivery accountability isn't being cautious for its own sake — it's protecting against a specific, quantifiable failure mode. Mid-market companies that proceed with an ungoverned offshore engineering engagement and later hit a serious quality or IP incident report remediation costs of €90,000-€160,000, and a board that approved the budget without asking the right questions bears direct responsibility for that gap, which makes board scrutiny of an offshore proposal considerably sharper than scrutiny of an equivalent domestic hiring decision.

## The Five Questions a European Board Actually Asks

A CFO walking into a board approval meeting for an offshore software engineering budget should expect five specific questions, not a general cost discussion, because these are the questions that determine whether the board is approving a managed risk or an open-ended one.

The first question is ownership: who architecturally owns the code once it's written — does the client hold full source-controlled ownership from day one, or does IP assignment happen only at contract milestones, leaving a gap where work-in-progress code has ambiguous ownership status. A board wants a specific, contractual answer here, not a general assurance.

The second question is accountability separation: is there a governance layer independent of the engineering team itself that verifies delivery quality and reports honestly on status, or is the team that writes the code also the sole source of "everything's on track" reporting. Boards that have seen a vendor relationship deteriorate know this distinction matters — self-reported status without independent verification is the single most common precursor to a board discovering a problem months later than it should have.

The third question is jurisdiction: under what legal framework does the contract sit, and where is dispute resolution seated. A board with EU fiduciary obligations wants to know the company isn't signing away recourse to a jurisdiction its own legal counsel can't meaningfully navigate.

The fourth question is continuity risk: what happens if the offshore team's senior engineers turn over — is institutional knowledge documented and does ownership rest with a pod structure rather than named individuals, or does the engagement's success depend entirely on specific people staying in their roles.

The fifth question, increasingly common as boards have become more sophisticated about offshore engagements specifically, is about the governance-to-execution split itself: is there a genuinely separate entity or team handling oversight, or is "governance" just a slide in the vendor's deck with no structural backing.

A CFO who can answer all five with specifics — not reassurances — gets budget approval in one meeting. A CFO who can only answer the cost question gets sent back.
