Two software companies in Amsterdam pitch the same project with nearly identical proposals, nearly identical day rates, and nearly identical portfolio slides. One delivers on schedule. The other slips four months and quietly reassigns your project's lead engineer twice. What actually separated them, and could you have seen it coming?

**The Pain:** A CTO shortlisting software companies in Amsterdam for a build is working from the same shallow signals every buyer uses — office address, client logos, a polished case study deck — none of which reliably predicts whether the specific team assigned to your project will actually deliver. Amsterdam has a dense, competitive software company market, and the marketing materials of a genuinely strong delivery partner and a mediocre one often look nearly identical from the outside.

**The Agitation:** A CTO who picks based on portfolio polish rather than delivery structure has roughly even odds of landing a partner whose actual assigned team looks nothing like the senior engineers featured in the pitch deck — a mismatch that typically surfaces six to ten weeks into the engagement, once the "senior architect" from the sales call has moved on to the next pitch and a materially less experienced team is doing the actual work, costing €30,000-€70,000 in schedule slip and rework by the time the client notices and escalates.

## The Signals That Actually Predict Delivery, Not the Ones on the Website

Amsterdam's software company market spans a wide range: boutique studios of a dozen people, mid-size consultancies with hundreds of consultants, and hybrid firms whose actual delivery happens partly or fully offshore under Amsterdam governance. None of that structural variety is visible from a homepage. What is knowable, if a CTO asks the right questions during evaluation, are four signals that correlate strongly with actual delivery risk.

The first signal is team continuity guarantees. Ask directly: is the team demonstrated in the sales process contractually the team that executes the project, and what happens if a named engineer leaves mid-engagement? Many Amsterdam software companies present their most senior people during sales and then staff delivery from a broader, more junior bench once the contract is signed — a bait-and-switch that isn't usually malicious, just structurally incentivized by how sales and delivery are organized internally. A vendor with a real answer names specific continuity commitments in the contract, not just in conversation.

The second signal is where actual engineering hours are spent versus where the company is headquartered. A software company with an Amsterdam address doesn't guarantee Amsterdam-based delivery — a significant share of Amsterdam-headquartered firms subcontract or route execution through partner networks whose quality and structure the client never sees clearly. This isn't inherently bad, but it's a material fact a CTO should know before signing, because unclear subcontracting chains are where accountability gaps hide.

The third signal is how the vendor prices scope changes. A software company confident in its estimation process defines change-order triggers clearly upfront — what counts as in-scope refinement versus a billable change. A vendor vague on this during sales is often vague on it during delivery too, and vagueness here is where budget overruns quietly accumulate over a multi-month engagement.

The fourth signal, and the most reliable single predictor, is reference-checking the specific team, not the company. Asking a shortlisted vendor's previous client "was the company good" gets a generically positive answer almost every time. Asking "was the specific team assigned to your project the same team promised during sales, and did they stay through delivery" gets a genuinely differentiating answer, because that's the exact failure mode that separates strong Amsterdam software companies from mediocre ones dressed identically in the pitch.
