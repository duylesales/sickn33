The financial model says a Vietnam-based software outsourcing engagement saves 50% against the local alternative — but the board doesn't approve financial models, it approves accountability structures, and a CFO who confuses the two loses the room.

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European company has a well-built financial case for a software outsourcing engagement in Vietnam and expects the board conversation to be about the savings — but board members with any outsourcing scar tissue will interrogate accountability, compliance, and governance long before they engage seriously with the cost model, and a CFO unprepared for that shift struggles to hold the room.

**The Agitation:** A board that doesn't get satisfactory answers on accountability rarely says no outright — it defers, asking for "more information" and pushing the decision to the next quarterly meeting. That deferral routinely costs a company three to four months of delayed capacity, during which competitors who've already resolved this question keep moving, and the CFO's next budget request starts from a position of diminished credibility.

## What the Board Actually Wants to See Before Sign-Off

European boards evaluating an offshore software outsourcing budget are, almost without exception, pattern-matching against a specific anxiety: an outsourcing arrangement that looked financially attractive and turned into an unaccountable mess — lost IP, a compliance gap, or a vendor relationship nobody could actually hold to account when delivery failed. A CFO who anticipates the real questions gets through approval materially faster than one who leads with the savings number alone.

**Who is the accountable legal counterparty, specifically?** This is nearly always the first question, and it needs a specific, named answer — not "the outsourcing company" in the abstract. The answer should name the EU-based governance entity holding contractual accountability (Amsterdam, in Manifera's structure) under Dutch or EU-familiar law, because a board will not accept an offshore-only counterparty as adequate accountability.

**What's our IP exposure, and does it survive a future transaction?** Boards at companies anticipating any future funding round, acquisition, or strategic partnership will probe IP assignment specifically, because a weak IP structure becomes a due-diligence blocker exactly when the company can least afford delay. The prepared answer covers individual engineer NDAs, client-owned source repositories from the first commit, and an IP clause that survives contract termination under enforceable EU law.

**How is this structurally different from an outsourcing failure someone on the board has experienced?** Most boards have at least one member with a direct or secondhand outsourcing failure story. The CFO's answer needs to name the specific structural safeguard — an independent governance layer separated from execution, contracted staff continuity, documented process standards — rather than a general assurance that "we picked a better vendor."

**Is the savings number risk-adjusted, or just the headline day-rate differential?** A financially sophisticated board will ask the CFO to defend the number against realistic rework, delay, or governance-failure risk, not just present the raw day-rate gap. The strongest answer explicitly includes the governance layer's cost (typically 8-15% over pure execution pricing) as the line item that prices out exactly the risks the board is probing.

**What's the fallback if this doesn't work?** Boards want to know the downside is bounded before approving the upside. A CFO who can describe a defined pilot phase with explicit success criteria, and a clear off-ramp if those criteria aren't met, converts an open-ended commitment into a bounded, reversible decision — which is a categorically easier approval to secure.

A CFO who walks in with specific, rehearsed answers to these five questions typically clears board approval in a single session. A CFO who leads only with the savings number and improvises the rest usually gets sent back for "further review" — a deferral that costs real time and momentum.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Ghent Healthcare Software Company's Board Approval

De Bruyne Health Systems, a Ghent-based healthcare software provider, had a CFO preparing to present a software outsourcing budget for a clinical scheduling module rebuild, in a sector where the board was especially sensitive to data governance given the healthcare data involved. Her initial approach was cost-led; a board advisor with outsourcing experience warned her the healthcare-data governance question would dominate the room regardless of the savings number.

She restructured the presentation around accountability first, naming Manifera's Amsterdam entity as explicitly accountable for GDPR and healthcare-data compliance, detailing the IP structure, and proposing a five-week pilot with defined clinical-data-handling success criteria before full commitment. The board approved the pilot without deferral, and the full engagement was approved after the pilot confirmed the compliance structure held up under review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO anticipating the board's first and hardest question) What's the single most important question to prepare for in board approval?

Who is the accountable legal counterparty, specifically. A European board needs a named, EU-based accountable entity, not a general reference to "the outsourcing company," and this is almost always the first substantive question raised.

### (Scenario: CFO worried IP questions could derail a future transaction) How specific does the IP answer need to be for a board anticipating a future funding round or acquisition?

Very specific: individual engineer NDAs, client-owned source repositories from the first commit, and an IP assignment clause enforceable under EU law that survives contract termination. Vague reassurance will not satisfy a board anticipating due diligence.

### (Scenario: CFO addressing a board member's past outsourcing failure) How should we respond when a board member raises a past outsourcing failure as a concern?

Name the specific structural safeguard in your proposal — an independent governance layer, contracted staff continuity, documented process standards — rather than offering a general assurance that this engagement will be different.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding how to present the savings figure) Should the savings number be presented as a headline percentage or something more detailed?

Present it risk-adjusted from the start, explicitly including the governance layer's cost as the line item pricing out legal, compliance, and delivery risk, rather than a headline percentage the board will discount once they start asking questions.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding between requesting full budget or a phased approach) Is a full-budget request or a phased pilot approach more likely to get board approval?

A bounded pilot with defined success criteria and a clear off-ramp is almost always easier to approve, particularly for a first offshore engagement or one touching sensitive data, since it converts an open-ended commitment into a reversible one.
