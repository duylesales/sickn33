Your board doesn't just want to approve "an outsourcing budget" — they want to approve a specific model, with a specific accountable party, and specific answers to the questions that sink most proposals in the room. Do you have all three ready?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European company is bringing a proposal to the board to fund an offshore software development engagement, and while the engineering team has a preferred model in mind — usually a dedicated pod, sometimes staff augmentation for a narrower need — the CFO knows the board won't approve "a model" in the abstract. They'll want to know why this structure, what happens if it underperforms, and who's accountable if it doesn't.

**The Agitation:** A board that tables an outsourcing proposal for "more structural clarity" doesn't just delay the budget — it signals to the rest of the organization that leadership couldn't articulate a clear plan, which makes every subsequent engineering budget request harder to land, not just this one. Proposals rejected on structural grounds typically take 6-10 weeks to return to the board agenda, and the roadmap items gated behind that budget slip by the same window, often longer once internal priorities shift during the delay.

## The Five Questions a Board Raises About Outsourcing Structure

Across board conversations about offshore engineering engagements, five questions recur regardless of company size or sector, and a CFO who arrives with pre-built answers converts a much higher share of first-pass proposals than one relying on the engineering team's preferred model alone.

"Why this model and not a cheaper one?" If the proposal is for a dedicated pod, a board member will ask why staff augmentation — which looks cheaper on a per-hour basis — wasn't chosen instead. The answer needs to name the specific coordination and continuity risk staff augmentation carries for an ongoing product area, not just assert that the pod is "better."

"What's our exposure if this underperforms?" Boards want a bounded downside, not an open-ended commitment. The answer needs to describe a scoped pilot phase with defined success criteria, so the honest answer to "what if it fails" is a contained pilot cost, not the full engagement budget.

"Who is accountable, specifically?" A vague answer like "the vendor" doesn't satisfy a board member who's dealt with an unaccountable vendor relationship before, personally or by reputation. The answer needs a named entity in an EU jurisdiction — Amsterdam, in Manifera's structure — that the board can understand as legally and practically accountable.

"How does this compare to hiring locally?" Even when the board supports the outsourcing direction in principle, someone will ask for the local-hiring comparison as a sanity check. The answer needs real figures: fully-loaded local cost, typical time-to-fill in the current market, and the corresponding numbers for the proposed offshore structure — not a hand-wave that offshore is "generally cheaper."

"What does success look like in six months, and how will we know?" Boards approve initiatives they can track, not open-ended relationships. The answer needs concrete, measurable milestones — sprint velocity targets, specific features shipped, a defined pilot-to-full-engagement transition point — that make the engagement auditable at the next board review rather than a vague ongoing line item nobody revisits.

A CFO who walks in with these five answers prepared isn't just increasing approval odds — they're reframing the proposal from "should we outsource" to "here is the specific, accountable, bounded structure we're asking you to approve," which is a fundamentally easier decision for a board to say yes to in one sitting.
