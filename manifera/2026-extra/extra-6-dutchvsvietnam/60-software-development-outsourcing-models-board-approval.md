Your board doesn't just want to approve "an outsourcing budget" — they want to approve a specific model, with a specific accountable party, and specific answers to the questions that sink most proposals in the room. Do you have all three ready?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European company is bringing a proposal to the board to fund an offshore software development engagement, and while the engineering team has a preferred model in mind — usually a dedicated pod, sometimes staff augmentation for a narrower need — the CFO knows the board won't approve "a model" in the abstract. They'll want to know why this structure, what happens if it underperforms, and who's accountable if it doesn't.

**The Agitation:** A board that tables an outsourcing proposal for "more structural clarity" doesn't just delay the budget — it signals to the rest of the organization that leadership couldn't articulate a clear plan, which makes every subsequent engineering budget request harder to land, not just this one. Proposals rejected on structural grounds typically take 6-10 weeks to return to the board agenda, and the roadmap items gated behind that budget slip by the same window, often longer once internal priorities shift during the delay.

## The Five Questions a Board Raises About Outsourcing Structure

Across board conversations about offshore engineering engagements, five questions recur regardless of company size or sector, and a CFO who arrives with pre-built answers converts a much higher share of first-pass proposals than one relying on the engineering team's preferred model alone.

"Why this model and not a cheaper one?" If the proposal is for a dedicated pod, a board member will ask why staff augmentation — which looks cheaper on a per-hour basis — wasn't chosen instead. The answer needs to name the specific coordination and continuity risk staff augmentation carries for an ongoing product area, not just assert that the pod is "better."

"What's our exposure if this underperforms?" Boards want a bounded downside, not an open-ended commitment. The answer needs to describe a scoped pilot phase with defined success criteria, so the honest answer to "what if it fails" is a contained pilot cost, not the full engagement budget.

"Who is accountable, specifically?" A vague answer like "the vendor" doesn't satisfy a board member who's dealt with an unaccountable vendor relationship before, personally or by reputation. The answer needs a named entity in an EU jurisdiction — Amsterdam, in Manifera's structure — that the board can understand as legally and practically accountable.

"How does this compare to hiring locally?" Even when the board supports the outsourcing direction in principle, someone will ask for the local-hiring comparison as a sanity check. The answer needs real figures: fully-loaded local cost, typical time-to-fill in the current market, and the corresponding numbers for the proposed offshore structure — not a hand-wave that offshore is "generally cheaper."

"What does success look like in six months, and how will we know?" Boards approve initiatives they can track, not open-ended relationships. The answer needs concrete, measurable milestones — sprint velocity targets, specific features shipped, a defined pilot-to-full-engagement transition point — that make the engagement auditable at the next board review rather than a vague ongoing line item nobody revisits.

A CFO who walks in with these five answers prepared isn't just increasing approval odds — they're reframing the proposal from "should we outsource" to "here is the specific, accountable, bounded structure we're asking you to approve," which is a fundamentally easier decision for a board to say yes to in one sitting.

## What a Line-Item Budget Looks Like When the Board Asks for One

Boards that approve the structural questions still frequently send a proposal back one more time for a simple reason: the budget line is a single number instead of a breakdown they can audit against outcomes. A defensible line-item budget for an offshore software development outsourcing engagement splits into three components a board can independently sanity-check: governance and oversight (the Amsterdam layer), typically 10-15% of total spend; execution capacity (the Vietnam pod), the largest line at roughly 75-80%, priced per engineer per month rather than a blended day rate; and a contingency reserve of 5-10% for scope adjustments discovered after the pilot phase. For a three-person dedicated pod, this typically resolves to €15,000-€25,000 per month in execution cost plus €2,000-€3,000 in governance overhead — figures a board member can compare directly against a single senior developer's fully-loaded Dutch salary, which is usually the fastest way to make the case land without a follow-up meeting. CFOs who present the budget pre-split this way get fewer "what's actually in this number" questions, because the answer is already on the page.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO whose proposal was deferred once already) Our board deferred our outsourcing proposal once. What's the fastest way to get it approved on the second attempt?

Rebuild the proposal around the five recurring board questions — model justification, bounded downside, named accountability, local-hiring comparison, and measurable success criteria — rather than reworking the underlying cost case alone.

### (Scenario: CFO asked to justify a dedicated pod over cheaper options) How do I justify choosing a dedicated pod when staff augmentation looks cheaper on paper?

Name the specific coordination and continuity risk staff augmentation carries for ongoing, evolving product work, and show that the pod's higher per-hour rate is offset by avoided management overhead — a concrete comparison, not a general preference.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting to bound the board's downside exposure) How do we structure the proposal so the board isn't approving an open-ended budget?

Include a scoped pilot phase with defined success criteria and a clear decision point before full budget release, so the board is approving a bounded initial commitment, not the full engagement upfront.

### (Scenario: CFO needing comparative data for the board) What local-hiring comparison data should be in the proposal?

Fully-loaded local salary and overhead figures, typical time-to-fill in the current market, and the equivalent cost and ramp-up figures for the proposed offshore structure, presented side by side.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting the engagement to be trackable after approval) How do we make sure the board can track progress after approving the engagement?

Define measurable milestones — sprint velocity targets, specific deliverables, a pilot-to-full-engagement transition point — so the next board review has concrete progress to assess rather than a vague status update.

### (Scenario: CFO asked to break down the budget line item) How should the outsourcing budget be broken down for board scrutiny?

Split it into three audit-able components: governance and oversight (roughly 10-15% of spend), execution capacity priced per engineer per month (75-80%), and a contingency reserve (5-10%) for scope adjustments after the pilot. A single blended number invites more follow-up questions than a pre-split one.

### (Scenario: CFO comparing offshore development services types) What's the difference between staff augmentation, a dedicated pod, and a fully managed project as outsourcing services?

Staff augmentation adds individual contractors under your existing management, a dedicated pod adds a self-managing team under Amsterdam governance, and a fully managed project transfers delivery ownership entirely to the vendor against defined outcomes — each shifts a different amount of coordination overhead off your internal team.

### (Scenario: CFO needing to justify governance overhead cost) Why does the Amsterdam governance layer add cost on top of the Vietnam execution rate, and is it worth it to the board?

The governance layer is what lets the board name a single accountable EU-jurisdiction party instead of a diffuse offshore vendor relationship — the 10-15% overhead buys the architecture sign-off, risk management, and contractual accountability that make the rest of the proposal approvable in one sitting.

### (Scenario: CFO preparing for follow-up board questions after initial approval) What follow-up questions does the board typically ask after approving the outsourcing model, at the next review?

Expect questions on velocity against the milestones set at approval, cost variance against the original line-item budget, and whether the pilot's success criteria justify scaling the pod — all of which are easy to answer if the milestone and budget structure from the original proposal was documented rather than verbal.
