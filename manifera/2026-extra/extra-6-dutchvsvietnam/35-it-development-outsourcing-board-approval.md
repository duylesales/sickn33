What happens in the ten minutes after a CFO presents an IT development outsourcing budget line to the board, and one director asks the single question the proposal deck never answered?

**The Pain:** A CFO has built the business case for an offshore IT development outsourcing engagement — the cost savings are real, the vendor looks credible, the internal champion is convinced. But the board meeting isn't a rubber stamp. Directors with fiduciary duty will ask about risk, IP, and accountability in ways a vendor's sales deck was never built to answer, and a CFO who walks in without those answers pre-built risks the entire budget line getting tabled for another quarter.

**The Agitation:** A deferred approval isn't a neutral outcome — it's a quarter of delayed roadmap, a competitor who didn't wait, and a CFO whose judgment on the next proposal gets scrutinized more closely because the first one wasn't board-ready. Boards that have seen one offshore engagement go wrong at a portfolio company — and most European boards have, at least once — ask sharper questions the second time, and an unprepared answer to any one of them can stall a budget that was otherwise sound.

## The Five Questions Every Board Asks

Across European boardrooms, the questions directors ask about an outsourcing development company proposal cluster around five recurring themes, and a CFO who has pre-built answers to all five walks into that meeting fundamentally differently than one relying on the vendor's own pitch deck.

The first question is almost always about IP ownership: does the company actually, defensibly own what gets built, and under what jurisdiction is that ownership enforceable. A CFO needs to be able to state plainly that the contracting entity sits under EU or Dutch law, that individual engineer NDAs exist beneath the vendor-level agreement, and that source code repositories are owned by the company from day one — not "the vendor has assured us of this," but a specific, citable contract clause.

The second is accountability: who, specifically, is responsible if delivery fails. Boards are trained by experience to distrust diffuse accountability, and "the vendor" is not a specific enough answer. A governance structure with a named entity — in this case, an Amsterdam-based team with defined sign-off authority over architecture and quality — gives a CFO a concrete answer rather than a vague reassurance.

The third is risk concentration: what happens if the vendor itself fails, loses key people, or the relationship simply doesn't work out. Boards want to know the exit path before approving the entry, and a CFO should be able to describe, in specific terms, how source code ownership, documentation standards, and a persistent (rather than rotating) team structure mean a failed engagement is a costly disappointment, not an unrecoverable one.

The fourth is the financial model itself, and specifically whether the savings number presented is realistic or optimistic. Boards that have seen a "40% cost reduction" projection evaporate into change orders and rework want to see the model broken down: team composition, scope discipline, and a defensible comparison against the true fully-loaded cost of the local alternative — not a single headline percentage without the underlying assumptions shown.

The fifth, increasingly common on European boards specifically, is regulatory and compliance exposure: does an offshore engagement affect the company's obligations under GDPR, sector-specific regulation, or upcoming frameworks like the EU AI Act if AI-adjacent work is involved. A CFO should be able to state where data resides, how it's processed, and what contractual data-processing terms govern the offshore team's access — a question that, if unanswered, is often enough on its own to stall approval.

A CFO who walks into the board meeting with pre-built, specific answers to all five — not just confidence that the vendor is good — converts what could be a contentious approval into a straightforward one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO preparing a first board presentation) What's the single most common reason a board tables an outsourcing budget proposal?

Unclear accountability — when the answer to "who is responsible if this fails" is "the vendor" rather than a named, specific governance entity, boards frequently table the decision pending a clearer structure.

### (Scenario: CFO whose proposal was previously rejected) Our board already rejected one offshore proposal — how do we rebuild credibility for a resubmission?

Rebuild the proposal around the five recurring board questions — IP jurisdiction, accountability, exit path, cost model, and compliance — with specific, citable answers to each, rather than a more polished version of the same vendor pitch.

### (Scenario: CFO asked about data residency and compliance) How do we answer board questions about GDPR or data residency for an offshore engagement?

State explicitly where data resides and is processed, and ensure the contract includes specific data-processing terms governing the offshore team's access — this is increasingly the question that stalls approval when left vague.

### (Scenario: CFO asked what happens if the vendor relationship fails) What's our actual exit path if the offshore engagement doesn't work out?

With source code owned by the company from day one, thorough documentation standards, and a persistent rather than rotating team, a failed engagement becomes a costly disappointment rather than an unrecoverable loss — and boards want that path described explicitly before approving the budget.

### (Scenario: CFO questioned on the realism of projected savings) How do we defend a cost-savings projection against board skepticism?
