Delivery risk doesn't announce itself in a kickoff call — it shows up three sprints in, as a burndown chart that keeps sliding right while every status update still says "on track." What actually determines whether Vietnam-based execution avoids that pattern?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering weighing software development outsourcing services in Vietnam already knows the cost argument. What isn't clear is the delivery-risk argument — whether a distributed team six timezones away, operating under a different employment culture, will actually hit sprint commitments with the same reliability as a co-located team, or whether "reliable" is just what every vendor claims until the first missed milestone.

**The Agitation:** Delivery risk that isn't actively managed compounds silently. A single missed sprint rarely triggers escalation on its own — it's the fourth consecutive slightly-missed sprint, each individually explainable, that adds up to a release slipping by a full quarter and a VP of Engineering having to explain a €60,000-€100,000 cost overrun in re-planning, expedited QA, and delayed go-to-market to a board that thought the project was "basically done" two months ago.

## The Four Risk Categories That Actually Matter

Delivery risk in offshore software development outsourcing isn't one thing — it's four distinct categories, each requiring a different control, and a VP of Engineering evaluating Vietnam specifically should test each one independently rather than accepting a single blanket assurance that "delivery is reliable."

Technical risk is the first, and it's about whether the team's engineering practices actually produce maintainable, tested code rather than code that merely passes a demo. The concrete signal to check: automated test coverage requirements written into the delivery contract, not left as a vague quality promise, alongside a defined code review process where every merge requires sign-off from someone other than its author. A mature Vietnam-based delivery organization runs this exactly like a mature Western engineering team — trunk-based or well-structured branching, CI pipelines that block merges on failing tests, and a Definition of Done that includes documentation, not just passing code.

Schedule risk is the second, and the control here is story-point calibration, a step most vendors skip because it's tedious and doesn't show up in a sales pitch. A pod's early sprint estimates are frequently miscalibrated against the client's historical velocity — not from bad faith, but because the two teams haven't yet built shared reference points for what a "medium" story actually takes. The fix is a defined calibration period, typically the first two to three sprints, where estimates are explicitly tracked against actuals and adjusted, rather than trusted at face value from sprint one — a VP of Engineering should ask whether this calibration step exists in the vendor's standard process, or whether estimates are simply asserted.

Communication risk is the third, and it's the one most commonly mishandled through underspecification rather than through language ability — English proficiency among Vietnam's mid-to-senior engineering talent is generally strong, particularly at organizations with sustained EU client exposure. The actual risk is structural: whether requirements are captured in a shared, versioned specification both teams reference, or whether critical decisions get made verbally in a call that only one side remembers accurately a week later. Asynchronous documentation discipline — every significant decision written down, timestamped, and linked to the relevant ticket — is the control that prevents this, and it's a much better predictor of communication risk than the overlap window's length.

Quality risk is the fourth, and it's structurally about whether QA is independent of development or an afterthought performed by the same engineers who wrote the code. A pod where the same person builds and tests a feature has an inherent blind spot; a pod with a structurally separate QA function, running against a defined test plan rather than ad hoc manual clicking, catches materially more defects before a release, and its defect-escape rate — the percentage of bugs that reach production versus get caught pre-release — is a number a VP of Engineering should ask for directly, not assume.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about early sprint estimate accuracy) Why do offshore sprint estimates so often miss in the first few sprints?

The two teams haven't yet built shared reference points for what a given story size actually takes. A defined calibration period, tracking estimate-versus-actual for the first two to three sprints, closes this gap quickly and predictably.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering questioning code quality safeguards) How do we know code quality won't degrade once the pod is running independently?

Insist on a mandatory code review process where every merge requires sign-off from someone other than the author, and CI pipelines that block merges on failing automated tests, written into the delivery contract rather than left as an informal practice.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering concerned about self-tested code) Why does QA independence matter more than the overall QA effort?

A developer testing their own code has an inherent blind spot for the assumptions baked into their own implementation. A structurally separate QA function, testing against a defined plan, consistently catches materially more defects before release.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering wanting a concrete quality metric) What's the single best metric for evaluating whether a vendor's delivery risk is under control?

Defect-escape rate — the percentage of bugs that reach production rather than get caught pre-release — is a concrete, trackable number that reveals whether QA and code review discipline are actually working, unlike a subjective quality claim.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering evaluating documentation practices) How do we prevent miscommunication from undocumented verbal decisions?

Require every significant decision to be written down, timestamped, and linked to the relevant ticket in a shared system both teams reference, rather than relying on memory of a call — this control matters more for communication risk than timezone overlap alone.
