The budget line is approved before the board meeting even starts, until someone on the board asks the one question the CFO didn't prepare for — and then it isn't approved anymore, it's tabled for next quarter.

**The Pain:** A CFO at a Netherlands or EU-based company is bringing a proposal to the board to fund an offshore dedicated development team, expecting the conversation to center on cost savings. Instead, the board's questions skip past cost entirely and go straight to accountability, data protection, and what happens if it doesn't work — questions the CFO's spreadsheet doesn't answer.

**The Agitation:** A budget request that gets tabled costs more than the delay itself. Every quarter a board defers an offshore decision, the company either continues paying inflated local contractor rates to cover the capacity gap — often €15,000-€30,000 a month in premium day-rate contracting — or the roadmap slips further, with competitive features shipping late enough to matter to a renewal conversation or a competitive deal the sales team is already trying to close.

## The Five Questions a Board Actually Asks, and How to Answer Each

Boards approving an offshore engineering budget for the first time tend to ask a consistent set of questions, in a consistent order, once the discussion moves past the headline cost number. A CFO who's prepared for these five specifically walks into the meeting with an approval, not a deferral.

**"Who is accountable if delivery fails?"** This is almost always the first substantive question, and it's a governance question, not a technical one. The answer that satisfies a board is a named, EU-jurisdiction entity with contractual accountability for delivery quality — not "the offshore team is very good," which isn't an accountability structure, it's a hope. A structure where an Amsterdam-headquartered entity holds the commercial contract and signs off on delivery against agreed SLAs gives the board a concrete answer: accountability sits with a company operating under law the board's own legal advisors understand.

**"What happens to our data and IP?"** For any company handling customer data — which under GDPR means essentially every B2B software company operating in the EU — this question has real regulatory teeth. The answer needs to cover three things specifically: a GDPR-compliant data processing agreement naming the offshore entity as a documented subprocessor, source code and IP assigned to the client from the first commit under a contract clause that survives termination, and individual engineer NDAs, not just a vendor-entity-level agreement that leaves individual conduct unaddressed.

**"How is this different from an engagement that's failed before, here or elsewhere?"** Boards with any institutional memory of a bad offshore experience — their own or a peer company's — will ask this directly, and a CFO needs a structural answer, not a reassurance. The answer is the governance/execution separation itself: most failed offshore engagements failed because the execution team had no independent quality check and no accountable party above it, not because offshore delivery is inherently unreliable. Naming that distinction, and showing the specific structure that addresses it, converts board skepticism into board buy-in.

**"What's the real cost comparison, not just the headline savings?"** A board that's seen a vendor pitch before is wary of a cost number that doesn't survive scrutiny. The credible answer includes the governance layer's cost inside the total, not as a hidden line item discovered later, and compares against the fully-loaded cost of the local alternative — salary, employer costs, recruiting, and management overhead — rather than against a bare salary figure that understates the true cost of the status quo.

**"What's our exit path if this doesn't work?"** This question tests whether the CFO has actually modeled downside risk or is presenting only the upside case. A credible answer describes a scoped pilot period before long-term commitment, a contract structure that doesn't lock the company into a multi-year term without milestone checkpoints, and — critically — confirmation that IP and code ownership means the company retains everything built even if the engagement ends, so a failed pilot doesn't mean starting from zero with a different vendor.

## The Economics

Every quarter a board tables an offshore budget decision has a real cost that rarely gets attributed correctly — it shows up as continued premium contractor spend or a slipping roadmap, not as a line item labeled "cost of deferred decision." Premium local contracting to cover the same capacity gap typically runs €15,000-€30,000 a month more than a governed offshore pod would cost outright, meaning a single deferred quarter can cost more than the entire due-diligence process the board was trying to be careful about.
