The vendor is chosen, the proposal is strong, the price is right — and then the board asks one question about the contract structure that the CFO can't answer cleanly, and the signature gets delayed another month.

**The Pain:** A CFO at a Netherlands or EU-based company has selected an offshore software development company and needs board sign-off before the contract is signed. The CFO expects the board's scrutiny to focus on the vendor's technical credentials and the price. Instead, the questions center on the contract structure itself — jurisdiction, data governance, and what happens if the relationship goes wrong — territory the vendor's own sales materials never addressed.

**The Agitation:** A signature delayed by unanswered board questions has a real cost, not just an inconvenience. Every month a signing is delayed, the company either continues paying for interim capacity at a premium — commonly €12,000-€25,000 a month in contractor or overtime costs to cover the gap — or a competitive project deadline slips, with the commercial consequence (a delayed launch, a missed renewal window) landing on whichever function was waiting on the software.

## The Contract-Structure Questions a Board Asks That a Sales Deck Doesn't Answer

Boards evaluating an offshore software development company contract, once past the technical credentials, converge on a consistent set of structural questions. A CFO prepared with specific answers to these five closes the signing gap; one who isn't gets sent back for another round.

**"Who is the actual contracting entity, and where are they based?"** Many offshore vendor proposals are fronted by a recognizable brand but contracted through an entity registered in a jurisdiction the board's legal advisors have no direct familiarity with. The board needs a direct answer: is the signing counterparty an EU-jurisdiction entity, and if not, what specific legal recourse exists and how has it been tested. An Amsterdam-headquartered contracting entity, with execution subcontracted to the delivery team, answers this cleanly.

**"What does our data processing agreement actually say?"** Boards with any GDPR exposure — again, most EU B2B software companies — increasingly ask this specifically rather than accepting "we have a DPA" as sufficient. The answer needs to name the offshore entity as a documented subprocessor, specify data handling and retention terms, and ideally be reviewable by the board's own compliance advisor before signing, not after.

**"How is delivery quality independently verified, not just self-reported?"** This question tests whether the CFO has built in any check beyond trusting the vendor's own status reporting. The credible answer describes a governance function — architecture review, milestone sign-off — that sits structurally apart from the team being paid to deliver, so the board isn't relying on the vendor to grade its own work.

**"What's the realistic downside scenario, and how exposed are we?"** Boards want to hear the CFO has modeled failure, not just success. The credible answer covers: a scoped pilot or phased milestone structure rather than a single large upfront commitment, contractual IP ownership that protects the company's work product regardless of how the engagement ends, and a defined exit mechanism if quality or delivery falls short of agreed standards.

**"Why this vendor specifically, against the alternatives you evaluated?"** Boards are wary of a single-vendor recommendation that reads as a foregone conclusion rather than a genuine comparison. The credible answer shows the CFO evaluated the vendor against at least one alternative — commonly a local agency or a competing offshore company — on cost, governance structure, and verifiable track record, not just price.

A CFO who walks in with structural answers to all five, rather than technical reassurances about the vendor's engineering quality, gets the signature approved in the room rather than tabled for the next meeting.
