A CFO walks into a board meeting with a proposal to engage a Vietnam software development company for 40% of the engineering budget, and the first question isn't about cost at all — it's "who's accountable if this goes wrong," and a shrug isn't an answer a European board will accept twice.

**The Pain:** A CFO has built a compelling cost case for engaging a Vietnam software development company, but the board — particularly members with prior offshore experience elsewhere in their portfolio — is going to ask pointed questions about accountability, IP, and continuity that a purely cost-focused business case doesn't address, and getting caught without answers at the board table damages the CFO's credibility on the next proposal too.

**The Agitation:** Boards that have seen an offshore engagement fail before don't ask softball questions the second time. A CFO who can't answer specifics on legal accountability, IP chain of custody, and continuity risk in the room typically gets sent back to "firm up the proposal" — a delay that costs a scale-up 6-8 weeks of momentum on a roadmap decision, and a second pass through board review that makes every subsequent vendor proposal face heavier scrutiny by default.

## The Five Questions Every Experienced European Board Actually Asks

A CFO preparing a Vietnam software development company proposal for board approval should assume the board has either lived through, or heard about, an offshore engagement that went wrong somewhere in their portfolio companies. That means the proposal needs to preempt five specific questions with evidence, not reassurance.

The first question is jurisdictional accountability: which legal entity is the counterparty, and under what legal framework does the contract sit? A board with prior offshore exposure knows that a pure Vietnam-incorporated contracting entity means any dispute, IP claim, or breach of contract runs through a jurisdiction the company's own legal counsel doesn't practice in day to day. The strongest answer a CFO can bring is a governance structure where the contracting entity sits under a European legal framework the board's own counsel can directly evaluate — turning an open question into a settled one before it's asked.

The second question is IP chain of custody, and boards ask this with unusual specificity because they've watched it become a due diligence blocker in a later funding round or acquisition. The CFO needs to be able to state precisely: are individual engineers bound by NDA, not just the vendor entity; is source code repository ownership assigned to the client from day one; does the IP assignment clause survive contract termination. A CFO who can answer these three sub-questions with a yes and a citation to the specific contract clause closes this line of questioning immediately.

The third question is continuity risk — what happens if the vendor's business changes, key people leave, or the relationship needs to be unwound. Boards that have seen offshore engagements fail have usually seen this specific failure mode: a vendor's best people rotate off, quality degrades gradually, and by the time it's visible on a delivery metric, months of technical debt have accumulated. The answer that satisfies this question is a named, continuity-contracted pod with defined reassignment notice periods, plus a standing governance function whose explicit job is catching quality drift before it compounds — not "we've had a good relationship so far."

The fourth question is cost transparency beyond the headline day rate — specifically whether the quoted number reflects genuine senior-to-mid team composition or a junior-heavy team dressed up in a blended rate. A CFO should come prepared with named team member seniority breakdown, not just an aggregate rate, because boards with prior offshore exposure have specifically been burned by this gap before.

The fifth question, often unspoken until pressed, is exit optionality — can the company leave cleanly if the engagement isn't working, with code, documentation, and institutional knowledge intact, or is the company effectively locked in once the codebase has grown dependent on the vendor's undocumented tribal knowledge. A CFO who can point to contractual transition-out provisions and ongoing documentation-as-a-deliverable practice answers this before a board member has to ask it directly.

A CFO who walks in with evidence-backed answers to all five — not reassurance, actual contract clauses and structural evidence — gets a materially faster approval than one relying on cost savings alone to carry the room.
