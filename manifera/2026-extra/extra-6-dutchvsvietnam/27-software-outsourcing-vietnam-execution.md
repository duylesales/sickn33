Two EU companies outsource the same category of software work to Vietnam this quarter — one ships on time with clean architecture, the other spends the next year unwinding technical debt — so what's the actual variable that split those outcomes, because it clearly wasn't country choice?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a Netherlands or EU-based company has decided in principle that Vietnam-based software outsourcing is worth evaluating, but every case study — good and bad — seems to point to a different cause of success or failure, leaving no clear picture of what actually determines whether an engagement works.

**The Agitation:** Attributing outcome to the wrong variable is how a VP repeats a mistake with a different vendor. A team that blames "Vietnam" for a failed engagement when the real cause was an ungoverned, poorly-structured contract will make the same structural mistake with the next vendor too — and each failed cycle costs a mid-market company an average of four to six months of stalled roadmap plus €70,000-€150,000 in sunk engagement cost.

## What Actually Determines Success

The single largest determinant of outsourcing success isn't country, it's specification quality — and this holds true whether the work is outsourced to Vietnam, Poland, or down the street. Engagements that fail almost always trace back to ambiguous requirements handed to an execution team without a structured discovery phase to convert them into buildable specifications. Vietnam-based teams execute extremely well against clear specifications; no team anywhere executes well against vague ones, and blaming the geography for a specification failure is a category error that leads companies to repeat it with the next vendor.

The second determinant is process discipline, independent of geography: defined code review standards, a real CI/CD pipeline, sprint-level technical debt tracking, and a QA function embedded in delivery rather than bolted on at the end. Vietnam has a mature, deep talent pool — the country produces over 50,000 IT graduates annually, and Ho Chi Minh City specifically has become a dense hub for teams that have delivered production software for European and North American clients for well over a decade — but talent without process discipline still produces inconsistent outcomes, the same as it would anywhere.

The third determinant is communication structure across the timezone gap. Successful engagements design a deliberate overlap window — typically a few hours of CET morning aligning with Vietnam's afternoon — for live standups and decision-making, paired with async-friendly documentation as a default work output, not an afterthought. Failed engagements rely purely on synchronous communication and break down the moment a decision needs to happen outside the overlap window, creating exactly the delay and misalignment that gets blamed on "the offshore team" when it's really a cadence design failure.

The fourth determinant is continuity. Engagements that succeed keep the same core team assigned for the project's duration, building institutional knowledge of the client's codebase and business context over time. Engagements that fail frequently show a pattern of staff rotation — the vendor reassigning engineers to other clients mid-project — which resets context repeatedly and shows up as declining velocity that gets misattributed to team capability rather than staffing structure.

The fifth determinant, and the one that ties the others together, is whether there's a governance layer independently accountable for scope and quality — separate from the execution team itself. Without it, there's no independent check catching a specification gap, a process shortfall, or a continuity break before it compounds into a failed engagement. This is the structural reason a Netherlands-based VP of Engineering should evaluate the governance model as carefully as the execution team's technical skill: the two together determine the outcome, and either one alone is insufficient.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering who had a failed offshore engagement previously) We had a bad experience with Vietnam outsourcing before — what should we check differently this time?

Assess whether the failure traced to specification quality, process discipline, communication cadence, staff continuity, or governance — nearly every failed engagement traces to one of these five, rather than to geography itself.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering evaluating how much upfront discovery is needed) How much discovery work is actually necessary before an offshore team starts building?

Enough to convert loose requirements into specifications with clear acceptance criteria per feature — typically two to four weeks for a mid-sized engagement, and skipping it is one of the most common causes of failed outsourcing outcomes.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering assessing Vietnam's talent depth specifically) Is Vietnam's engineering talent pool actually deep enough for complex work?

Yes — Vietnam produces over 50,000 IT graduates annually, and Ho Chi Minh City has a mature ecosystem of engineers experienced in delivering for European and North American clients, provided process discipline and governance are in place to match that talent to the work well.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about staff rotation undermining continuity) How do we prevent staff rotation from resetting our project's context repeatedly?

Contract explicitly for project-duration continuity with a clause barring cross-client reassignment without approval, and verify this contractually rather than accepting a verbal assurance.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering deciding how to structure communication across the timezone gap) What communication structure actually works across the Netherlands-Vietnam timezone gap?

A deliberate daily overlap window, typically a few hours of CET morning aligning with Vietnam's afternoon, for live standups and decisions, paired with async-friendly documentation as a standard sprint output.
