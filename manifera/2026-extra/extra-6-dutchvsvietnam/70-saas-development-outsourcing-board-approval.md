Your board approved the ARR targets. Will they approve the engineering structure that has to hit them — especially once someone asks what happens to uptime if the offshore team underperforms?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European SaaS company has built a compelling cost case for SaaS development outsourcing to an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam pod, roughly halving the engineering cost of the next roadmap phase, but the board includes a director who previously sat through a portfolio company's failed offshore engagement and is instinctively skeptical of anything described the same way.

**The Agitation:** For a SaaS company specifically, a board that rejects or stalls an offshore custom software development proposal isn't just delaying a cost saving — it's implicitly forcing continued reliance on a more expensive engineering structure while ARR growth targets stay fixed, which compresses margin exactly when the board is also asking why gross margin hasn't improved. A CFO who can't get an outsourcing proposal approved in one cycle typically loses €50,000-€90,000 in avoidable spend over the delay, on top of the credibility cost of returning to the board with the same unresolved questions twice.

## The SaaS-Specific Version of the Board's Standard Questions

Boards ask a fairly standard set of questions about any offshore software development proposal, but for a SaaS company those questions carry sharper stakes because engineering quality connects directly to recurring revenue, uptime commitments, and customer trust — and a CFO's proposal needs to answer the SaaS-specific version of each question, not the generic one.

The first is uptime and reliability risk. A board evaluating a generic software project asks "will it be delivered on time"; a board evaluating a SaaS engineering change asks "will this affect the uptime our enterprise contracts guarantee." The answer needs to name the specific mechanism that protects reliability: an Amsterdam governance layer that independently reviews any architecture or deployment change with platform-wide blast radius before it ships, rather than trusting the execution team's self-assessment.

The second is data protection, sharpened for SaaS by the fact that a SaaS platform typically holds live customer data continuously, not just during a project. The board needs a specific answer about where and how customer data is processed during development and testing — ideally confirming that production customer data isn't used in offshore development or testing environments without explicit, documented controls, with the Amsterdam entity contractually accountable for this under GDPR.

The third is recurring-cost structure, which is a SaaS-specific twist on the standard "how much does this cost" question. A board wants to know not just the initial engagement cost but the steady-state cost of maintaining the outsourced structure as the product scales — does the pod's cost per engineer stay predictable as headcount grows, or does the proposal only make sense at the current, smaller scope. A credible proposal shows the cost structure at both the current scope and a projected scale-up scenario.

The fourth is the vendor-continuity question in its SaaS-specific form: what happens to feature velocity and incident response if the vendor relationship ends. For a SaaS company with a continuously evolving product, the board wants assurance that critical architectural knowledge isn't concentrated entirely in an offshore team with no internal redundancy — the answer here is that Amsterdam's ongoing architecture review means institutional knowledge about the platform's key decisions doesn't live solely with the execution pod.

The fifth is the pilot-first structuring question, identical in spirit to any offshore proposal but with a SaaS-specific pilot scope that matters: choosing a pilot module that's meaningful but not central to uptime-critical infrastructure, so the board is approving a genuinely bounded first step rather than a de facto full commitment disguised as a trial.

A board that stalls a SaaS outsourcing proposal typically costs the company continued reliance on a more expensive engineering structure for another quarter or two — on a €150,000-€220,000 annual savings case, that delay alone can represent €40,000-€70,000 in avoidable spend, independent of any opportunity cost from features that don't ship. ## SLA Terms a SaaS Board Will Want in Writing

Once the governance mechanism answers "will this affect uptime," the board's next move is usually to ask for the SLA in writing, not as a verbal assurance. A defensible SLA for an outsourced SaaS engineering pod names four things: incident severity tiers with specific response-time commitments — typically 30 minutes for a Sev-1 platform-wide outage, 4 hours for a Sev-2 degraded-service issue, and next-business-day for a Sev-3 cosmetic defect; an on-call rotation that includes at least one Amsterdam-based escalation contact, not solely the Vietnam execution team, so a board-facing incident review always has an EU-jurisdiction point of contact; a defined mean-time-to-resolution target tracked monthly, typically under 4 hours for Sev-1 incidents in a mature engagement; and service credits tied to missed SLA commitments, structured as a percentage of monthly fees rather than a symbolic gesture. Boards that see these four terms named explicitly in the contract, rather than folded into a general "we take uptime seriously" statement, treat the uptime risk question as resolved rather than deferred to the first incident — which is usually the difference between an approval in the room and a request for a follow-up meeting with legal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO preparing for a board director skeptical from a past failed offshore engagement) How do we address a board member who has seen offshore outsourcing fail before?

Lead with the specific mechanism that would have prevented their prior experience — an independent Amsterdam review of any deployment with platform-wide blast radius before it ships — rather than a general reassurance that "this vendor is different."

### (Scenario: CFO anticipating uptime and SLA questions from the board) How do we answer whether outsourcing puts our uptime commitments at risk?

Present the specific governance mechanism: Amsterdam independently reviews architecture and deployment decisions affecting platform-wide reliability before release, rather than relying on the execution team to self-approve changes.

### (Scenario: CFO needing to show cost structure holds as the company scales) Does the cost advantage of offshore SaaS development outsourcing hold as our product and team scale?

Present pricing at both current scope and a projected scale-up scenario. Manifera's pod cost structure remains predictable as engineering headcount grows, which a credible proposal should demonstrate rather than assume.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting a lower-risk pilot structure for board approval) What's a good pilot scope to propose for board approval on a SaaS product specifically?

Choose a module that's meaningful but not central to uptime-critical infrastructure — a reporting or internal-tooling feature rather than core transaction or billing logic — so the board approves a genuinely bounded first step.

### (Scenario: CFO concerned about institutional knowledge concentration in the offshore team) What happens to our platform knowledge if the vendor relationship ends?

Amsterdam's ongoing architecture review means institutional knowledge about key platform decisions is documented and held at the governance layer, not concentrated solely with the execution pod.

### (Scenario: CFO needing SLA specifics for the board) What response-time SLA should be in a contract for a Sev-1 platform outage?

A 30-minute response commitment for a Sev-1 platform-wide outage is standard, with a mean-time-to-resolution target under 4 hours for a mature engagement. Get both figures named explicitly in the contract, not folded into a general uptime commitment.

### (Scenario: CFO asked whether Amsterdam or Vietnam handles incidents) Who is the escalation contact during a production incident, Amsterdam or the Vietnam pod?

Both — the on-call rotation should include at least one Amsterdam-based escalation contact alongside the Vietnam execution team, so a board-facing incident review always has an EU-jurisdiction point of contact, not just the team that wrote the code.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting enforcement teeth on the SLA) How are missed SLA commitments enforced in the contract?

Through service credits structured as a percentage of monthly fees for each missed severity-tier commitment, not a symbolic gesture. This is what turns the SLA from an aspiration into a contractual term with actual consequences.

### (Scenario: CFO tracking SLA performance post-approval) What metric should the board review at each check-in to confirm the SLA is being met?

Mean-time-to-resolution by severity tier, tracked monthly against the contracted targets — a single number the board can compare meeting to meeting without needing an engineering briefing to interpret it.
