Who actually owns the code the moment an engineer in Ho Chi Minh City commits it — the engineer, the vendor entity, or the client who's paying for it? Most offshore contracts answer this question vaguely enough that nobody finds out the real answer until a dispute, an acquisition, or a departing contractor forces the issue.

**The Pain:** A CFO evaluating a dedicated software development team delivered from Vietnam needs a defensible answer to "do we actually own our own IP" before signing, but most offshore contract templates handle IP assignment at the vendor-entity level only, leaving a gap between what the contract technically says and what actually protects the client if an individual engineer, not the vendor company, is the one who created the disputed work.

**The Agitation:** An IP assignment gap doesn't cost anything until the moment it does — and that moment is almost always the worst possible one. A company that discovers during acquisition due diligence or a funding round that its core product IP was never properly assigned from the individual engineers who built it faces remediation costs of €50,000–€150,000 in retroactive legal work, and in the worst cases a materially reduced valuation or a stalled deal while the gap gets fixed under time pressure the company doesn't control.

## How IP Assignment Actually Has to Work in a Cross-Border Engagement

Custom software development delivered across a Netherlands-Vietnam structure requires IP assignment to function correctly at three separate layers, and a contract that only addresses one of the three leaves the client exposed at the other two.

The first layer is the master services agreement between the client and the contracting entity, which should state explicitly and unambiguously that all work product, including source code, documentation, architecture diagrams, and derivative works, is assigned to the client upon creation — not upon final payment, not upon project completion, but upon creation. This distinction matters because a "upon completion" assignment clause creates a window, however brief, during which IP ownership is ambiguous, and ambiguous IP ownership is precisely the kind of gap that surfaces during due diligence.

The second layer, and the one most offshore contracts skip entirely, is the individual engineer level. Under most jurisdictions' default intellectual property law, including Vietnam's, work created by an employee in the course of employment is generally owned by the employer — but "generally" is not the standard a European buyer's due diligence process will accept. The gold-standard structure requires each individual engineer assigned to a client's codebase to sign an individual confidentiality and IP assignment acknowledgment specific to that engagement, layered on top of their employment agreement with the vendor entity. This closes the theoretical gap where an individual engineer could later claim contribution rights, and it's the specific documentation a due diligence legal team asks to see, not just the master agreement.

The third layer is technical, not purely legal: repository ownership and access control. IP assignment on paper means little if the client doesn't have direct, verifiable ownership of the source control repository itself from day one — meaning the repository lives under the client's own organizational account, not the vendor's, with the vendor's engineers granted access as contributors rather than owners. A client who has to formally request a repository transfer at the end of an engagement has effectively been trusting the vendor's goodwill for the IP's custody chain the entire time, which is not a position a CFO should accept when the alternative — client-owned repositories from the first commit — costs nothing extra to set up.

There's a fourth consideration specific to a Netherlands-Vietnam structure: which legal framework the IP assignment clause itself sits under. An assignment clause governed purely by Vietnamese contract law is generally enforceable, but it puts the client's legal team in the position of validating unfamiliar jurisdictional language every time a question arises. Structuring the master agreement under Dutch or another EU-recognized legal framework — with the Vietnam-based engineering delivery governed as a subcontracted execution layer beneath that master agreement — means the IP assignment terms a CFO is relying on sit inside a framework the company's own counsel can evaluate directly, rather than requiring specialized Vietnamese legal counsel every time the question comes up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO reviewing an existing offshore contract for IP gaps) How do we know if our current offshore contract has an IP assignment gap?

Check whether the assignment clause operates at the vendor-entity level only, whether individual engineers have signed IP acknowledgments, and whether your organization directly owns the source repositories rather than receiving them at offboarding.

### (Scenario: CFO preparing for acquisition due diligence) What specific documents will a due diligence legal team ask for regarding offshore-developed IP?

Individual engineer confidentiality and IP assignment acknowledgments, evidence of client-owned repositories from early in the engagement, and confirmation that the assignment clause survives contract termination.

### (Scenario: CFO concerned about which country's law governs the IP clause) Does it matter whether the IP assignment clause is governed by Vietnamese law or Dutch law?

It affects how easily your own legal team can evaluate and enforce it. A Dutch or EU-recognized governing framework means your counsel can assess the clause directly, rather than requiring specialized Vietnamese legal review for every question.

### (Scenario: CFO worried about a departed engineer's contribution rights) Can a former engineer who worked on our codebase later claim any ownership rights?

Not if individual IP assignment and confidentiality acknowledgments were executed before that engineer began work. Without that documentation, the theoretical exposure exists even if practically unlikely to be pursued.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding whether to set up client-owned repositories from the start) Why does repository ownership matter if the contract already assigns IP?

Because contractual assignment and technical custody are two different protections. Client-owned repositories from the first commit remove any dependency on the vendor's cooperation to transfer custody later, closing a practical gap that paper assignment alone doesn't fully address.
