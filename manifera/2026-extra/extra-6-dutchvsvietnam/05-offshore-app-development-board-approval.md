The engineering case for going offshore is usually settled by the time it reaches the board — so why do so many CFOs still walk into that meeting unprepared for the five questions that actually decide whether the budget gets approved?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European company has built a solid cost case for an offshore app development engagement in Vietnam and is now three days from presenting it to the board. The engineering rationale is sound. What isn't yet on paper are answers to the questions a risk-conscious board member always asks — about data protection, IP ownership, vendor accountability, and what happens if it goes wrong — and a CFO who gets caught without those answers loses the room regardless of how good the cost numbers are.

**The Agitation:** A board that isn't satisfied on governance and risk doesn't just delay the vote — it often sends the CFO back to redo the entire proposal, costing another board cycle, sometimes another quarter, while the engineering team sits idle waiting on a decision. Delayed offshore approvals routinely cost €30,000–€60,000 in lost roadmap time for every quarter the decision slips, on top of the reputational cost of a CFO whose proposal wasn't board-ready the first time.

## The Five Questions That Decide the Vote, and How to Have the Answers Ready

Board members evaluating an offshore app development budget rarely push back on the cost savings — those numbers are usually the easiest part of the pitch to accept. What determines whether the budget clears is whether the CFO has pre-answered the risk questions a governance-minded board member is trained to ask, before they have to ask them.

The first question is almost always about data protection and jurisdiction: where is customer data processed, stored, and who is accountable for GDPR compliance if the app handles EU user data. A board wants to hear that a Netherlands or EU-based entity — not the offshore delivery team alone — owns compliance sign-off on data architecture before anything ships, and that this is a contractual commitment, not a verbal assurance from the sales call.

The second is IP ownership, phrased bluntly by any board member who has sat through a due diligence process before: "if this vendor relationship ends tomorrow, do we own everything that's been built?" The answer needs to be unambiguous — source-controlled repositories owned by the client from the first commit, individual engineer NDAs and IP assignment terms that survive contract termination, and no ambiguity about internal tooling or reusable components built along the way.

The third is vendor accountability structure: who is the actual contracting counterparty, and what happens if that counterparty runs into financial or operational trouble. Boards are increasingly sophisticated about vendor concentration risk post-pandemic, and a single offshore delivery entity with no separate governance backing is a weaker answer than a dual-entity structure where a Netherlands-headquartered company holds the client contract and stands behind delivery.

The fourth question, usually from whoever has run a failed outsourcing engagement before, is about what happens when things go wrong mid-engagement — not if, when. A credible answer describes a documented escalation path with a named accountable contact, defined SLAs for response time on critical issues, and a governance layer that catches problems before they reach the board rather than after. "We'll figure it out" is the answer that kills confidence in the room.

The fifth is the exit and continuity question: what's the actual cost and disruption of unwinding the engagement if it isn't working after six or twelve months. Boards want to hear that source code, documentation, and institutional knowledge stay with the client throughout — that there's no vendor lock-in structurally engineered into the relationship — and that a pilot phase exists specifically to de-risk the full commitment before it's made.

A CFO who walks in with pre-built, specific answers to these five questions — not generic reassurances — converts what could be a multi-meeting negotiation into a single-session approval, because the board's actual job in that room is risk assessment, and risk assessment satisfied efficiently is a budget approved efficiently.
