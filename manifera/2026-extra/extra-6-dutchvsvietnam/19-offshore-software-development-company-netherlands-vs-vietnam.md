Two proposals on the table, one from a Dutch agency at €140 an hour and one from a Vietnam-based company at €55 an hour — and the CTO signing off knows the decision isn't actually about the hourly rate, even though that's the number everyone in the room keeps returning to.

**The Pain:** A CTO at a Netherlands-based company needs to choose a software development company for a defined project and has quotes from both a local Dutch agency and an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam-based company. Internal stakeholders keep the conversation anchored on the hourly rate difference, when the actual decision has several other variables that matter more to whether the project succeeds.

**The Agitation:** A decision made purely on hourly rate, in either direction, produces predictable regret. Choosing the local agency purely for comfort can mean a budget overrun when a fixed-scope project inevitably needs more hours than quoted at a rate that makes every overage expensive; choosing the offshore company purely for the discount, without checking governance, can mean a technical debt bill that erases the savings within a year.

## Comparing the Two Options on the Variables That Actually Predict Outcomes

A CTO evaluating a local Dutch development company against an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam-based company should build the comparison around four variables, weighted by what actually predicts project success, rather than defaulting to whichever rate number is more comfortable.

**Effective cost, not hourly rate.** A Dutch development company at €120-€160 an hour and a governed Vietnam company at €45-€65 an hour aren't actually comparable on the headline number alone, because total project cost depends on hours consumed, and a well-governed offshore engagement with the same architectural rigor consumes a comparable number of hours to a local agency doing equivalent work. Run correctly, total project cost for a mid-complexity engagement typically comes out 40-55% lower with a governed Vietnam company, not just the headline hourly discount — because the governance layer prevents the scope creep and rework that erode a cheap local quote just as easily as an offshore one.

**Communication overhead and timezone reality.** A local Dutch agency offers same-timezone, potentially in-person collaboration — a genuine advantage for projects requiring frequent, ambiguous, evolving requirements discussions. A Vietnam-based company operates on a five-to-six-hour offset from Central European Time, which is manageable with a deliberately structured overlap window (late morning to early afternoon CET works for most teams) but does require the CTO to accept some asynchronous handoff for anything outside that window. For projects with well-defined scope, this overhead is minor; for projects requiring constant real-time iteration, it's a real factor worth weighting.

**Governance and accountability structure.** This is the variable a rate comparison alone completely misses. A local Dutch agency's accountability is straightforward by virtue of proximity and shared jurisdiction. A Vietnam-based company's accountability depends entirely on whether it has a genuine governance layer — ideally EU-jurisdiction — sitting above execution; without one, the CTO inherits quality-assurance responsibility personally, which for a resource-constrained team is a real hidden cost even if it never appears on an invoice.

**Talent pool depth for the specific technical requirement.** For a mainstream stack (web applications, standard API architectures, common cloud platforms), both markets have deep talent, and this variable is close to neutral. For a highly specialized or niche technical requirement, a CTO should verify specific expertise exists in whichever market they're evaluating, rather than assuming either location automatically has it.

Run through these four variables rather than the rate alone, the comparison for most mainstream software projects favors a properly governed Vietnam-based company — not because Dutch agencies are worse, but because the cost differential is large enough to fund the governance layer that closes the accountability gap, while still landing well below local pricing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CTO comparing quotes anchored purely on hourly rate) Is comparing hourly rate the right way to evaluate a Dutch agency against a Vietnam-based company?

No — total project cost, driven by hours consumed and rework avoided through governance, is the more predictive number. A governed Vietnam company typically delivers 40-55% lower total project cost, not just a lower headline rate.

### (Scenario: CTO worried about real-time collaboration across timezones) How much does the timezone gap actually affect project delivery?

For well-scoped projects, a structured four-hour CET overlap window handles most real-time needs, with async handoff covering the rest. Projects requiring constant, ambiguous real-time iteration feel the gap more than clearly scoped ones.

### (Scenario: CTO deciding whether to test both options before committing) Is it worth running a side-by-side pilot before choosing?

Yes, for any project above roughly €50,000 in expected scope, a scoped four-to-six-week pilot on both options is a low-cost way to validate governance quality and delivery speed with evidence rather than proposal claims.

### (Scenario: CTO assessing whether Vietnam-based companies have depth for a specialized technical stack) Does a Vietnam-based company have the same talent depth as a Dutch agency for niche technical requirements?

For mainstream stacks, both markets have comparable depth. For a highly specialized requirement, verify specific expertise exists with the actual proposed team, regardless of which market you're evaluating.

### (Scenario: CTO deciding how governance affects total cost) Does adding an Amsterdam governance layer erase the cost advantage of the Vietnam company?
