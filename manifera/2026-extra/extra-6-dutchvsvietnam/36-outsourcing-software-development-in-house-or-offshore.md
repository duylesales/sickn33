If a Netherlands-based founder could only keep one part of the product roadmap in-house, which part would it be — and does the current all-or-nothing hiring plan even ask that question?

**The Pain:** A first-time founder or CTO in the Netherlands, staring at a product roadmap that's grown faster than the team, is framing the outsourcing decision as a binary: build everything in-house, or hand the whole roadmap to an outside vendor. Neither option actually matches how the strongest Dutch product companies operate, and the binary framing is what's making the decision feel harder than it needs to be.

**The Agitation:** Treating "in-house vs. offshore" as all-or-nothing leads to one of two expensive mistakes: over-hiring locally for work that didn't need daily on-site context, burning €90,000-€120,000 per unnecessary senior hire annually, or handing core differentiating product logic to an undifferentiated outsourcing vendor with no stake in the company's long-term architecture — a mistake that shows up eighteen months later as a codebase nobody in-house actually understands anymore.

## Core, Context, and the Roadmap Split

The useful question isn't "should we outsource software development" — it's "which parts of our roadmap are core, and which are context," a distinction borrowed from how mature product organizations actually allocate engineering attention, and one that resolves the in-house-versus-offshore question far more precisely than a blanket policy in either direction.

Core work is the part of the product that constitutes the actual competitive differentiation — the algorithm, workflow, or domain logic that, if a competitor copied it exactly, would still be hard to replicate because it depends on institutional knowledge the company has built up over time. For a Netherlands-based healthtech company, that might be a clinical-matching algorithm; for a fintech, a risk-scoring model. This work benefits enormously from daily proximity to the people who understand the domain — product, compliance, clinical or financial subject-matter experts — and is generally the strongest candidate for retaining in-house, at least at the architectural and design level.

Context work is everything else that the product needs to function but that isn't, itself, the differentiator: the admin dashboard, the reporting layer, the integration with a third-party payments or logistics API, the mobile app shell around a core web platform. This work is typically well-specified, doesn't require daily access to founder-level domain judgment once the requirements are clear, and is exactly where a well-governed offshore pod delivers the most value with the least risk — because getting it slightly wrong doesn't threaten the company's differentiation, and getting it built faster and cheaper directly extends runway.

The mistake most first-time founders make is applying a single policy — "everything in-house because it's our product" or "everything offshore because it's cheaper" — to both categories at once, when the correct model treats them completely differently. A founder who insists on hiring locally for the reporting dashboard is burning scarce, expensive local hiring capacity on work that didn't need it. A founder who hands the clinical-matching algorithm's core logic to an outsourcing vendor with no long-term stake in the company is trading differentiation for a short-term cost saving that the company will regret the first time it needs to extend that logic and discovers nobody in-house understands why it was built the way it was.

The practical framework, then, is a roadmap audit: classify every major workstream as core or context before making a single hiring or outsourcing decision, keep core work's architecture and design in-house even if execution is shared, and route context work toward a governed offshore pod that can absorb it at a materially lower cost and faster ramp than a local hire would require. This single exercise — done honestly, before the org chart gets built — prevents most of the expensive mistakes founders make in their first outsourcing decision.

## The Economics

Misapplying a single outsourcing policy across a whole roadmap has two distinct failure costs, and both are expensive in different ways. Over-hiring locally for context work costs €90,000-€120,000 per unnecessary senior hire annually, capacity that a governed pod could deliver at 40-60% less. Under-protecting core differentiating logic by fully outsourcing it costs far more unpredictably — the Barcelona case above cost roughly a year of lost architectural understanding before it was corrected, a delay that directly affected the company's ability to respond to an investor-driven roadmap request.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: First-time founder unsure how to classify roadmap work) How do we actually decide what counts as "core" versus "context" on our roadmap?

Ask whether a competitor who copied this workstream exactly would still struggle to replicate the outcome, because it depends on institutional or domain knowledge unique to your company. If yes, it's core. If the workstream is well-specified and doesn't depend on daily founder-level judgment, it's context.

### (Scenario: Founder worried about outsourcing anything that touches the core product) Is it ever safe to outsource execution of core product logic, even if we keep architecture in-house?

Yes, with the right structure — keeping architecture, design decisions, and domain judgment in-house while a governed pod executes against clearly specified requirements is different from fully outsourcing the logic itself, and can work well once the core design is stable.

### (Scenario: Founder who already outsourced everything and regrets it) We already outsourced our whole roadmap to one vendor — can this be corrected without a full rebuild?

Usually yes. The correction is typically a re-scoping exercise: identify which existing workstreams are actually core, bring their architecture back in-house or under closer governance, and leave well-specified context work with the offshore team.

### (Scenario: Founder deciding what to hire locally versus route offshore) What kinds of roles are worth hiring locally in the Netherlands even at higher cost?

Roles requiring daily proximity to domain experts, compliance stakeholders, or clients — typically product leadership and roles owning core differentiating logic — justify the higher local cost. Execution-heavy context work rarely does.

### (Scenario: Founder wanting to validate the split before committing) How do we test whether the core-context split will actually work for our team?

Start with a single context workstream — a dashboard, an integration, a secondary feature — routed to a pod for a four-to-six week pilot, while your in-house team continues owning the core logic, to see how the split performs before extending it further.
