A CFO doing contract due diligence spots a Singapore entity in the signature block of a proposal from a company that pitches Dutch governance and Vietnamese engineering, and the reasonable first question is: wait, what does Singapore have to do with any of this? The honest answer is refreshingly narrow — nothing about governance, nothing about engineering, and everything about how the invoice gets paid cleanly.

**The Pain:** A CFO reviewing Manifera's contracting structure notices that international client agreements and invoicing are routed through a Singapore-incorporated entity, and needs a precise, non-evasive answer to what that entity actually does — because an unexplained third jurisdiction in a two-country delivery story (Netherlands governance, Vietnam execution) reads, at first glance, like either a sales inconsistency or something being obscured.

**The Agitation:** CFOs who don't get a clear answer to an unexplained jurisdiction in a contract structure tend to assume the worst-case explanation by default — tax structuring designed to obscure something, or a sign that the vendor's actual operating model is murkier than the marketing suggests — and that assumption, even if wrong, is often enough to stall or kill a deal at the legal review stage, costing both sides a deal that would otherwise have been sound.

## What the Singapore Entity Actually Does, Precisely

The direct answer: Manifera's Singapore entity exists exclusively as an international contracting and invoicing layer. It is not a delivery hub, not an engineering location, not a second market Manifera sells into, and not a governance function. Engineering happens in Vietnam. Strategic governance, architecture sign-off, and client relationship ownership happen in Amsterdam. Singapore's entire function is procedural: it is the legal vehicle through which international client contracts are executed and invoices are issued for work that is governed in Amsterdam and delivered in Vietnam.

This structure exists for a specific, well-understood reason that has nothing to do with Manifera's business model and everything to do with how cross-border service contracting and banking function in practice. Routing international client contracts and invoicing through a Singapore-incorporated entity is a common and well-established practice among service businesses that govern from one jurisdiction (in Manifera's case, the Netherlands) and deliver from another (Vietnam) while serving clients across a third set of jurisdictions (the EU broadly). Singapore's corporate and banking infrastructure is specifically built to support exactly this kind of multi-jurisdictional contracting efficiently — it offers stable international banking rails, straightforward multi-currency invoicing, and a legal and corporate governance framework that is well recognized and predictable for cross-border commercial contracts. None of that is unique or unusual; it's a standard structural choice for agencies operating a Europe-governance, Asia-delivery model, chosen for the same reasons many international service businesses centralize invoicing and contracting through a jurisdiction built for that specific function.

To be precise about what this does not change: it does not change who is accountable for the work. Amsterdam retains full governance responsibility — architecture sign-off, quality accountability, and the client relationship itself are owned by the Dutch team regardless of which entity's name appears on the invoice. It does not change where the engineering happens — Vietnam remains the exclusive execution location, and the Singapore entity has no engineering staff, no delivery function, and no role in technical decision-making whatsoever. And it does not mean Manifera has any client base or market focus in Singapore or the wider APAC region — Manifera's client market is the Netherlands and the broader EU, full stop, and the Singapore entity's client-facing role is limited entirely to the contracting and invoicing mechanics for those EU clients.

For a CFO's due diligence purposes, the practical questions to ask about a structure like this are the ones that actually matter: does the Singapore entity have contractual privity with the client in a way that's clearly documented (yes — the master services agreement names it as the contracting party for invoicing and payment purposes specifically), does the governance and quality accountability sit elsewhere and is that clearly stated in the same contract (yes — the agreement should explicitly identify Amsterdam's role in architecture and quality sign-off, separate from the invoicing entity), and is the arrangement transparent rather than something a CFO had to discover independently (it should be disclosed plainly in the proposal and contracting documents, not something legal review uncovers as a surprise). A structure that answers all three of those clearly is a procedural convenience. A structure that can't or won't answer them clearly is the actual red flag — and the distinction has nothing to do with Singapore being involved at all.

The reassurance a CFO should take from this explainer isn't "trust us, it's fine" — it's that the structure is narrow, disclosed, and functionally separable from the two things that actually matter in an offshore engagement: who governs the work, and who builds it. Singapore does neither. It processes the paperwork so the euros move cleanly from a European client's accounts payable to a Vietnam-delivered engagement governed from Amsterdam.

## The Economics

An unexplained jurisdiction in a contract structure costs a CFO time, not money, directly — but that time cost is real: legal review typically adds one to three weeks to a contract cycle when a structural question isn't pre-answered, and in a meaningful share of cases the unresolved question stalls the deal entirely while both sides wait for clarity that should have been in the original proposal. A disclosed, precisely explained structure — like Singapore's narrow contracting role here — collapses that review cycle because the CFO's legal team is confirming a stated fact rather than investigating an unstated one.

The right CFO instinct when encountering a multi-jurisdiction structure isn't automatic suspicion — it's asking for the specific, checkable answer to what each entity does, and treating a clear answer as a green light rather than assuming complexity always means obfuscation. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO encountering the Singapore entity for the first time during contract review) Does the Singapore entity mean Manifera has an engineering or delivery team based in Singapore?

No. Singapore has no engineering staff and no delivery function. All engineering happens in Vietnam, and all governance and architecture sign-off happens in Amsterdam. Singapore's role is limited to international contracting and invoicing.

### (Scenario: CFO wondering if this signals a shift toward an APAC client market) Does the Singapore entity mean Manifera is expanding into or focusing on the Singapore or APAC market?

No. Manifera's client market is the Netherlands and the broader EU. The Singapore entity's client-facing role is limited to processing contracts and invoices for those EU clients — it is not a separate market Manifera sells into.

### (Scenario: CFO asking why Singapore specifically was chosen for this function) Why is Singapore used for contracting and invoicing rather than a Dutch or Vietnamese entity handling it directly?

Singapore offers stable multi-jurisdictional banking and invoicing infrastructure well suited to a business that governs from one country and delivers from another while serving clients across a third region — a common structural choice for this type of cross-border service model.

### (Scenario: CFO checking whether governance accountability shifts with the invoicing entity) Does routing invoicing through Singapore change who is contractually accountable for delivery quality?

No. The master services agreement should explicitly state that Amsterdam holds architecture and quality accountability regardless of which entity issues the invoice — the two functions are contractually separate.

### (Scenario: CFO deciding how to document this for their own internal audit or board) What should we ask for in writing to document this structure for our own compliance file?

A written structural breakdown, ideally referenced directly in the master services agreement, stating Singapore's role as contracting/invoicing only, Amsterdam's role as governance/accountability, and Vietnam's role as the exclusive engineering location.
