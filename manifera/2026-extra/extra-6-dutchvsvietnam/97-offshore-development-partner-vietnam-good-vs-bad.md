What if every offshore development partner a VP of Engineering shortlists in Vietnam claims "senior engineers" and "strong technical culture," and the only way to actually tell them apart is to ask the one question none of them volunteer an answer to?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering evaluating offshore development services in Vietnam has three vendors on the shortlist, each with a polished deck, comparable day rates, and near-identical claims about engineer seniority and process maturity. The differentiators that would actually predict engagement quality — technical depth, retention, and governance — are nowhere in the sales materials, because they're harder to fake and vendors know it.

**The Agitation:** Choosing between look-alike vendors on price alone, absent the criteria that actually matter, is how a VP of Engineering ends up with a pod that looks fine in the kickoff call and unravels by month four — junior engineers billed at mid-level rates, high attrition erasing institutional knowledge sprint over sprint, and a vendor that has no answer when a senior architectural question actually comes up. Replacing a failed vietnam software development engagement mid-project routinely costs €50,000-€90,000 in redone work and lost roadmap time, on top of the original spend that produced little of lasting value.

## The Signals That Actually Separate Good From Bad

The first genuine differentiator is engineer retention, and it's almost never asked about directly. A vietnam software development vendor with strong retention — engineers staying with the company multiple years, not rotating through in months — signals a healthy internal culture, competitive compensation, and real career development, all of which correlate directly with code quality and institutional continuity on your project. A vendor with high attrition will avoid a direct answer to "what's your average engineer tenure," because the honest number undermines the pitch. Ask it plainly, and ask for it by team, not company-wide, since company-wide averages can hide a churning junior bench behind a stable senior core.

The second differentiator is how technical leadership is actually assigned, not just described. A strong offshore development partner assigns a named tech lead to your pod before the engagement starts — someone with the seniority to push back on a client's technical request when it's genuinely wrong, not just execute whatever is asked. A weaker vendor treats "tech lead" as a rotating title assigned after the fact to whoever's available, which shows up later as a pod that implements requirements literally, including the ones that should have been questioned.

The third differentiator is verifiable technical depth in the specific domain your project needs, not generic full-stack competence. Vietnam's software development talent pool has grown substantially, but depth is unevenly distributed — strong in web and mobile application development, more variable in specialized domains like real-time systems, regulated-industry compliance work, or complex data engineering. A VP of Engineering should ask for evidence specific to the domain at hand — named prior projects, technical interview access to the actual proposed team, not a generic "we can do that" — because a vendor eager to win the deal will claim broad capability regardless of whether the depth is really there.

The fourth differentiator, and the one that ties the other three together, is whether governance sits above the delivery team as an independent check, or whether the same organization that's selling you the engagement is also the only source of truth on how it's going. A vendor with a genuine separation between commercial/governance functions and day-to-day delivery has a structural incentive to surface problems early, because the governance layer's credibility depends on accuracy, not on making the current sprint look good. A vendor without that separation is, functionally, grading its own homework.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering comparing near-identical vendor pitch decks) How do we tell a genuinely strong Vietnam offshore vendor apart from one that just sounds strong?

Ask about engineer retention by team, how technical leadership is assigned, verifiable domain-specific depth for your project's needs, and whether governance is independent of the delivery organization. These four signals rarely appear in a pitch deck.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about undisclosed staffing changes mid-project) How do we avoid discovering engineers have rotated off our project after the fact?

Require staffing-change disclosure as a contractual term, with any substitution communicated and approved before it happens rather than discovered in a later standup.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering evaluating a vendor's claimed technical depth) How do we verify a vendor's claimed expertise in our specific technical domain?

Ask for named prior projects in that specific domain and request direct technical interview access to the actual proposed team members, not a generic capability statement from sales.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering wondering why engineer retention matters so much) Why does engineer retention matter more than the day rate when evaluating a vendor?

High retention correlates directly with institutional continuity, code quality, and reduced rework, while a low headline rate built on high attrition often costs more in the long run through repeated re-onboarding and lost context.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering unsure how to check for independent governance) How do we know if a vendor's governance is actually independent of its delivery team?

Ask who reviews delivery progress and quality separately from the engineers doing the work, and whether that reviewer has authority to flag problems to you directly rather than through the same reporting chain as the delivery team.
