If your offshore engagement has no accountable party under Dutch or EU jurisdiction, who exactly is liable the day a delivery failure costs you a client contract — and where do you actually serve the claim?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a Netherlands or EU-based company has approved the budget for an offshore dedicated development team, but the finance and legal review that follows raises an uncomfortable question: the contract sits entirely with an entity in a jurisdiction with no meaningful enforcement relationship to the company's own legal exposure. The engineering team may be excellent — but nobody signed off on who's accountable if it isn't.

**The Agitation:** An offshore engagement without a governance layer under recognizable jurisdiction turns every delivery risk into an unrecoverable cost. When a vendor with no EU-accountable entity misses a critical delivery or ships a security flaw, a CFO's practical recourse is limited to a contract dispute in a foreign jurisdiction — a process that routinely costs €30,000-€60,000 in legal fees alone before any resolution, with no guarantee of recovering the underlying loss, which for a missed compliance deadline or a breached SLA can run into the hundreds of thousands.

## Why the Governance Layer Is a Risk Instrument, Not a Marketing Line

CFOs evaluating offshore engagements tend to focus first on cost — understandably, since cost arbitrage is the headline pitch. But the more consequential variable, and the one a properly skeptical CFO should scrutinize harder, is where accountability actually sits when something goes wrong. This is what a governance layer is: not a courtesy add-on, but a specific risk-transfer instrument.

The first dimension is contractual jurisdiction. An engagement contracted directly with an execution-only entity in the delivery country puts a CFO in the position of pursuing remedies through that country's courts or arbitration system — often slower, less predictable, and less familiar to EU legal counsel than a dispute resolved under Dutch or EU contract law. A structure where the commercial contract sits with an Amsterdam-headquartered entity, with execution subcontracted internally to the Vietnam team, keeps the CFO's legal recourse inside a framework their own counsel already understands.

The second dimension is quality accountability, distinct from legal accountability. A governance layer that reviews architecture, signs off on major releases, and owns SLA compliance creates a checkpoint between "the offshore team believes this is ready" and "this actually ships to production." Without that checkpoint, quality assurance is self-reported by the same entity whose commercial incentive is to report the work as complete. A CFO evaluating vendor risk should ask directly: who signs off on delivery quality, and are they the same party being paid for the delivery itself? If the answer is yes, there's no independent check in the structure.

The third dimension is data and IP governance under a recognizable regulatory framework. For any Netherlands or EU company handling customer data — which is most B2B software companies given GDPR's reach — a CFO needs assurance that data handling practices, subprocessor agreements, and IP assignment are governed under a framework their compliance function can actually audit. An Amsterdam-based governance layer that manages GDPR-compliant data processing agreements and maintains IP assignment under Dutch law gives a CFO an answer that survives a compliance review, rather than a promise that a foreign entity says the right words about data protection without a framework enforcing them.

The fourth dimension is financial continuity risk. A dedicated pod contracted directly with a smaller execution-only entity carries counterparty risk that a CFO's own risk framework should flag — what happens to a multi-year engagement if that entity has financial difficulty. A structure backed by an established Amsterdam-headquartered company with its own balance sheet and reputation at stake provides materially more counterparty stability than a standalone execution shop with no governance parent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO reviewing an existing offshore contract for jurisdiction risk) Why does contract jurisdiction matter if the engineering work itself is good?

Because good engineering doesn't protect you when a dispute arises over scope, IP, or a missed SLA — at that point, the contract's jurisdiction determines how fast and how predictably you can seek remedy, and a foreign delivery-country jurisdiction is typically slower and less familiar to EU counsel.

### (Scenario: CFO auditing GDPR compliance across vendor contracts) Does an offshore dedicated team create GDPR exposure?

It can, if the data processing agreement doesn't specifically document the offshore team as a GDPR-compliant subprocessor. A properly governed engagement includes this documentation as standard, auditable by your own compliance function.

### (Scenario: CFO assessing counterparty risk in a multi-year engagement) What happens to our engagement if the execution vendor has financial trouble?

This is precisely the risk a governance layer mitigates — an engagement backed by an established, Amsterdam-headquartered entity carries materially less counterparty risk than one contracted directly with a smaller, standalone execution shop.

### (Scenario: CFO questioning who verifies delivery quality) Who checks that delivered work actually meets the agreed standard, if not the CFO personally?

An independent Amsterdam-based governance function reviews architecture and signs off on releases, separate from the execution team being paid for the work, so quality assessment isn't self-reported by the party with a commercial incentive to call it done.

### (Scenario: CFO preparing for a board-level risk review) What's the fastest way to close a governance gap in an existing offshore engagement?

Restructuring the commercial contract under an EU-jurisdiction entity and formalizing a GDPR-compliant data processing agreement typically closes the most material gaps within a single quarter, without requiring the underlying execution team to change.
