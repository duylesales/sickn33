Any vendor can show you a portfolio of shipped apps — so how does a VP of Engineering tell the difference between a team that shipped one release and disappeared, and one that's still maintaining what it built two years later?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a Netherlands-based scale-up is evaluating Vietnam-based vendors for a native iOS and Android build, and every portfolio looks impressive at a glance — polished screenshots, recognizable app categories, confident case studies. What none of the portfolios show is release cadence after launch, crash-rate history, or how the team actually handled a rejected App Store submission or a platform version upgrade that broke something in production.

**The Agitation:** Mobile-specific execution risk is different from general software risk, and it surfaces in ways a portfolio review doesn't catch: an app that passes internal QA but gets rejected at App Store review, a crash rate that spikes after an OS update the team didn't test against, or a push-notification implementation that silently violates a platform's data policies. Fixing a post-launch mobile quality issue after users have already downloaded a broken release costs a company both engineering time — typically €20,000–€50,000 in emergency remediation — and app-store rating damage that takes months of good releases to repair.

## What Actually Predicts Reliable Mobile Execution From a Vietnam-Based Pod

Evaluating offshore mobile app development execution requires checking for signals specific to mobile, not just the general offshore vendor diligence that applies to any software build. A VP of Engineering who only asks general questions — team size, process, communication — will miss the mobile-specific failure modes that actually determine whether a release goes smoothly.

The first mobile-specific signal is platform release history, not just portfolio screenshots. Ask for apps the vendor has taken through multiple release cycles — not just a single initial launch — because ongoing App Store and Play Store maintenance reveals whether the team understands review guidelines, handles rejected submissions competently, and keeps pace with OS version updates. A team that's only ever shipped a v1.0 hasn't demonstrated the harder skill: keeping an app healthy through iOS and Android's constant platform evolution.

The second is native platform depth versus cross-platform-only capability. Many offshore vendors default to React Native or Flutter regardless of project needs, because it's a faster path to a working demo — which is fine when cross-platform genuinely fits the use case, and a real liability when the app needs native performance, deep hardware integration, or platform-specific UX patterns the framework can't cleanly deliver. Ask specifically whether the vendor has genuine native Swift/SwiftUI and Kotlin depth, not just cross-platform fluency, and probe which they'd recommend for your specific app and why — a vendor defaulting to cross-platform without asking about your requirements is optimizing for their delivery speed, not your outcome.

The third is crash-rate and performance testing discipline. A reliable mobile pod tests against a defined device and OS-version matrix before release, not just "it worked on the developer's phone." Ask what device lab or testing service the vendor uses, how they handle the long tail of older Android OS versions and device fragmentation specifically, and what their pre-release QA checklist looks like for memory profiling and battery impact — issues that don't show up in a quick demo but destroy app-store ratings within weeks of a real launch.

The fourth is data and SDK compliance awareness, which matters more for a Netherlands or EU-based buyer than it might for a vendor's other markets. Mobile apps routinely integrate analytics, crash-reporting, and push-notification SDKs that each carry their own data-handling implications under GDPR. A vendor with genuine EU-market experience will flag these implications proactively during architecture design, rather than leaving a Netherlands-based buyer to discover a compliance gap after an SDK is already integrated and shipping.

The fifth is post-launch support structure — what actually happens after release day. Mobile apps need ongoing maintenance in a way many web apps don't: OS updates break things on a predictable annual cycle, and a pod that disappears after initial launch leaves the buyer exposed at exactly the point a fix is most urgent. Ask specifically about post-launch SLA terms and whether the same pod that built the app stays engaged for maintenance, or whether support gets handed to a different, less-familiar team.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering comparing mobile vendor portfolios) How do we tell if a vendor's portfolio reflects real ongoing capability, not just a one-time launch?

Ask specifically for apps the vendor has maintained through multiple release cycles, not just initial launches, and request crash-rate or rating history if the vendor has access to it — ongoing maintenance is the harder, more revealing skill.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering deciding between native and cross-platform for a new build) Should our app be native or cross-platform, and how do we know the vendor isn't just defaulting to whichever is easier for them?

Ask the vendor to justify their recommendation against your specific performance and hardware-integration needs. A vendor with genuine native depth will recommend cross-platform when it fits and native when it doesn't — not default to one regardless of your requirements.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about crash rates and device fragmentation) How does a reliable vendor handle Android's device and OS-version fragmentation?

By testing against a defined device and OS-version matrix before every release, not just the latest flagship devices — ask to see the vendor's actual test matrix and QA checklist as part of diligence.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering concerned about GDPR exposure from mobile SDKs) Does adding analytics or push-notification SDKs to our app create GDPR exposure?

It can, depending on the SDK's data handling. A vendor with genuine EU-market experience flags this during architecture design and reviews SDK choices for compliance before integration, rather than after.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering planning for the first year after launch) What happens to our app's stability after launch, when the next OS update ships?

A reliable engagement includes post-launch maintenance SLA terms with the same pod that built the app staying engaged, so platform updates get tested and addressed proactively rather than discovered through user complaints.
