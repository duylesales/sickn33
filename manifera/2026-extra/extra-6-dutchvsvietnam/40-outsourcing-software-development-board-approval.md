Cost and risk aren't the only objections a European board raises to a Vietnam outsourcing budget — what happens when a director asks how this looks to the existing engineering team, or to the company's public narrative about where it invests?

**The Pain:** A CFO has built a sound financial case for engaging a software outsourcing company in Vietnam, only to discover that the board's hesitation isn't really about cost or delivery risk — it's about optics, employee relations, and precedent. Will existing engineers feel threatened. Will investors ask uncomfortable questions about outsourcing at a company that markets itself on European engineering talent. Has anyone on this board approved something like this before, and how did it actually go.

**The Agitation:** These softer objections kill more outsourcing proposals than a bad financial model does, precisely because they're harder to answer with a spreadsheet — and a CFO who shows up prepared only for the numbers conversation gets caught flat-footed by the people conversation, resulting in a proposal that gets shelved not because it was wrong, but because it wasn't framed to survive the actual questions in the room. A shelved proposal at this stage typically costs a full budget cycle, six months or more, before the same case gets a second hearing.

## The Questions Behind the Cost Question

Beyond the standard IP, accountability, and financial-model questions any board raises about an outsourcing budget, European boards specifically — more so than boards in markets less shaped by works councils, strong employment protections, and a cultural sensitivity around job displacement — raise three additional questions a CFO needs to answer credibly.

The first is employee relations: does building an offshore development team team signal to the existing in-house engineering staff that their jobs are at risk, and does that signal, even if unintended, damage retention and morale enough to offset the outsourcing savings. The credible answer here isn't a denial — it's a structural one: framing and actually implementing the engagement as additive capacity for defined new workstreams, not a replacement plan for existing roles, communicated transparently to the in-house team before the board vote rather than after, with the roadmap classification work (which workstreams move offshore, which stay local) documented and shared internally. Boards respond far better to "here's exactly what moves and what doesn't, and the team already knows" than to a vague reassurance offered defensively after a director raises the concern.

The second is reputational and narrative risk, particularly relevant for companies that market technical capability as part of their brand — a fintech emphasizing engineering rigor, a healthtech emphasizing clinical-software quality. The credible answer is that the governance structure itself is the narrative: an Amsterdam-based architecture and quality layer means the company's engineering standards don't change, only the execution location does, and a CFO who can describe the governance model specifically — not just assert "quality is maintained" — gives the board a defensible answer for any external question about the arrangement.

The third is precedent and comparables: has this worked at a company the board actually respects, in a comparable regulatory or market context, and what went wrong when it didn't. This is where a CFO benefits from having specific, citable comparable cases — not abstract industry statistics, but a named company, in a comparable EU jurisdiction, with a specific outcome — because boards are, structurally, more persuaded by "this worked at a comparable company under comparable governance" than by an aggregate market statistic.

A CFO who pre-empts all three of these questions — employee relations framing, reputational narrative, and specific comparables — alongside the standard financial and risk case, converts a proposal that might get tabled for "more discussion" into one the board can approve in a single sitting, because every objection that would otherwise surface mid-meeting has already been addressed in the deck.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Warsaw Fintech's Employee-Relations Objection, Resolved

Płatność Cyfrowa S.A., a Warsaw-based payments fintech, had a Vietnam outsourcing proposal stall at the board level after a director raised concern about how the existing twenty-person Warsaw engineering team would react, and whether the move undercut the company's public positioning around "engineered in Poland" technical talent — a phrase used in its own investor materials.

The CFO worked with Manifera's Amsterdam team to restructure the proposal around additive capacity only: a defined new workstream — a merchant-onboarding module the in-house team didn't have capacity for — routed to a Vietnam pod, with the classification work shared transparently with the Warsaw team before the board vote, and the governance narrative describing exactly how Amsterdam oversight preserved the company's quality standard. The board approved the revised proposal in a single sitting, and the Warsaw team's engagement survey scores showed no measurable dip following the announcement.
