Ask a CTO why they haven't hired the four senior engineers the roadmap needs, and the honest answer is rarely "we can't afford the salaries" — it's that the last two senior hires took five months each, and the roadmap can't wait five months twice.

**The Pain:** A CTO at a Dutch scale-up has been asked to model two paths for scaling the engineering org: hiring in-house across Amsterdam, Utrecht, or Rotterdam, versus engaging a Vietnam software development company. The in-house path has an obvious appeal — direct control, cultural fit, no communication gap — but the CFO is asking for numbers, and the in-house numbers are harder to pin down than they first appear.

**The Agitation:** The Dutch senior engineering talent market is genuinely tight, and pretending otherwise in a board model is how a roadmap commitment quietly slips a full quarter. A single senior engineering hire in the Netherlands currently runs a fully-loaded cost of €95,000–€130,000 annually including employer contributions, and the average time-to-fill for a senior role in Amsterdam or Rotterdam sits between 14 and 20 weeks — meaning a CTO who commits to an in-house hiring plan in a board deck is implicitly committing to a roadmap that doesn't actually start moving for four to five months per hire.

## The Comparison a Spreadsheet Alone Won't Capture

A naive comparison puts Dutch salary against Vietnam day rate and calls it done. That comparison is real but incomplete — it misses three structural factors that determine whether either path actually delivers the roadmap on time, and a CTO building a board-ready model needs all three.

The first factor is time-to-productive-capacity, not time-to-hire. A Dutch in-house hire needs roughly 14-20 weeks to source and close, then another 4-8 weeks to reach full productivity depending on seniority and domain complexity — meaning a CTO who starts hiring today is realistically 5-7 months from a fully productive new engineer. A governed Vietnam software development company pod, by contrast, can typically reach productive capacity in 2-4 weeks against a documented ramp plan, because the pod's tech lead and process are already established before the engagement starts — the codebase onboarding is the only new variable, not team formation itself.

The second factor is scaling elasticity. An in-house team is a fixed cost that's expensive to scale down — Dutch employment law makes reducing headcount a materially slower and more expensive process than in many other markets, which means a CTO who over-hires against an optimistic roadmap is carrying that cost through the inevitable correction. A Vietnam-based pod structured with contractual capacity flex can scale from three engineers to seven and back down within a matter of weeks, which matters enormously for a scale-up whose roadmap depends on landing a specific enterprise contract or funding milestone that isn't guaranteed.

The third factor, and the one most often missed in a pure cost model, is where senior architectural judgment actually sits. An in-house Dutch team has an obvious advantage here by default — the senior engineers are the CTO's direct reports, embedded in company context daily. A genuinely comparable offshore alternative has to replicate that judgment layer deliberately, which is exactly what a governance structure is for: architecture sign-off and technical direction owned by senior people with visibility into the company's full context, sitting close enough — in Amsterdam, inside a Dutch business and legal framework — to function almost like an internal function rather than an external vendor relationship.

Put together, the honest framing for a CTO's board deck isn't "in-house is better but expensive" versus "offshore is cheaper but riskier." It's that a purely in-house model trades cost predictability for hiring-timeline risk and headcount inflexibility, while a purely ungoverned offshore model trades cost for architectural continuity risk — and a governed hybrid structure is specifically designed to capture the cost advantage of the second path while replicating the architectural judgment advantage of the first.

A CTO who models only salary versus day rate misses the real cost driver: the roadmap quarter lost to a 14-20 week hiring cycle that a board deck usually assumes away. At a fully-loaded senior cost of €95,000-€130,000 per Dutch hire and a typical time-to-productive-capacity of five to seven months, the real cost of an all-in-house scaling plan includes months of unmet roadmap commitments that a governed offshore pod, reaching productive capacity in weeks, simply doesn't carry. ### (Scenario: CTO worried about losing architectural control by going offshore) Does engaging a Vietnam software development company mean giving up architectural control?

Not under a governed structure. Amsterdam-based technical leadership retains architecture sign-off and roadmap alignment authority, functioning similarly to an internal engineering leader rather than an external vendor relationship.

### (Scenario: CTO considering running in-house hiring and offshore delivery simultaneously) Can we run in-house hiring and a Vietnam pod at the same time rather than choosing one?

Yes, and it's frequently the strongest structure — the pod covers immediate roadmap gaps while in-house hiring proceeds on its natural timeline, then the pod scales down once new hires reach full productivity.

Reducing in-house headcount in the Netherlands is a materially slower and more expensive process than adjusting external pod capacity, which means an in-house plan carries real cost if roadmap assumptions change after hiring.
