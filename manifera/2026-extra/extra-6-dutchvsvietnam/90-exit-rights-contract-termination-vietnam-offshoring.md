Every offshore contract negotiation focuses entirely on how the relationship starts — onboarding timeline, sprint cadence, rate card — and almost none of them spend serious time on how it ends, which is exactly backwards, because the terms nobody negotiated at signing are the ones a CFO discovers were inadequate at the worst possible moment.

**The Pain:** A CFO negotiating a Vietnam offshore software development engagement focuses, reasonably, on getting the engagement started well — rates, team composition, delivery timelines. Exit terms get relegated to standard boilerplate, on the assumption that termination is a remote scenario not worth negotiating hard for. It isn't remote, and the cost of inadequate exit terms only becomes visible when the company actually needs to invoke them.

**The Agitation:** A contract without clearly negotiated exit rights turns an ordinary vendor transition — a scenario that happens to a meaningful share of offshore engagements within three years for entirely normal reasons, a strategic pivot, an internal reorg, dissatisfaction with delivery — into a genuine operational crisis. Companies that hit contract termination without pre-negotiated handover terms report transition costs of €60,000-€140,000 in emergency knowledge-recovery, code archaeology, and productivity loss during the gap before a replacement team becomes productive, costs that are almost entirely avoidable with exit terms negotiated calmly before signing rather than negotiated under pressure during an actual termination.

## What Actually Needs to Be Negotiated Before Signing, Not During Termination

A CFO evaluating an offshore software development company's standard contract should specifically look for four exit-related provisions, because a vendor's standard terms are written to protect the vendor's transition costs, not the client's, unless the client negotiates otherwise.

The first provision is a defined transition period with specified obligations, not a vague "reasonable cooperation" clause. The contract should specify a minimum notice period, a required knowledge-transfer timeline, and named deliverables during that period — documentation handover, a defined number of live knowledge-transfer sessions with the incoming team or internal staff, and continued access to the codebase and infrastructure during the transition window, not cut off the day termination is invoked.

The second provision is documentation-as-you-go as a standing contractual obligation, not a termination-triggered scramble. A vendor that only produces comprehensive documentation once termination is invoked is producing it under time pressure, from institutional memory that's about to walk out the door, which produces materially worse documentation than the same work done incrementally throughout the engagement. The contract should specify documentation as an ongoing sprint deliverable, verified periodically, so a transition doesn't depend on a rushed final effort.

The third provision is source code and infrastructure access continuity — specifically, that the client's ownership and access to source-controlled repositories, cloud infrastructure credentials, and third-party service accounts is never contingent on the vendor relationship remaining active. A structure where the vendor entity retains any credential or access control that could be withheld during a contentious termination is a structure that hands the vendor real leverage in exactly the scenario where the client needs none.

The fourth provision is a defined dispute-resolution path specifically for transition disagreements, separate from the general contract dispute clause, since disagreements about what counts as adequate handover are common and benefit from a faster resolution mechanism than a full commercial dispute process.

None of these four provisions cost meaningfully more to negotiate into a contract at signing. All four cost significantly more to negotiate — or fail to negotiate — during an actual termination, when the vendor's incentive to cooperate has already changed.

## The Economics

Negotiating exit terms at signing costs a CFO one additional contract-review conversation, typically adding no meaningful time to the negotiation timeline since the terms are standard asks a reasonable vendor should already support. Discovering inadequate exit terms during an actual termination costs whatever the knowledge-recovery effort turns out to require — commonly €60,000-€140,000 in delayed roadmap work, duplicated reconstruction effort, and productivity loss during the gap before a replacement team becomes fully productive.

A CFO reviewing any offshore development contract, current or prospective, should specifically check whether transition period, documentation obligations, and infrastructure ownership are addressed with defined terms rather than general language.
