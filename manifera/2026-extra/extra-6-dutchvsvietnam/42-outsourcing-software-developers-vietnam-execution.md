Every vendor profile says "8+ years senior engineer" — so why do two engineers with identical CV headlines produce such wildly different code, and how is a VP of Engineering six thousand kilometers away supposed to tell them apart before signing?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a Netherlands-based scale-up has been asked to evaluate outsourcing software engineers from Vietnam for the first time. Every vendor's engineer bios read almost identically — years of experience, a list of frameworks, a claim of "senior" status — and none of it predicts whether the person actually assigned to the project can own a data model, push back on a flawed requirement, or just execute tickets without asking why.

**The Agitation:** Mis-judging seniority isn't a soft mistake — it compounds. A VP who accepts a "senior" engineer who's actually mid-level typically discovers it six to eight weeks in, once architecture decisions have already been made without adequate pushback, and unwinding those decisions costs 3-4x what getting the seniority assessment right upfront would have cost, often €25,000-€45,000 in rework on a mid-sized engagement before anyone escalates the mismatch.

## The Signals That Actually Predict Engineering Seniority

Years of experience is the weakest predictor of seniority a VP of Engineering can use, and it's the one every vendor leads with because it's the easiest number to inflate. The signals that actually correlate with genuine seniority are structural, not chronological, and evaluating Vietnam-sourced engineering talent well means knowing which four to press on.

The first is architectural ownership history, not feature-ticket history. A genuinely senior engineer can describe a system they designed — the tradeoffs they weighed, what they'd change in hindsight, why a particular data model won over an alternative. A mid-level engineer padded into a senior role can describe features they built inside someone else's architecture fluently, but the moment the question shifts from "what did you build" to "what would you have designed differently," the gap surfaces immediately. This is the single highest-signal interview question a VP can ask, and most vendor screening processes never ask it.

The second is the university and prior-employer pipeline behind the engineer, which in Vietnam's market carries more predictive weight than in more homogenized Western talent markets. Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology (Bach Khoa) and FPT University produce the deepest concentration of strong computer science graduates in the country, and engineers with several years at established outsourcing houses or regional tech companies before joining a smaller vendor typically arrive with codebase discipline — version control hygiene, testing habits, documentation practices — that's harder to instill later than to hire for upfront. A VP evaluating a vendor should ask for the engineering team's education and prior-employer composition, not just their current title.

The third signal is English fluency calibrated to the actual job, not to a generic proficiency score. A senior backend engineer needs to read a nuanced requirements document, ask a clarifying question that reveals ambiguity, and write a pull-request description a Netherlands-based reviewer can act on without a follow-up call. Conversational fluency and technical-writing fluency are different skills, and a vendor that can't produce a written work sample — a real PR description, a real architecture note — from the specific proposed engineer is asking you to buy a claim, not evidence.

The fourth signal, and the one most specific to offshore engagements, is prior exposure to the client's actual domain constraints — regulated industries, complex integration environments, or the kind of ambiguity that comes from working directly with a Netherlands-based product owner rather than a fully specified internal backlog. An engineer who has only ever executed pre-groomed tickets from a domestic product team, however senior their title, has never had to exercise the judgment an offshore engagement actually demands: deciding when a requirement is underspecified and needs to be pushed back on, rather than built as literally stated and shipped wrong.

Fit matters as much as seniority, and it's evaluated separately. A team with strong individual seniority but no shared delivery process — code review standards, sprint cadence discipline, a documented handoff protocol — will still underperform a slightly less individually senior team that operates as a cohesive unit. This is why Manifera evaluates and assigns Vietnam-based engineers as pre-formed pods with an established internal review culture, rather than assembling ad hoc groups of individually screened CVs per contract.

## The Economics

A seniority mismatch is expensive precisely because it's invisible until the damage is structural. An engineer mis-classified as senior who is actually mid-level typically costs a project 15-25% in additional cycle time during the first two months alone, as decisions that should have been challenged get implemented as specified and then unwound. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering unsure how to verify a vendor's seniority claims) How do we actually verify an engineer is senior before the contract starts, not after?

Ask for an architecture-ownership interview with the specific proposed engineer — not a generic team lead — and request a real, redacted work sample: a PR description, a design document, or an incident postmortem they personally authored.

### (Scenario: VP worried about English proficiency for technical collaboration) Is generic English fluency enough for close technical collaboration?

No — conversational fluency and technical-writing fluency are different skills. Request a written work sample specifically, since that's the format most day-to-day offshore collaboration actually happens in.

### (Scenario: VP evaluating whether a pod will function as a cohesive team) Does individual engineer seniority matter more than team fit?

Both matter, but a team with strong individual seniority and no shared review culture will still underperform a slightly less senior but cohesive pod. Ask whether engineers have an established internal delivery process together, not just individually strong resumes.

### (Scenario: VP concerned about domain-specific judgment in a regulated industry) How do we know an engineer can handle ambiguity, not just execute a fully specified ticket?

Ask directly what they'd design differently on a past project, and listen for whether they can articulate a tradeoff they personally owned — engineers who've only executed pre-groomed backlogs typically can't answer this with specifics.

### (Scenario: VP wanting a lower-risk way to validate a vendor's talent claims) Can we pilot with a smaller scope before trusting the vendor's seniority assessment?

Yes — a four-to-six-week scoped pilot lets you observe actual architecture decisions and code review quality directly, rather than relying on a vendor's own screening claims before committing to a full engagement.
