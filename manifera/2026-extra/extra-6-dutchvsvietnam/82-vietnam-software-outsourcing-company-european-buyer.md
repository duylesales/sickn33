## The Delivery-Process Criteria That Actually Separate Vendors

Most European buyers evaluate a Vietnam software outsourcing company on inputs — team size, tech stack coverage, years in business. Those inputs correlate weakly with outcomes. What predicts whether an engagement holds up under pressure is delivery process architecture, and there are four elements of it worth interrogating specifically before signing.

The first is incident escalation ownership. Ask the vendor to walk through, step by step, what happens from the moment a production issue is detected to the moment it's resolved — who gets paged, on what SLA, and who has authority to make a rollback decision without waiting for client sign-off during a genuine emergency. A vendor that can't produce a written escalation matrix with named roles (not just "our team monitors 24/7") is telling you that structure gets improvised in the moment, which is the worst possible time to improvise.

The second is quality assurance independence. In a well-structured outsourcing engagement, the people testing the code are organizationally separate from the people who wrote it, with defined acceptance criteria agreed before a sprint starts, not negotiated after delivery. Many Vietnam-based outsourcing shops fold QA into the development pod itself, which quietly incentivizes marking tickets "done" rather than "correct." A European VP of Engineering should ask specifically whether QA reports to the same tech lead as development, or to an independent quality function — the answer changes what "done" actually means on every sprint report.

The third criterion is documentation discipline, which matters disproportionately more in an outsourced relationship than an in-house one because there's no hallway conversation to fall back on when something is unclear. A capable IT outsourcing company in Vietnam should produce architecture decision records, API documentation, and runbooks as a sprint deliverable, not as an afterthought requested during offboarding. The test is simple: ask to see a redacted sample of documentation from an existing client engagement. A vendor with nothing to show has nothing built into their process.

The fourth criterion is how the vendor handles scope disagreement. Fixed-price outsourcing contracts frequently break down not because the vendor is dishonest but because "done" was never defined precisely enough, and the two sides discover the gap only when the client says "that's not what I meant" and the vendor says "that's exactly what the ticket said." The right structure is a change-control process defined in the contract itself — how a scope disagreement gets raised, estimated, and either absorbed or priced as a change order — agreed before the first sprint, not negotiated mid-dispute.

A European VP of Engineering who pressure-tests these four process elements before signing will learn more about a Vietnam software outsourcing company in one call than a stack of reference logos will tell them in a month of vetting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about who has authority during a production incident) Who should have rollback authority during a production emergency in an outsourced engagement?

A named technical lead on the vendor side should hold pre-agreed rollback authority within defined boundaries, so a genuine emergency doesn't stall for hours waiting on a client sign-off that could have been pre-authorized in the contract.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering evaluating whether QA is genuinely independent) Should QA report to the same person as the development team?

No. Independent QA reporting, separate from the development pod's tech lead, removes the incentive to mark tickets complete rather than correct, and gives a more honest defect-rate signal each sprint.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering assessing documentation practices before signing) How do we verify a vendor's documentation discipline before signing a contract?

Ask to see a redacted sample of architecture decision records or runbooks from an existing client engagement. A vendor with genuine documentation discipline will have something to show; one without will improvise an answer.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering concerned about fixed-price scope disputes) How do we prevent "that's not what I meant" disputes on a fixed-price outsourcing contract?

Define a change-control process in the contract itself before the first sprint — how a scope disagreement gets raised, estimated, and either absorbed or priced — rather than negotiating the definition of "done" after delivery.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering deciding how to structure emergency coverage across time zones) Can a Vietnam-based team provide meaningful coverage outside their normal working hours?

Yes, when the contract specifies a defined emergency-response SLA and a European governance contact reachable for genuine incidents, rather than assuming standard business-hours coverage will stretch to cover every scenario.
