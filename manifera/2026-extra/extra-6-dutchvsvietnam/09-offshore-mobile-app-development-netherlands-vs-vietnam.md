Two proposals are on your desk — one for a local mobile team, one for an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam pod — so why does neither document actually let you compare cost per shipped feature, not just cost per hour?

**The Pain:** A CTO at a Netherlands-based company has a board-approved budget and two competing mobile app development proposals, and is expected to recommend one this quarter. The local proposal quotes hourly and day rates. The Vietnam-based proposal quotes a monthly pod cost. Neither is normalized to the metric that actually matters — total cost to ship the defined feature set on the required timeline — and the CTO is the one who has to do that math manually before the decision meeting.

**The Agitation:** A mobile build decided on incomplete comparison tends to reveal its true cost mid-project, when it's most expensive to correct. Switching mobile development structure after a build is underway — re-onboarding a new team to an existing native codebase, re-establishing app-store account continuity, re-testing against a device matrix a new team hasn't validated — typically costs €50,000–€100,000 in lost time and rework on a mid-sized mobile build, roughly the same magnitude as the decision the CTO is trying to get right the first time.

## Normalizing the Comparison to What a Mobile Build Actually Costs

A CTO at the decision stage doesn't need a generic offshore-versus-local argument — that decision was likely made in principle already. What's needed is the specific, current-market numbers for mobile app development specifically, since mobile carries cost and risk dynamics that differ meaningfully from a general software build.

Start with specialist salary cost, which for mobile runs higher than general engineering due to the narrower talent pool. Senior native iOS and Android engineers in the Netherlands command €80,000–€100,000 gross annually — often above general full-stack salary bands specifically because of specialization scarcity — before 30%-ruling adjustments and employer overhead. A comparable native mobile pod under Amsterdam governance in Vietnam typically costs 50-65% less on a fully-loaded basis, while maintaining genuine native platform depth rather than defaulting to a cheaper cross-platform-only approach that might not fit the app's actual requirements.

Layer on the specialization scarcity effect on hiring timeline specifically. Where a general senior engineer search in the Netherlands might resolve in four to five months, a senior native iOS search specifically frequently stretches past six months, because the total addressable pool is smaller and every fintech, retailer, and scale-up in the Randstad is recruiting from the same narrow group. A governed Vietnam mobile pod, by contrast, is typically assembled and productive within two to four weeks — a gap that widens the total comparison well beyond the salary difference alone once the CTO's actual deadline is factored in.

Platform-specific delivery risk is the variable most local-versus-offshore comparisons omit entirely, and it matters for cost as much as for quality. A newly-assembled local team — even a competent one — is learning to work together on mobile-specific problems (device fragmentation testing, App Store review cycles, offline-sync architecture) at the same time it's building the app, which shows up as slower initial velocity and more post-launch defects than an established pod that has shipped multiple mobile releases together already. A governed offshore pod with a documented multi-release track record starts at a velocity and defect rate a newly-formed local team typically takes two to three months to reach.

App-store and post-launch maintenance cost is the final variable, and it's ongoing rather than one-time — which matters because a mobile app isn't "done" at launch the way a comparison sometimes implicitly assumes. Both local and offshore options need a maintenance plan for annual OS updates, and the comparison should include that ongoing cost, not just the initial build cost, because a cheap initial build with no maintenance plan becomes an expensive emergency the following year when an OS update breaks something nobody was retained to fix.

Put together, a fair mobile-specific comparison isn't just "Dutch salary versus Vietnam salary" — it's fully-loaded specialist cost, hiring-timeline risk weighted against the CTO's actual deadline, established-pod velocity versus newly-assembled-team ramp-up, and ongoing maintenance cost, all normalized to cost-per-shipped-feature rather than cost-per-hour.
