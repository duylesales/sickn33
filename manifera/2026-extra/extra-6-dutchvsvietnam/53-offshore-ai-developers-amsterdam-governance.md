If an offshore AI vendor can't name the single person accountable for a GDPR-relevant data-handling decision, who exactly is your CFO supposed to hold responsible when the auditor asks?

**The Pain:** A CFO at an EU-based company is being asked to approve budget for an offshore AI development engagement, and the finance and legal risk questions are piling up faster than the engineering team can answer them. Where does training or inference data physically sit? Who's accountable if a model output causes a compliance incident? What happens to IP and model artifacts if the vendor relationship ends? The engineering team's answer — "the vendor handles that" — is not an answer a CFO can put in a board memo.

**The Agitation:** Ungoverned offshore AI engagements are where financial exposure hides in the fine print. A data-handling misstep involving EU personal data can trigger GDPR fines calculated as a percentage of global turnover, and even short of a formal fine, a single compliance incident traced back to an offshore AI vendor typically costs €50,000-€200,000 in legal review, remediation, and reputational cleanup — money that dwarfs whatever was "saved" by choosing the cheapest vendor on the shortlist. The CFO who approves an ungoverned engagement owns that exposure personally in the eyes of the board, whether or not it was disclosed clearly at signing.

## Why Governance Has to Sit in an EU Jurisdiction, Not Offshore

The risk framework a CFO needs isn't "is Vietnam a safe place to build software" — it is, and the country has a mature, internationally-serving software industry. The actual risk framework is about where accountability legally and practically sits, and the answer has to be an EU-aligned jurisdiction for four specific categories of decision, because these are the categories that create real financial exposure if mishandled.

Data residency and processing agreements: any engagement touching EU personal data needs a Data Processing Agreement structured under GDPR, with clear terms on where data is processed, who has access, and what happens on contract termination. This has to be negotiated and owned by an entity operating inside an EU legal framework — not a delivery entity in a jurisdiction where GDPR enforcement has no direct reach.

Model evaluation and output accountability: when an AI system produces an output that causes harm — a wrong recommendation, a biased outcome, a hallucinated answer presented as fact — someone has to own the evaluation standard that was supposed to catch it, and that ownership needs to be traceable to a specific accountable party your legal team can name in an incident report, not a vendor entity three jurisdictions removed.

IP and model-artifact ownership: fine-tuned models, evaluation datasets, and internal tooling built during an engagement need contractual ownership terms that survive contract termination, structured under law your legal team can actually enforce, not a foreign jurisdiction's commercial code your general counsel has never litigated in.

Vendor financial stability and continuity: a CFO approving a multi-year AI engagement needs assurance the delivery organization itself is financially stable and won't dissolve mid-engagement, taking institutional knowledge with it — assurance that's meaningfully easier to diligence against a Netherlands-headquartered entity with an Amsterdam Chamber of Commerce registration than an offshore-only vendor.

The structural answer is a split model: governance, contracting, data processing agreements, and accountability sit with an Amsterdam-headquartered entity operating under Dutch and EU law, while the execution capacity — the actual engineering — sits in Vietnam. This isn't a compromise between cost and control; it's the only structure that gives a CFO both the cost profile that makes the initiative fundable and the accountability structure that makes it auditable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: CFO facing a compliance audit of an existing AI vendor) Our current AI vendor can't produce a proper Data Processing Agreement. How urgent is this?

Very. Absence of a GDPR-structured DPA is a live compliance gap, not a paperwork formality, and it's typically one of the first things an auditor or regulator asks for. It should be resolved before further data processing continues.

### (Scenario: CFO comparing jurisdictions for contracting) Why does the governance entity need to be Netherlands-based specifically, rather than just any EU country?

It doesn't strictly have to be the Netherlands, but Amsterdam gives EU-aligned buyers a jurisdiction their legal teams already understand and can enforce against, with the added advantage of being Manifera's own headquarters and accountability center.

### (Scenario: CFO worried about IP ownership after contract end) What happens to models and IP built during the engagement if we end the contract?

Contract terms should specify that model artifacts, fine-tuned weights, evaluation datasets, and internal tooling transfer to your organization on termination. Manifera structures this explicitly under Dutch/EU-enforceable contract law.

### (Scenario: CFO diligencing vendor financial stability) How do we verify an offshore AI vendor won't dissolve mid-engagement?

Diligence the governance entity, not just the delivery entity. A Netherlands Chamber of Commerce-registered company with a real operating history is materially easier to verify than an offshore-only vendor with no EU-jurisdiction presence.

### (Scenario: CFO wanting a single accountable contact for incidents) If something goes wrong in production, who do we actually call?

A named Amsterdam-based governance contact, not a rotating account manager. Manifera structures every engagement with a clear accountability chain so incident response never depends on tracking down whoever happens to be available offshore.
