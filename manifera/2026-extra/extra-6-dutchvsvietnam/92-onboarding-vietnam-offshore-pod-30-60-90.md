What if the vendor who told you "our onboarding takes about six to eight weeks" wasn't describing a limitation of offshore development team — they were describing the absence of a plan?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering has approved a new offshore dedicated development team in Vietnam and is now three weeks in, watching a Gantt chart that shows "environment setup" and "codebase familiarization" as the only completed milestones. No feature has shipped. The vendor calls this normal ramp-up. It isn't clear whether it actually is.

**The Agitation:** An offshore developers pod that takes 8-12 weeks to become genuinely productive has, by the time it starts shipping meaningfully, already consumed a full quarter of budget and roadmap time producing close to nothing. On a five-engineer pod at a typical blended offshore rate, that's €40,000-€60,000 spent before the VP of Engineering has a single shipped feature to show the board — and by the time anyone questions the timeline, the sunk cost makes switching vendors feel more expensive than waiting it out, which is exactly the trap.

## What a Genuinely Structured Ramp Looks Like, Day by Day

The difference between an 8-12 week ramp and a 2-4 week ramp is almost never engineer talent — it's whether onboarding was designed as a project with its own milestones, or left to happen organically while everyone hopes it converges. A properly structured Vietnam offshore pod treats the first 90 days as three distinct phases, each with a defined, measurable output.

Days 1-30 is access and orientation, and it should be aggressive. Codebase access, environment credentials, and architecture documentation should be delivered on day one, not requested on day one and granted in week two after a security review nobody scoped in advance. A well-run offshore developers team pairs each new engineer with a structured onboarding document — architecture overview, coding standards, deployment pipeline, domain glossary — prepared before the engineer's first day, not assembled reactively as questions come in. By the end of week one, an engineer should have made at least one small, reviewed commit to a non-critical part of the system, purely to validate that the environment, review process, and deployment pipeline all function end-to-end. By day 30, the pod should have completed its first full sprint cycle against real backlog items, even if velocity is still below steady-state — the milestone isn't speed, it's proof that the process works.

Days 31-60 is the calibration phase, where velocity should be visibly climbing sprint over sprint as engineers move from "understanding the system" to "contributing independently to it." This is where a structured ramp diverges hardest from an unstructured one: a good pod tracks velocity explicitly against a forecast curve agreed with the client at kickoff, so a VP of Engineering can see whether the pod is on pace or falling behind before it becomes a quarter-end surprise. A pod that can't produce that forecast, or resists being measured against it, is signaling that onboarding was never actually planned — it's being figured out live, on the client's clock.

Days 61-90 is the point at which a well-onboarded offshore dedicated development team should be operating at, or very close to, steady-state velocity — taking ownership of a defined product area, contributing to architectural discussions rather than just implementing tickets, and requiring materially less oversight from the client-side team than it did in week one. If a pod is still asking basic architecture questions at day 75 that should have been answered in the onboarding documentation at day 5, that's not a talent problem — it's a process failure that happened before the engineers ever touched the keyboard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering watching a new pod stall in week three) How long should it actually take for a Vietnam offshore pod to become productive?

A well-structured pod should complete a validated first commit within week one, a full sprint cycle by day 30, and reach steady-state velocity by day 60-90 — not the 8-12 weeks many unstructured engagements quietly treat as normal.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering unsure what "day one" should include) What should be ready before a new offshore developers pod's first day?

Codebase access, environment credentials, architecture documentation, coding standards, and a domain glossary should all be prepared and delivered before day one, not assembled reactively once questions start arriving.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering trying to measure ramp-up progress objectively) How do we know if our pod's ramp-up is actually on track?

Ask for an explicit velocity forecast curve agreed at kickoff, and review actual sprint velocity against it at each 30-day checkpoint. A vendor that can't produce that forecast likely never planned the ramp in the first place.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering considering adding engineers to speed up a slow ramp) Will adding more engineers to a struggling pod speed up onboarding?

Usually not — adding headcount to an already-struggling ramp dilutes the context the existing engineers are still building and typically slows the pod down further rather than speeding it up.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering comparing vendor onboarding claims before signing) What should we ask a vendor to prove before committing to an offshore dedicated development team?

Ask for a named 30/60/90 day plan with specific milestones, not a general timeline estimate, and ask what documentation and access will be ready before the engineers' first day.
