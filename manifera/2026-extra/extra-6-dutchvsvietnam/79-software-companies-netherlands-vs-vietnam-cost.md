A finance team builds a clean spreadsheet comparing a Netherlands software company's quote against a Vietnam software company's quote, line by line. The numbers look decisive. The spreadsheet is still missing the one line that determines whether either number is trustworthy.

**The Pain:** A CTO has two quotes on the table — one from a Netherlands software company, one from a Vietnam software company — and has been asked to justify the choice in numbers a CFO and board will accept. The Vietnam quote is dramatically lower. The instinct is to treat that gap with suspicion, to assume something is being cut to hit that price, without a structured way to verify whether the assumption is true or whether the gap is simply legitimate cost-base arbitrage.

**The Agitation:** Choosing the Netherlands quote purely to avoid the discomfort of justifying a large price gap costs a mid-sized company €150,000-€350,000 a year in avoidable premium on a comparable-scope engagement — a number that, once discovered by a cost-conscious board eighteen months later, becomes a much harder conversation than justifying the gap would have been upfront.

## The Cost Comparison Broken Into Its Actual Components

A defensible cost comparison between a Netherlands software company and a Vietnam software company requires breaking the headline number into four components, because collapsing them into one blended rate is exactly what makes the comparison feel untrustworthy in either direction.

The first component is base engineering cost — pure salary and overhead economics, independent of quality or governance. Netherlands senior software engineering salaries, fully loaded with employer costs, typically run €85,000-€120,000 annually. Vietnam senior software engineering salaries, fully loaded, typically run €22,000-€35,000 annually. This gap is real, well-documented, and reflects genuine regional cost-of-living and wage-market economics — not a quality signal in either direction. A CTO presenting this component to a board should present it exactly as what it is: structural, not a corner being cut.

The second component is management and governance overhead, which is where the two models genuinely diverge in structure rather than just price. A Netherlands software company typically bundles governance into its rate implicitly — the assumption being that local senior staff inherently provide oversight as part of doing the work. A Vietnam software company's raw execution rate typically excludes this function entirely unless a governance layer is explicitly built in and priced separately, which is exactly why an ungoverned offshore engagement can look artificially cheap: the governance cost hasn't been removed, it's been omitted, and it resurfaces later as risk instead of as a line item.

The third component is delivery risk premium — the cost of things going wrong, priced into the comparison as an expected value rather than ignored. A Netherlands engagement with weak internal governance carries real delivery risk despite the higher headline rate; a Vietnam engagement without an explicit governance layer carries higher delivery risk that isn't visible in the quote at all. The honest way to price this component is to ask both vendors directly what documented quality assurance and architecture review process exists, and weight the comparison accordingly rather than assuming either geography solves this by default.

The fourth component, and the one that resolves the comparison most cleanly, is total cost with governance held constant. Once a CTO prices Vietnam execution plus an explicit, contractual Amsterdam governance layer — architecture review, quality audit, escalation authority — against a Netherlands quote with an equivalent governance commitment made explicit, the real comparison emerges: properly governed Vietnam delivery typically lands at 45-60% of the total cost of a comparably governed Netherlands engagement, for the same delivery risk profile. That's the number a board can actually trust, because it isn't hiding a stripped-out governance function on either side.
