Every offshore sales deck claims "reliable delivery" — so what's the actual, checkable evidence a Netherlands or EU engineering leader should demand before believing that Vietnam-based execution will hold up past the honeymoon sprint?

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering evaluating IT development outsourcing to Vietnam for the first time keeps hearing the same reassurances — "strong engineering culture," "excellent English," "government-backed tech investment" — without a single verifiable number attached to any of it. Every vendor sounds confident. None of them show the data a reliability decision should actually be based on.

**The Agitation:** Committing budget to an unreliable offshore partner doesn't fail loudly on day one — it fails quietly, over two or three sprints, as velocity estimates slip, code review comments pile up unaddressed, and the engineer who understood the module rotates off the account without warning. By the time a VP of Engineering escalates, the organization has typically lost a full quarter and €40,000-€70,000 in re-architected or re-written work, on top of the original contract value, before anyone admits the delivery model itself was the problem.

## What Actually Makes Execution Reliable

Reliability in offshore execution isn't a vibe, and it isn't nationality — it's a small number of measurable structural factors, and a VP of Engineering evaluating Vietnam specifically should be checking each one before signing.

The first is talent pipeline depth, not talent existence. Vietnam graduates roughly 50,000-57,000 IT and computer-science students annually from a system anchored by institutions like Hanoi University of Science and Technology, VNU, and FPT University, feeding a developer population now numbering well over half a million. That's a genuinely deep pipeline — but depth doesn't guarantee the engineers assigned to your project are senior. The checkable question isn't "does Vietnam have good engineers," it's "what is the actual seniority mix of the specific pod being proposed, and can I see individual profiles, not just aggregate claims."

The second factor is attrition, and this is where reliability most often breaks down invisibly. Vietnam's broader IT sector has historically carried attrition rates in the 15-25% range at less disciplined shops, driven by aggressive poaching between agencies competing for the same limited senior talent. A pod that loses its tech lead mid-engagement doesn't just lose a person — it loses the undocumented context that person was carrying, and the client rarely finds out until a bug traces back to a decision nobody left behind an explanation for. The structural fix is contractual: a persistent-pod model with documented handoff protocols and retention-linked incentives, not a staffing pool where individuals are freely swapped.

The third factor is process maturity, which is where an ISO 27001-certified, CMMI-aligned delivery organization behaves fundamentally differently from an informal outsourcing shop. Sprint cadence, code review gates, defined Definition of Done, and a QA function that is structurally separate from the development team rather than "the developers testing their own code" — these aren't nice-to-haves, they're the difference between a pod that reports accurate sprint velocity and one that reports optimistic velocity until the release date proves otherwise.

The fourth factor is timezone-overlap architecture. Vietnam sits six hours ahead of Central European Time — a gap that's manageable, but only if the delivery model is deliberately structured around it: a defined daily overlap window for live standups and pairing, async handoff documentation that lets a Vietnam-based engineer pick up exactly where a Netherlands-based product owner left off, and a communication cadence that doesn't silently default to "wait until tomorrow" every time a question arises outside the overlap window.

None of these four factors are unique to Vietnam as a geography — they're what separates a mature delivery organization from an immature one anywhere in the world. The reason Vietnam specifically works for EU buyers is that the mature end of that market now exists at meaningful scale, at a cost base substantially below Western Europe, provided the buyer knows which questions separate the mature vendors from the rest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering worried about hidden junior staffing) How do we verify the seniority of the team actually assigned, not just what's promised in the pitch?

Request named team member profiles with verifiable project history as a condition of the proposal, and build a contractual right to review team composition at each milestone — aggregate claims about "senior engineers" without individual profiles are the first red flag.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering evaluating attrition risk) What happens to our project if a key engineer leaves mid-engagement?

With a persistent-pod structure, retention incentives and documented handoff protocols are built into the contract specifically to prevent this. Manifera's pods are staffed for project duration, and any transition includes overlap time with the outgoing engineer before departure.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering questioning reported sprint velocity) How can we independently verify sprint velocity instead of relying on the vendor's own reporting?

Insist on velocity tracked against actual merged, tested code in a shared repository your organization can access directly, rather than a vendor-generated status report — this alone eliminates most optimistic-reporting risk.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering concerned about the six-hour timezone gap) Is a six-hour gap between the Netherlands and Vietnam actually workable day to day?

Yes, with deliberate structure. Manifera builds a defined daily overlap window for live standups and pairing, backed by async handoff documentation, so questions outside the window don't default to a 24-hour delay.

### (Scenario: VP of Engineering wanting proof before a full commitment) Can we validate delivery reliability on a small scope before a larger engagement?

Yes — a four-to-six week pilot on a real, bounded workstream is the standard way to observe actual velocity, code review discipline, and communication quality before extending into a longer-term pod.
