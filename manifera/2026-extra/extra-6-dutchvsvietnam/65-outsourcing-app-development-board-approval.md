You've built the business case for outsourcing app development to a Vietnam-based pod. Can it survive twenty minutes of board questions from a director who's never heard of Ho Chi Minh City's engineering sector and is instinctively suspicious of anything described as "offshore"?

**The Pain:** A CFO has prepared a budget request to move a European company's app roadmap to an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam pod, with a compelling cost case — roughly half the price of the current Netherlands-based agency relationship. The board meeting is in five days. Two directors have already emailed questions ranging from data protection to "what happens if this vendor just disappears," and the CFO's deck currently answers none of them with specifics.

**The Agitation:** A budget request that survives the numbers but fails the risk questioning doesn't get rejected outright — it gets tabled "pending more information," which in board dynamics means it dies quietly over the next two quarters while the status quo agency relationship continues billing at twice the cost. A CFO who returns to the board a second time with the same unanswered questions spends political capital that's far more expensive than the €150,000-€200,000 the original proposal was trying to save.

## The Six Questions Every European Board Actually Asks

Boards evaluating an outsourcing app development proposal for the first time ask a predictable set of questions, and a CFO who walks in with specific answers rather than reassurances gets approval in one meeting instead of three.

The first question is almost always data protection and GDPR exposure. Directors want to know where data is processed, stored, and who is contractually liable for a breach. The answer that satisfies this isn't "the vendor is compliant" — it's a specific data processing agreement naming the Amsterdam entity as the accountable party under EU law, with Vietnam-based engineers working under data-handling protocols that never require production data to leave EU-controlled infrastructure unless explicitly scoped and documented.

The second is IP ownership — who owns the code, and can that ownership be proven during a future acquisition or funding round. The answer needs to be concrete: individual engineer NDAs, source-controlled repositories owned by the company from the first commit, not a vendor-entity-level agreement that frequently doesn't survive due-diligence scrutiny.

The third is vendor continuity risk — the "what if they disappear" question. This is best answered structurally: an Amsterdam-headquartered entity with EU-jurisdiction contractual accountability is a fundamentally different counterparty risk than a Vietnam-only vendor with no European legal presence, because contract enforcement doesn't require cross-border litigation.

The fourth is quality accountability — who is responsible if delivered work doesn't meet specification. The answer is the governance layer itself: Amsterdam reviews and signs off on architecture and delivery before it reaches the client, meaning there's an accountable party independent of the execution team, not a vendor grading its own homework.

The fifth is cultural and communication risk — will a Vietnam-based team actually understand a European product's requirements. This is best answered with specifics about overlap hours, English-language proficiency standards, and prior experience delivering for European (not just American or domestic) clients, ideally with named reference clients a board member could theoretically call.

The sixth, often unspoken but always present, is reputational risk — will approving this look bad if it goes wrong. This is answered not with a promise but with a structure: a scoped pilot engagement before full commitment, so the board is approving a bounded, reversible first step rather than a full-scale, irreversible bet. A CFO who brings a pilot-first structure to the board converts a binary yes/no decision into a lower-stakes first approval, which is almost always easier to get.

## The Warranty and Liability Clause Board Counsel Will Ask to See

Beyond the six relationship-level questions, a board's legal counsel typically red-lines one specific contract clause before signing off on an outsourcing app development budget: the warranty and liability structure. A defensible contract includes a 90-day post-delivery warranty period covering defects traceable to delivered code, during which fixes are covered under the original engagement fee rather than billed as new work. Liability should be capped at a stated multiple of fees paid — Manifera's standard structure caps general liability at 12 months of fees, with a separate, uncapped carve-out for breaches of confidentiality or IP warranties, which is the split most European in-house counsel expect rather than a single blanket cap. Professional indemnity and cyber liability insurance, held at the Amsterdam entity level, should sit in the €1-2 million range for a mid-size app engagement — proof of which a CFO should request as a certificate of insurance attached to the contract, not a verbal assurance. Boards rarely ask about this clause directly in the meeting, but counsel reviewing the contract afterward will, and a CFO who has it ready avoids a second round of legal redlines delaying signature by two to three weeks.
