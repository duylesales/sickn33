The cost case for offshoring your mobile build is done — so why does the board meeting still feel like the riskiest part of the whole decision?

**The Pain:** A CFO at a European company has a strong cost case for an Amsterdam-governed Vietnam offshore mobile app development engagement, and a board presentation scheduled for later this week. The cost numbers are solid. What isn't yet documented are the answers to mobile-specific risk questions a governance-minded board will raise — app-store account ownership, user-data handling across the SDKs the app will use, and what happens if the app needs emergency maintenance after an OS update six months post-launch.

**The Agitation:** A board that isn't satisfied on mobile-specific risk doesn't approve on faith, and a CFO sent back to close gaps loses an entire board cycle — often a full quarter — while the mobile roadmap sits stalled and competitors with working apps keep shipping. Every quarter a mobile launch slips typically costs €25,000–€55,000 in delayed user acquisition and idle engineering capacity, compounding the longer the approval takes.

## The Mobile-Specific Questions That Decide the Vote

A board evaluating a mobile app development budget asks the same governance questions it would for any offshore engagement, plus a set that's specific to mobile — and a CFO who's only prepared for the general questions will get caught flat-footed on the mobile-specific ones.

The first mobile-specific question is app-store account and listing control: who will own the Apple Developer and Google Play Console accounts the app publishes under. Boards with any prior outsourcing scar tissue know to ask this, because a vendor-controlled account creates a dependency that surfaces exactly when it's least convenient — during a vendor transition or dispute. The answer needs to be explicit: client-owned accounts from day one, with the offshore pod operating under, not instead of, that ownership.

The second is data handling across the app's specific SDK stack — analytics, crash reporting, push notifications, and any payment or advertising integrations. A board increasingly expects a CFO to name, specifically, who reviews these integrations for GDPR-relevant data flows before they ship, not after a legal or compliance team discovers an issue during an unrelated audit. "The vendor handles it" is not a satisfying answer to a board member who has read about app-store data-privacy enforcement actions in the trade press.

The third is IP ownership scoped correctly for mobile's technical structure — not just the app's feature code, but any custom native modules, internal component libraries, or reusable frameworks built during the engagement. A board member with technical fluency will specifically ask whether this is covered, because mobile codebases often contain more reusable internal tooling than a typical web build, and vague IP language that only covers "the app" can leave valuable tooling in a gray zone.

The fourth is post-launch continuity — what happens when the next major iOS or Android release ships and the app needs testing and updates against it. Unlike many web-based systems, mobile apps face forced technical debt on a predictable annual cycle, and a board wants confirmation that maintenance isn't an afterthought requiring a new procurement cycle every year, but a structured part of the ongoing engagement with defined SLA response times.

The fifth is the same governance-and-exit question that applies to any offshore engagement, with a mobile-specific edge: if the engagement needs to end, does the company retain full control of its app-store presence, source code, and user base, or does unwinding the relationship put the app itself at risk of disruption. A CFO who can answer this cleanly — client-owned accounts and code throughout, a defined transition process — removes what is often the single biggest source of board hesitation on any outsourced technical function: the fear of losing control of a customer-facing asset.

A CFO who arrives with specific, pre-built answers to these five mobile-specific questions typically converts a multi-round board negotiation into a single-session approval, because the board's underlying concern was never really the cost case — it was whether anyone had thought through what could go wrong with a customer-facing mobile asset before asking for the budget to build one.

### (Scenario: CFO whose board previously rejected a mobile proposal on control-related concerns) Our board rejected our first mobile proposal over concerns about losing control of the app — how is this different?
