If your board just approved an "AI initiative" and your first instinct is to open a vacancy for a senior AI engineer, how confident are you that you'll fill it before the initiative loses its sponsor?

**The Pain:** A CTO at a Netherlands-based scale-up has been told to ship a production AI feature this fiscal year — retrieval-augmented search, an internal automation layer, or a customer-facing recommendation engine. The instinct is to hire locally, the way the company has always hired. But the CTO hasn't yet priced out what "hiring locally" actually means for a discipline this specialized, and the vacancy has been open for six weeks with two screening calls and no shortlist.

**The Agitation:** Every month the role sits open is a month the roadmap slips, and AI roadmap slippage compounds differently than ordinary feature delays — competitors who shipped first capture the data feedback loop that makes their models better, widening the gap every quarter after. A single unsuccessful quarter of recruiting for a senior AI/ML engineer in the Netherlands commonly costs €15,000-€25,000 in recruiter fees and internal hours alone, before a single line of production code exists, and that's before factoring in the opportunity cost of a board that starts asking why the "AI initiative" has produced a job posting and nothing else.

## The Build vs. Offshore Decision, Framed Correctly

Most CTOs frame this as "in-house vs. outsourced," which is the wrong axis. The real question is whether you're trying to hire one generalist to cover a five-discipline problem, or whether you're structuring access to a coordinated team that already has each discipline covered. Production AI development is not one skill wearing an "AI engineer" job title — it's MLOps (versioning, evaluation harnesses, deployment pipelines), retrieval architecture (vector database selection, chunking, embedding strategy), model selection and prompt/fine-tuning methodology, data pipeline engineering, and a code-review discipline calibrated to how AI-assisted output actually fails, which is different from how hand-written code fails.

A single in-house hire, however senior, gives you depth in one or two of those disciplines and thin coverage everywhere else. That's not a knock on the hire — it's a structural limit of hiring one person for a five-discipline job. The Dutch AI/ML labor market compounds the problem: demand from Randstad-based scale-ups, banks, and international tech offices has pushed senior AI engineering compensation into a range few mid-market companies can sustain, and even successful hires frequently leave within eighteen months for the next competitive offer, taking undocumented tribal knowledge on model behavior and data quirks with them.

The offshore alternative isn't "the same role, cheaper" — that framing under-sells what a properly structured pod actually offers. A Vietnam-based AI pod, governed by Amsterdam-based technical leadership, gives a Netherlands CTO simultaneous access to an MLOps specialist, a retrieval/vector-database engineer, and an evaluation-focused engineer working as one unit, at a cost structure that a single senior local hire's salary alone often exceeds. The build-vs-offshore decision, honestly framed, isn't about trusting a team on the other side of the world with your architecture — it's about whether one local hire was ever going to deliver full-stack AI capability in the first place.

The governance question is what actually keeps CTOs up at night about offshore AI work, and it's a fair concern: who owns model evaluation standards, who signs off on data handling for GDPR-relevant training or inference data, who's accountable when an LLM-based feature ships something wrong in production. That's precisely the layer that needs to sit locally, in the same jurisdiction and legal framework as your company — not offshore, and not outsourced to a vendor account manager three timezones removed from the decision.
