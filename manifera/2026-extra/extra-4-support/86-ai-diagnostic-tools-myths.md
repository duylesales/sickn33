A CEO or founder building an AI-powered diagnostic support tool — image analysis, symptom-based triage, or clinical decision support — often carries assumptions shaped by AI's genuine progress in narrow diagnostic benchmark performance, assumptions that don't fully account for the specific validation, regulatory, and deployment realities clinical diagnostic support carries. Several of these assumptions deserve direct correction.

## Myth 1: "Strong Benchmark Performance on a Published Dataset Demonstrates Real Clinical Readiness"

Getting clear on this distinction early shapes not just the product's eventual clinical safety, but also how credible the company appears to anyone evaluating it closely, including regulators, hospital partners, and investors.

A founder building diagnostic AI reasonably points to strong published benchmark performance — accuracy matching or exceeding specialist performance on a specific curated dataset — as evidence of the tool's genuine clinical value. What this underweights is that published benchmark datasets are frequently curated in ways that don't fully represent the actual diversity and difficulty distribution of real clinical practice: real patient populations present more varied demographic characteristics, more ambiguous or borderline cases, and more genuine diagnostic difficulty variation than a curated benchmark dataset, assembled specifically to enable clean model comparison, typically represents. A model validated primarily against benchmark performance, without genuine prospective validation against real, unselected clinical populations reflecting actual practice diversity, risks a real gap between benchmark accuracy and genuine real-world clinical reliability.

## Myth 2: "A Tool Positioned as 'Decision Support' Rather Than 'Diagnostic' Avoids the Strictest Regulatory Requirements"

A founder reasonably explores whether careful product positioning — framing a tool as providing information to support a clinician's own decision-making, rather than making an autonomous diagnosis — might place the product in a lighter regulatory category than a tool making autonomous diagnostic claims. What this underweights is that medical device regulatory frameworks in most major markets evaluate a software product's actual clinical risk and function, not simply its marketing positioning or terminology, and a tool that in practice meaningfully influences clinical decisions, regardless of how carefully its positioning language is chosen, is likely to be evaluated against the regulatory requirements matching its actual clinical risk and influence, not the lighter category its positioning language might suggest. A founder relying on positioning language alone to avoid stricter regulatory requirements risks a genuine, potentially severe regulatory correction once a regulator evaluates the product's actual clinical function rather than its marketing framing.

## Myth 3: "Once Regulatory Clearance Is Achieved, the Model's Validation Requirements Are Essentially Complete"

A founder who has successfully navigated the genuinely demanding process of achieving initial regulatory clearance for a diagnostic AI tool reasonably feels that the hardest validation work is now behind the product. What this underweights is that a diagnostic model's real-world performance can genuinely drift over time as clinical practice patterns, patient population characteristics, and even underlying disease presentation evolve, meaning initial regulatory clearance, however rigorous, represents a snapshot validation at a specific point in time, not a permanent guarantee of continued real-world accuracy. Genuinely responsible diagnostic AI deployment requires ongoing post-market performance monitoring specifically designed to detect this kind of real-world drift, a requirement increasingly reflected in evolving regulatory expectations for AI-based medical devices specifically, given growing regulatory awareness of this genuine, well-documented risk category.

## Why These Myths Are Genuinely Understandable

These assumptions aren't unreasonable — strong benchmark performance is a genuinely meaningful signal worth taking seriously, and it's a reasonable instinct to explore product positioning options within genuine regulatory ambiguity. What makes diagnostic AI specifically different from many other AI application categories is the combination of a genuine, well-documented gap between curated benchmark performance and real clinical population diversity, regulatory frameworks that evaluate actual clinical function rather than positioning language, and a genuine, ongoing model drift risk that initial validation, however rigorous, doesn't permanently resolve.

## What This Means for Scoping a Diagnostic AI Product Correctly

- **Pursue genuine prospective validation against real, unselected clinical populations**, not relying solely on retrospective benchmark performance against curated datasets that may not represent actual clinical practice diversity.
- **Engage regulatory strategy based on the tool's genuine clinical function and risk, not primarily on positioning language**, working with genuine regulatory expertise to understand how a specific product's actual clinical influence will likely be evaluated regardless of marketing framing.
- **Build ongoing post-market performance monitoring into the product's core operational infrastructure**, treating model drift detection as a genuine, ongoing responsibility rather than a one-time validation milestone completed at initial clearance.
- **Communicate the tool's actual validated scope and limitations honestly to clinical users**, avoiding overstating benchmark performance as a direct proxy for real-world clinical reliability across the full diversity of actual patient populations.

A specific, practical point worth naming directly: sophisticated health technology investors, particularly those with genuine medical device or clinical regulatory background on their investment or advisory team, increasingly probe these exact three myths directly during due diligence for a diagnostic AI investment, recognizing them as well-known, recurring failure patterns in the category rather than obscure technical concerns.
