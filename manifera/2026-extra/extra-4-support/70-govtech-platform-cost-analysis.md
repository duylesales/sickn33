A CTO or IT director scoping a custom public sector digital services platform typically receives an initial cost estimate weighted toward the visible citizen-facing service functionality. The cost categories that most reliably get underestimated in government technology projects live in the specific compliance, security, and legacy integration requirements public sector platforms carry considerably more heavily than typical commercial software, requirements frequently underweighted in an estimate scoped against private-sector development norms.

## Cost Category 1: Genuine Accessibility Compliance, Not Cosmetic Adjustment

Getting an accurate picture of these four categories upfront, before a public budget and timeline are formally committed to, is considerably easier than correcting course once a project is already underway and stakeholder expectations have already been set against an unrealistic initial number.

As covered in dedicated technical guidance on this specific requirement, genuine EN 301 549 and WCAG compliance for public sector platforms requires accessibility designed into the core component architecture, not surface-level markup adjustments applied to an already-built interface. Building this architectural accessibility correctly, including ongoing testing with real assistive technology throughout development, is a substantial engineering investment frequently underrepresented in an initial estimate that treats accessibility as a final compliance checklist item rather than a foundational architecture requirement affecting how the entire platform's interactive components are built.

## Cost Category 2: Security and Audit Requirements Beyond Typical Commercial Standards

This category alone frequently accounts for a disproportionate share of the gap between an initial commercial-norms estimate and the realistic cost a genuinely compliant public sector platform actually requires.

Public sector platforms, particularly those handling citizen personal data or supporting critical government functions, frequently need to meet security and audit standards more rigorous than typical commercial software — comprehensive audit logging of data access and modifications, specific data residency and sovereignty requirements, and formal security certification processes that themselves carry real cost and timeline implications. Building genuinely comprehensive audit trails and meeting formal government security certification requirements is a substantially more demanding engineering and process undertaking than typical commercial application security, frequently underweighted in an initial estimate that scopes security against general best practices rather than the specific, often more stringent standards public sector deployment actually requires.

## Cost Category 3: Legacy System Integration

Government agencies rarely operate a clean, modern technology environment — a new public sector platform frequently needs to integrate with legacy systems, sometimes decades old, using outdated protocols, inconsistent data formats, and limited or poorly documented integration interfaces. Building reliable integration with this kind of genuinely challenging legacy technology landscape is a specific, substantial engineering undertaking frequently underrepresented in an initial estimate that assumes a modern, well-documented integration environment rather than the genuinely difficult legacy system reality many government technology projects actually encounter.

## Cost Category 4: Multi-Stakeholder Requirements Gathering and Procurement Process Overhead

Public sector technology projects typically involve genuinely more complex stakeholder requirements gathering than commercial software projects — multiple government departments with potentially conflicting requirements, formal procurement processes with specific documentation and evaluation requirements, and often longer approval cycles for significant scope or design decisions. This process overhead carries real project cost and timeline implications frequently underweighted in an initial estimate that scopes the project's requirements gathering and approval process against a typical commercial project's more streamlined stakeholder structure.

## Why These Categories Get Underestimated Consistently

A consistent pattern across public sector platform cost underestimation: an initial estimate is frequently built by a team more experienced with commercial software norms, applying commercial development cost and timeline assumptions to a project category with genuinely different accessibility, security, legacy integration, and stakeholder process requirements. These requirements aren't simply "more of the same" commercial development work at slightly higher rigor — they're categorically different engineering and process undertakings that a commercial-norms-based estimate systematically underrepresents, which is why public sector technology projects specifically benefit from cost scoping informed by genuine prior public sector delivery experience, not solely commercial software development experience applied to a public sector context.

## A Practical Budgeting Approach

- **Budget architectural accessibility as a foundational engineering category**, including ongoing assistive technology testing throughout development, not a final compliance checklist item.
- **Scope security and audit requirements against the specific formal standards your target public sector context actually requires**, not general commercial security best practices alone.
- **Conduct an early legacy system integration assessment**, documenting the actual protocols, data formats, and integration interfaces of systems the new platform needs to connect with, before finalizing integration cost estimates.
- **Budget realistic timeline and process overhead for multi-stakeholder requirements gathering and procurement processes**, recognizing that public sector projects typically involve more complex approval structures than commercial software projects.

## Why the Right Vendor's Own Prior Public Sector Experience Is Itself a Real Cost Factor

A specific, practical point worth naming directly: a development team's own genuine prior experience delivering public sector projects specifically is itself a meaningful cost and risk factor, distinct from general technical competence. A team with strong commercial software delivery experience but no genuine prior public sector delivery history is considerably more likely to produce exactly the kind of commercial-norms-based estimate this article describes, not through any lack of skill, but simply because the specific requirements this article covers aren't part of standard commercial software delivery practice and aren't something a team naturally anticipates without direct prior exposure to public sector delivery specifically.

This is a specific, practical reason a public sector procurement evaluation should weight a vendor's genuine, specific public sector delivery track record meaningfully in its own right, not simply as one general competence signal among many equally weighted factors — a vendor with direct prior public sector experience is considerably more likely to produce an initial cost estimate that's actually realistic from the start, avoiding the disruptive mid-project cost correction Ayuntamiento Digital Alcalá experienced in the case study below, simply because the specific cost categories this article describes are already a familiar, anticipated part of that vendor's standard scoping process rather than a gap discovered only once the project is already underway.
