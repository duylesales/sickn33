"Full-stack" on a resume often means a developer who's comfortable in both a frontend and a backend framework. It rarely means someone who actually takes ownership of a feature from database schema to deployed UI without a handoff gap in between — and that gap is where most delays actually live.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a tourism-technology company in Harderwijk — a Veluwe-region town whose visitor-attraction and hospitality-tech sector needs software that moves at seasonal speed — is evaluating a full-stack development company and finding that "full-stack" in vendor marketing usually just means the team covers both ends of the stack across different specialists, not that individual engineers own features end-to-end.

**The Agitation:** A VP of Engineering who hires a "full-stack" team that's actually frontend and backend specialists working in silos gets the same handoff delays and integration friction a single-discipline team would have, just relabeled — features stall at the frontend-backend boundary exactly the way they would have without the full-stack label at all.

## Testing for Genuine End-to-End Ownership

A full-stack development company's real value isn't that it employs people who know multiple technologies — it's that individual engineers can take a feature from data model to deployed interface without waiting on a handoff to a different specialist, which is a meaningfully different and rarer capability.

The first test is asking a vendor directly whether individual engineers ship full features solo, from schema change to UI, or whether "full-stack" describes the team's combined skill set while individual engineers still specialize and hand off internally — the second pattern reintroduces exactly the coordination friction a full-stack team is supposed to eliminate.

The second test is reviewing a sample pull request history, if available, to see whether individual contributors' commits actually span the stack, or whether they cluster narrowly around one layer despite a "full-stack" title.

The third test is asking how the vendor handles genuine specialization needs — because some work legitimately requires deep frontend or backend expertise, and a good full-stack team knows when to bring in that depth rather than forcing a generalist to handle something outside their real strength.
