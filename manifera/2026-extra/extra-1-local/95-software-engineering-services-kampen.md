Replacing a manufacturing ERP is one of the few software projects where the riskiest day isn't somewhere in the middle of development — it's cutover day, when the switch actually flips and every order in flight has to survive the transition intact.

**The Pain:** A CTO at a furniture-manufacturing company in Kampen — a historic Hanseatic town on the IJssel with a long-standing furniture and interior-goods manufacturing tradition — is commissioning software engineering services to replace an aging ERP system, and the vendor conversations so far have focused heavily on the new system's features and comparatively little on the specific mechanics of a safe cutover.

**The Agitation:** A CTO who underinvests in cutover planning discovers the risk on cutover day itself, when orders in progress, inventory counts, and production schedules all have to transfer cleanly to the new system simultaneously — a poorly planned cutover can produce lost or duplicated orders, incorrect inventory data, and a production floor that doesn't know what to build next, at a cost measured in actual missed shipments, not just IT downtime.

## Cutover Discipline as a Distinct Engineering Discipline

Replacing a manufacturing ERP requires software engineering services that treat cutover planning as a distinct, rigorously engineered discipline in its own right, not an administrative afterthought to the "real" development work.

The first requirement is a detailed data-migration validation process — every order, inventory record, and production schedule item verified against the old system after migration, with discrepancies caught and reconciled before cutover, not discovered by a confused production floor afterward.

The second is a rehearsed cutover runbook, tested against a realistic staging environment that mirrors production data volume and complexity, executed as a dry run before the actual cutover so the team has already worked through the specific sequence and timing under low-stakes conditions.

The third is a defined rollback threshold and procedure — a clear, pre-agreed point at which the team aborts the cutover and reverts to the old system if validation checks fail, rather than pushing forward under pressure and hoping issues resolve themselves after the fact, which is how a bad cutover day becomes a bad cutover week.

## Case Study & Testimonial

### A Danish Furniture Manufacturer's Cutover Incident

Nordisk Møbelproduktion A/S, a furniture manufacturer based in Aarhus, Denmark, had replaced its ERP system with a cutover plan that focused primarily on the new system's setup and lacked a rehearsed runbook or defined rollback threshold. Cutover day produced a batch of duplicated orders and several inventory discrepancies that weren't caught until the production floor was already working from incorrect data, costing two days of confused, partially re-worked production.

Manifera ran the subsequent phase-two rollout with full data-migration validation, a rehearsed cutover runbook tested against a staging environment mirroring real production volume, and a pre-agreed rollback threshold. The phase-two cutover completed without a single order or inventory discrepancy, and the production floor experienced no disruption.
