An industry that has built ships to survive open ocean for a century doesn't accept "it mostly works" from a shipyard, and a CFO in that same industry shouldn't accept a lower engineering standard from the software company building the systems that now run alongside those ships.

**The Pain:** A CFO at a maritime-engineering company in Papendrecht — a Drechtsteden town home to one of the world's most respected shipbuilding and dredging-equipment industries — is choosing a software company in the Netherlands for a vessel-systems integration platform, and needs a vendor evaluation standard that reflects the same engineering rigor the company's own maritime work is held to, not a generic software-vendor checklist borrowed from an unrelated industry.

**The Agitation:** A CFO who evaluates a software vendor on generic criteria — portfolio, references, day rate — without translating the company's own industry engineering discipline into the vendor evaluation risks selecting a software company that builds competently by generic software standards and inadequately by the standards a maritime engineering culture actually requires.

## Translating Industry Engineering Discipline Into Vendor Evaluation

Choosing a software company in the Netherlands for maritime-adjacent systems means holding the vendor to an evaluation standard that reflects the industry's own engineering culture, not a generic software checklist that doesn't distinguish adequate from genuinely rigorous.

The first translation is around documentation and traceability — maritime engineering culture treats thorough documentation as non-negotiable, and a software vendor evaluated by that standard needs to demonstrate the same discipline: every architectural decision documented, every system behavior traceable, not just "it works" without a paper trail a maritime engineer would find acceptable.

The second is around failure-mode analysis — an industry that designs for open-ocean survivability thinks systematically about what happens when something fails, and a software vendor should be evaluated on whether they apply that same systematic failure-mode thinking to software architecture, not just optimistic-path feature delivery.

The third is around long-term maintainability — maritime equipment is built and maintained for decades, and a CFO evaluating a software company for maritime-adjacent systems should specifically probe whether the vendor builds with that same multi-decade maintainability mindset, or with the shorter-horizon assumptions common in much of the software industry.
