The original developers who built the system are gone, the documentation is thin, and the VP of Engineering is now choosing a maintenance partner for software the company can't afford to have go dark for even a week — and "cheapest available" is not a continuity plan.

**The Pain:** A VP of Engineering at a maritime-technology company in Gorinchem — a historic Alblasserwaard town with deep ties to the Dutch shipbuilding and maritime-engineering sector — needs to outsource software maintenance for a vessel-tracking system built years ago by developers no longer with the company, and the documentation gap makes a wrong maintenance-vendor choice unusually costly.

**The Agitation:** A VP of Engineering who selects a maintenance vendor purely on hourly support rate and discovers, at the first serious incident, that the vendor never actually built institutional understanding of the undocumented system risks an extended outage on software the business depends on daily. An extended outage on a maritime-tracking system isn't a minor inconvenience — it's a direct operational and safety exposure that a maintenance contract exists specifically to prevent.

## What a Real Continuity Plan Requires

Outsourcing software maintenance on a poorly documented legacy system is a fundamentally different problem than outsourcing new feature development, and a VP of Engineering needs a continuity plan that reflects that difference explicitly.

The first requirement is a structured knowledge-transfer phase before the maintenance contract formally starts — a dedicated period where the incoming team builds and documents an understanding of the undocumented system, rather than learning it live during the first production incident.

The second is a defined incident-response SLA with named escalation paths, not a general "we'll get to it" support arrangement. A VP of Engineering needs to know exactly how fast a critical incident gets a response and who is accountable for it, in writing, before an incident occurs.

The third is ongoing documentation as an explicit deliverable of the maintenance engagement itself, not an afterthought — every fix and every piece of tribal knowledge uncovered during maintenance should close a documentation gap permanently, so the system becomes less fragile over time rather than staying exactly as undocumented as it started.

### A Norwegian Maritime-Tech Firm's Undocumented Legacy System

Fjordspor Maritime AS, a vessel-tracking technology company based in Bergen, Norway, had a legacy tracking system built by developers who had long since left the company, with almost no documentation. A prior maintenance vendor, chosen on hourly rate alone, took eleven hours to resolve a critical incident because the assigned support engineer had never actually built an understanding of the system before the incident occurred.

Manifera ran a structured four-week knowledge-transfer phase before the maintenance contract formally began, documenting the system's core logic as part of the process, and established a defined incident-response SLA with named escalation. The next comparable incident was resolved in under ninety minutes, with the root cause fully documented afterward.
