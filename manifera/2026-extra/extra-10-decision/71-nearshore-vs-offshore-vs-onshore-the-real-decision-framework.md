A vendor comparison spreadsheet with three hourly rates on it — €90 onshore, €55 nearshore, €35 offshore — looks like a simple decision. It isn't, and any CFO who has actually run all three models knows the spreadsheet is missing the variables that determine whether the cheapest number on it produces the cheapest outcome. Timezone overlap, communication overhead, delivery risk, and hidden coordination cost don't show up in an hourly rate, and they can erase a 40% rate advantage faster than most finance teams expect.

This decision sits with the CFO because it is fundamentally a cost-risk tradeoff dressed up as a geography choice, and geography is the least interesting variable in it. The real questions are: how much does communication friction cost per sprint, what does timezone misalignment do to your release cadence, and what is the actual total cost of ownership once you count the coordination layer that offshore, in particular, requires to work well. This article breaks the decision down by the variables that actually move the number, not the ones that look good in a vendor's marketing deck.

## Onshore: What You're Actually Paying For

Onshore development — hiring in your own country or a same-timezone market — buys same-hours overlap, no language or cultural translation layer, and the simplest possible legal and IP framework, since everyone operates under the same jurisdiction. What it costs is the highest blended rate of the three models by a wide margin: a senior engineer in the Netherlands or broader Western Europe commands total employment cost (salary plus employer contributions, benefits, and overhead) that typically runs 2.5-3.5x the all-in cost of an equivalent offshore engineer, and the market for that talent is genuinely tight — time-to-hire for a mid-to-senior engineer in the Netherlands regularly exceeds 90 days. Onshore is the right call when the work requires constant, high-bandwidth collaboration with stakeholders who cannot easily shift hours, or when regulatory or IP sensitivity genuinely requires same-jurisdiction development — not simply because it feels lower-risk by default.

## Nearshore: The Middle Ground That's Sometimes the Wrong Compromise

Nearshore — typically Eastern Europe for Western European buyers — offers meaningful timezone overlap (commonly 4-6 hours of shared working hours) and cultural proximity, at a rate discount versus onshore of roughly 30-45%. This makes nearshore genuinely attractive for engagements requiring frequent live collaboration but where full onshore cost isn't justified. The tradeoff CFOs underweight: nearshore rates have risen substantially over the past several years as Eastern European talent markets have matured and EU-wide remote hiring has intensified competition for the same pool, narrowing the cost gap versus onshore for the most in-demand skill sets (senior backend, DevOps, security) to sometimes 15-20%, well below the headline discount that made nearshore attractive in the first place. The nearshore decision needs a current rate check, not a rate assumption from a few years ago.

## Offshore: Where the Real Cost Advantage Lives, If You Structure For It

Offshore development — for Manifera's model, Vietnam-based engineering — offers the largest genuine cost advantage of the three, commonly 55-70% below onshore blended rates for comparable seniority, driven by a fundamentally different cost-of-living and talent-market baseline rather than a quality discount. The tradeoff is real and needs honest accounting: minimal or no working-hours overlap with Western Europe unless deliberately structured (Vietnam sits 5-6 hours ahead of Central European Time), which means offshore only works well when the engagement model is built around asynchronous handoff and overlap windows rather than assuming constant live collaboration will happen by default. Offshore engagements that fail typically fail not because the engineering quality was poor, but because the client tried to run an offshore team with an onshore communication cadence — daily stand-ups scheduled at an hour that's actually late evening for one side, decisions bottlenecked on real-time approval that could have been asynchronous.

Structured correctly — a defined daily overlap window (commonly the offshore team's morning, which lands in the European afternoon), async-first documentation discipline, and a dedicated pod model rather than fragmented individual contractors — offshore delivers the cost advantage without the coordination tax that gives offshore its reputation risk.

## The Total Cost of Ownership Calculation CFOs Should Actually Run

The hourly or day rate comparison across all three models is the least useful number for a CFO's decision, because it ignores coordination overhead, ramp time, and quality-driven rework — all of which vary systematically by model. A more honest comparison weighs: blended rate, times expected hours, plus an estimated coordination overhead factor (typically negligible for onshore, 10-15% for well-structured nearshore, and 15-25% for offshore if not well-structured, closer to 5-10% if it is), plus a one-time ramp cost that's roughly comparable across nearshore and offshore models but higher for a from-scratch onshore hire given typical 90+ day time-to-hire. Run this fuller calculation before accepting the headline rate comparison, and the offshore cost advantage typically still holds decisively even after the coordination overhead is priced in — it's the nearshore comparison against onshore that often shrinks more than expected once current rates and overlap value are counted honestly.

## Risk Factors That Don't Show Up in Any Rate Comparison

Beyond cost, each model carries a distinct risk profile worth pricing explicitly. Onshore carries the lowest execution risk but the highest opportunity cost of capital tied up in expensive headcount that's hard to flex down. Nearshore carries moderate execution risk with the specific exposure that talent markets in some Eastern European countries have experienced wage inflation and turnover pressure as global remote hiring increased competition for the same engineers. Offshore carries the most reputation-driven risk perception (unfairly, in Manifera's experience, when the engagement is structured well) but the least talent-market volatility risk, since Vietnam's engineering talent pool has grown substantially and remains less saturated by global remote-hiring competition than either Western or Eastern Europe.

## Making the Final Call

There is no universally correct answer among the three models — the right choice depends on how much genuine real-time collaboration the work requires, how mature your team is at managing asynchronous handoff, and how much of the rate advantage you're willing to structure your process around capturing. A CFO's practical framework: default to offshore for well-scoped, architecturally clear work where a dedicated pod model and defined overlap windows are achievable; use nearshore when frequent live collaboration is genuinely required but full onshore cost isn't justified, while verifying current rates rather than assuming a discount that may have narrowed; reserve onshore for work with hard regulatory, IP, or stakeholder-proximity requirements that make any timezone gap genuinely unworkable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is offshore development actually riskier than nearshore or onshore, or is that a perception problem?
It's largely a perception problem when the engagement is structured properly — with defined overlap windows, async-first documentation, and a dedicated pod rather than fragmented contractors, offshore execution risk is comparable to nearshore. The reputation risk offshore carries mostly traces back to poorly structured engagements that tried to run real-time collaboration across a non-overlapping timezone gap.

### How much has the nearshore rate advantage actually narrowed in recent years?
For the most in-demand skill sets — senior backend, DevOps, security engineering — the gap versus onshore has narrowed to as little as 15-20% in some Eastern European markets, down from a historical discount closer to 40-50%, as EU-wide remote hiring intensified competition for the same talent pool. It's worth verifying current rates rather than assuming a discount from a few years ago still holds.

### What's a realistic coordination overhead estimate for a well-structured offshore engagement?
Roughly 5-10% on top of the blended rate for a mature, well-structured offshore engagement with defined overlap windows and async discipline — versus 15-25% for a poorly structured one attempting real-time collaboration across a large timezone gap. This overhead should be built into the total cost of ownership calculation, not treated as a hidden surprise.

### Should we use a blend of models rather than choosing just one?
Often yes — a common effective pattern is offshore or nearshore for the bulk of engineering execution paired with a smaller onshore or nearshore presence for roles requiring constant stakeholder proximity, such as a product owner or a lead architect coordinating directly with internal teams. The framework in this article applies per-role and per-workstream, not necessarily as a single choice for an entire project.

### How does timezone overlap actually translate into delivery speed?
More overlap generally means faster resolution of blocking questions and shorter feedback loops on ambiguous requirements, which matters most in early-stage, exploratory work. Once requirements and architecture are well-defined, overlap matters less, since well-scoped execution work translates well to async handoff regardless of timezone — which is why offshore's cost advantage holds up best on clearly-scoped work.
