The board approved €400,000 for engineering capacity this fiscal year. Not €420,000, not "roughly 400 depending on how it goes" — a fixed number that has to survive contact with vendor proposals, scope creep, and at least one unplanned priority shift before the year closes. Every CFO who has run this process knows the real skill isn't finding a vendor who fits the number on paper — it's finding one whose actual delivered cost still fits the number in month nine, after reality has had its say.

This is a distinct decision from open-ended vendor evaluation, because a fixed budget inverts the normal question. Instead of "which vendor delivers the most value," the operative question becomes "which vendor delivers acceptable value without the structural risk of blowing past a number that cannot move." Those two questions produce different shortlists, and CFOs who evaluate vendors as if budget were flexible end up with proposals that look excellent on capability and collapse under change-request pricing by Q3.

## The Budget Number Isn't the Constraint You Think It Is

Treat the approved figure as a ceiling on total cost of engagement, not a target day rate, and the vendor evaluation changes shape immediately. A vendor at a higher day rate with a tightly scoped, fixed-price structure can land comfortably under budget; a cheaper day rate with open-ended time-and-materials billing against a loosely defined scope routinely overshoots, because the real budget risk isn't the rate — it's the absence of a cost ceiling on the engagement itself. When evaluating proposals against a fixed budget, weight pricing structure and scope definition as heavily as the headline rate, because the rate alone tells you almost nothing about whether the total will actually hold.

## Scope-to-Budget vs Budget-to-Scope: Two Different Conversations

There are two legitimate ways to run this process, and conflating them is where most budget overruns start. Scope-to-budget means defining the full desired scope first and finding a vendor who can deliver it within budget — which usually means cutting scope when the numbers don't reconcile, a hard but honest conversation to have before signing. Budget-to-scope means fixing the number first and asking vendors what they can deliver for it, which is more honest about the constraint but requires genuine trust in the vendor's scoping discipline, since it's their incentive, not yours, that determines what gets cut when the number is fixed. Neither approach is wrong, but pick one deliberately — running both conversations simultaneously with the same vendor is how "we'll figure out scope as we go" ends up eating a fixed budget by August.

## Where Fixed Budgets Push Buyers Toward the Wrong Vendor

A hard budget ceiling creates a specific bias: it rewards vendors who are good at winning the proposal over vendors who are good at estimating accurately, because the lowest number on paper wins the comparison regardless of whether it's realistic. This is where change-request pricing becomes the quiet budget killer — a vendor who underbids the initial scope to win against budget-constrained competitors recovers margin through amendments once the engagement is underway, by which point switching vendors mid-project costs more than absorbing the overage. The defense is structural, not just diligence: ask every finalist for their historical rate of change requests as a percentage of original contract value on comparable engagements, and treat a vendor unwilling to share this number the way you'd treat an unaudited financial statement.

## Rate vs Total Cost: The Comparison CFOs Get Wrong

Comparing vendor proposals by blended hourly rate alone is one of the most common budget-planning errors, because rate says nothing about the hours required to reach the same outcome. A vendor at €55/hour who needs 1,800 hours to deliver a scope costs more than a vendor at €65/hour who needs 1,400 hours for the same deliverable — and estimation accuracy, not rate, is usually the bigger driver of that gap. Ask vendors for a total-cost-to-delivery figure against a clearly specified scope, not a rate card, and normalize proposals on that basis before applying any budget filter. This single change in comparison method catches more true budget mismatches than rate negotiation ever will.

## Phasing the Roadmap to Fit the Fiscal Year

When the full desired scope genuinely exceeds the fixed budget, phasing is usually a better answer than compressing the whole roadmap into an under-resourced single engagement. Sequence the roadmap so the highest-value, most budget-defensible phase lands within this fiscal year's number, with subsequent phases scoped as separate, budget-year-two engagements rather than a single continuous commitment the vendor assumes will simply continue. This also preserves a genuine decision point — if the vendor relationship isn't performing, phase boundaries are a natural, low-friction place to re-evaluate rather than needing to break an active continuous engagement.

## Contingency: The Line Item Most Budgets Skip

Software budgets built without a contingency line are, in practice, budgets that assume zero scope discovery risk — an assumption that almost never holds. A reasonable contingency reserve for a well-scoped project sits around 10-15% of total contract value; for less-defined or first-time-with-this-vendor engagements, 15-20% is more realistic. The discipline isn't just setting the reserve aside — it's explicitly not communicating its existence to the vendor during scoping, since a vendor aware of unallocated contingency has a subtle incentive to price toward consuming it. A fixed budget with a hidden internal contingency behaves very differently, and much more safely, than the same number presented as the vendor's hard ceiling.

## Making the Final Call

A fixed annual budget doesn't eliminate vendor choice — it changes which criteria matter most, shifting weight from headline capability toward pricing structure, scope discipline, and historical change-request rates. CFOs who compare vendors on total cost to a clearly specified deliverable, hold a genuine internal contingency, and pick deliberately between scope-to-budget and budget-to-scope sequencing consistently land engagements that hold their number through the fiscal year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I choose the vendor with the lowest day rate to stay within a fixed budget?
Not without normalizing for total cost to delivery. A lower day rate combined with more required hours or a higher historical change-request rate frequently produces a higher total cost than a moderately higher rate with tighter estimation and a fixed-scope structure. Compare total cost against a clearly specified scope, not rate cards.

### What's the difference between scope-to-budget and budget-to-scope planning?
Scope-to-budget defines the full desired deliverable first and finds a vendor who can deliver it within the number, typically requiring scope cuts when the math doesn't reconcile. Budget-to-scope fixes the number first and asks vendors what they can deliver for it, requiring real trust in the vendor's scoping discipline since they determine what gets cut.

### How much contingency should be built into a fixed software budget?
A reasonable reserve for a well-scoped project is 10-15% of total contract value; for less-defined scope or a first engagement with a new vendor, 15-20% is more realistic. This reserve should generally not be disclosed to the vendor during scoping, since visibility into unallocated budget can subtly shift how a vendor prices toward consuming it.

### How do I evaluate whether a vendor's low proposal is realistic or a lowball to win the deal?
Ask every finalist for their historical rate of change requests as a percentage of original contract value on comparable engagements. A vendor unwilling to share this, or with a track record of high change-request rates, is more likely to recover margin post-signing on a scope that looked attractively cheap at proposal stage.

### Is it better to compress a full roadmap into one fixed-budget engagement or phase it across fiscal years?
Phasing is usually the safer approach when full scope exceeds the available budget. Sequencing the highest-value phase into the current fiscal year's number, with subsequent phases scoped as separate future engagements, preserves a genuine decision point to re-evaluate the vendor relationship at each phase boundary.
