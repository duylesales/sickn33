Engineering leadership wants a vendor onboarded in six weeks. Your standard software procurement process takes fourteen. Somewhere in that gap sits a decision every procurement lead eventually faces: which parts of the process are genuinely load-bearing risk controls, and which are sequential steps that exist mostly because nobody has questioned why they run one after another instead of in parallel. Getting that distinction wrong in either direction is expensive — cut a real control and you inherit vendor risk nobody signed off on; keep every step sequential and engineering routes around procurement entirely on the next urgent hire.

This is a genuinely solvable tension, not an inherent trade-off between speed and rigor. Most procurement timelines that run long aren't long because diligence takes that much actual work — they're long because of sequencing, redundant approval loops, and RFP scope that was copied from a much larger, unrelated purchase category. A procurement lead who understands which steps compress safely and which don't can cut real time without cutting real risk coverage.

## Why "Faster" and "Less Diligent" Aren't the Same Thing

The instinct to protect procurement's credibility is to resist compression outright, treating every request to move faster as pressure to cut corners. That framing is usually wrong. Diligence quality is a function of what gets checked and how rigorously, not how many calendar weeks elapse between steps — a reference check conducted thoroughly in week two is exactly as rigorous as one conducted in week eight. The actual risk in compression isn't speed itself, it's compressing steps that require external dependencies (a reference's availability, a legal review queue) versus compressing steps that are purely internal sequencing choices your own team controls. Distinguishing those two categories is the entire game.

## The RFP Bloat Problem

A large share of procurement timeline waste starts at the RFP itself — templates inherited from enterprise software purchases (multi-year SaaS licensing, hardware procurement) applied wholesale to a services engagement like dedicated development team staffing, where most of those questions are irrelevant. An RFP with 80 questions when 25 would actually differentiate finalists doesn't produce better vendor selection — it produces longer vendor response times, more finalist drop-off from vendors unwilling to invest in a bloated response, and a scoring exercise where reviewers skim rather than genuinely evaluate. Rebuild the RFP around the specific decision at hand: for a software development vendor, that's typically technical capability, delivery track record, team continuity practices, contract flexibility, and cultural/process fit — a tighter set of questions that actually separates finalists rather than testing their patience.

## Parallel-Tracking Instead of Sequential Gates

The single biggest timeline compression available without cutting any diligence step is running technical evaluation, reference checks, legal review, and security assessment in parallel rather than as sequential gates, each waiting for the prior one to fully close before starting. Most procurement processes default to sequential because it mirrors how the RFP document is organized, not because the steps have a genuine dependency on each other — legal reviewing the MSA doesn't need to wait for reference checks to complete. Map the actual dependencies (security assessment might genuinely need technical scope finalized first) and run everything else concurrently; this alone typically compresses a 12-14 week process to 7-9 weeks without touching diligence depth at all.

## Standardizing Due Diligence Without Standardizing Away Nuance

A standardized due diligence checklist — the same core questions and evidence requirements across every vendor evaluation — genuinely speeds up procurement, because it eliminates the time lost to reviewers reinventing the evaluation criteria each time and vendors guessing what's actually being assessed. The risk is over-standardizing into a checkbox exercise that misses engagement-specific risk. The fix is a two-tier structure: a standardized core (financial stability, insurance, data handling practices, reference availability) that applies to every vendor evaluation without modification, plus an engagement-specific supplement (data sensitivity level, integration complexity, regulatory exposure) scoped to the actual work. This keeps the fast, repeatable part fast while preserving judgment where it's actually needed.

## Reference Checks: The Step Everyone Rushes

Reference checks are simultaneously the diligence step procurement leads say matters most and the one most commonly compressed into a single generic call with a vendor-selected reference who has every incentive to say positive things. A genuinely useful reference process — one worth the time it takes — asks for at least one reference from an engagement that ended or changed scope significantly, not just an ongoing success story, and asks specific operational questions (turnover experienced, how change requests were actually priced, communication cadence in practice) rather than a general satisfaction question that produces a general positive answer. This step shouldn't be compressed for speed; it should be redesigned to extract more signal per call, which paradoxically makes it both faster and more useful.

## Approval Chains: Where Procurement Timelines Actually Die

The step procurement leads most consistently underestimate is internal approval routing — not vendor evaluation itself, but the sequential sign-off chain across finance, legal, and departmental leadership that happens after the vendor decision is effectively made. A decision reached in week six can sit for three additional weeks in an approval queue that was never actually contested, just sequentially routed. Pre-socializing the recommendation with each approver before the formal request — a five-minute heads-up conversation rather than a cold approval request landing in an inbox — routinely cuts this phase by half, because objections surface early, when they're cheap to address, rather than after a decision has already been formally submitted and stalled.

## Making the Final Call

Streamlining procurement without cutting diligence is almost entirely about sequencing and scope discipline, not about doing less checking. Parallel-track what doesn't have a genuine dependency, tighten the RFP to what actually differentiates finalists, standardize the repeatable core of due diligence while preserving engagement-specific judgment, and pre-socialize approvals instead of routing them cold. Done well, this typically compresses a process by 30-40% in calendar time with no reduction in what actually gets checked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the fastest way to compress a software vendor procurement timeline without reducing diligence?
Run technical evaluation, reference checks, legal review, and security assessment in parallel rather than as sequential gates, since most steps don't have a genuine dependency on each other. This single change typically compresses a 12-14 week process to 7-9 weeks without cutting any diligence step.

### How many questions should a vendor RFP for software development actually include?
Enough to differentiate finalists on the criteria that matter — typically technical capability, delivery track record, team continuity practices, contract flexibility, and process fit. Bloated RFPs inherited from unrelated purchase categories, often 80+ questions, produce more finalist drop-off and shallower reviewer scoring rather than better selection.

### Should due diligence checklists be standardized across all vendor evaluations?
A two-tier structure works best: a standardized core covering financial stability, insurance, and data handling that applies to every evaluation, plus an engagement-specific supplement scoped to data sensitivity, integration complexity, and regulatory exposure for that particular vendor and project.

### Why do reference checks often fail to surface real risk?
Because they're commonly compressed into a single generic call with a vendor-selected reference who has incentive to speak positively. A more useful process asks for a reference from an engagement that ended or changed scope significantly, and asks specific operational questions rather than a general satisfaction question.

### Where do procurement timelines lose the most time after the vendor decision is made?
In internal approval routing across finance, legal, and departmental leadership — a sequential sign-off chain that can add three or more weeks to a decision that was never actually contested. Pre-socializing the recommendation with each approver before formal submission routinely cuts this phase by half.
