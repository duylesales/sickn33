In week two, the vendor's team is in every standup, questions get answered within the hour, and the shared Slack channel is genuinely active. By month four, standups have quietly become optional, updates arrive a day late, and you're finding out about blockers only when a deadline slips. Nothing was decided; the cadence just eroded, the way cadences always do when they weren't built to survive the erosion.

Every new vendor relationship runs hot in the first few weeks — both sides are motivated to make a good impression, communication is frequent almost by default, and it feels, correctly, like a well-functioning partnership. The mistake product managers make is treating that early cadence as the steady state rather than recognizing it as a honeymoon phase that predictably cools. The cadence that matters is the one that's still functioning in month six, under the weight of routine work and normal competing priorities — and that cadence has to be deliberately designed, not assumed to persist on its own.

## Design for the Timezone Gap, Not Around It

For a Netherlands-based team working with an engineering partner in Ho Chi Minh City, the timezone gap is roughly 5-6 hours depending on daylight saving, which leaves a real overlap window — typically late morning CET through early afternoon — but not a full workday of overlap. The mistake is designing communication as if this gap doesn't exist, defaulting to real-time Slack expectations that quietly fail once the honeymoon urgency fades and people revert to their own working hours. The fix is designing explicitly around the gap: a core overlap window (say, two hours) reserved for synchronous conversation — standups, blocker resolution, ad hoc pairing — and an async-first default for everything else, with clear norms about expected response time outside the overlap window (same working day, not same hour). This isn't a workaround for a timezone problem; done well, it's actually a better structure than full-overlap real-time communication, because it forces written clarity that survives beyond the conversation that produced it.

## Choose a Standup Format That Doesn't Decay Into Status Theater

A live video standup is the default most teams reach for, and it works well in the first month, when everyone is invested in making a good impression. By month three, it frequently decays into a scripted status update nobody's really listening to, because the format was never really designed for information transfer — it was designed for visibility and rapport-building, which matters early and matters less once the relationship is established. A more durable structure for many engagements: a written async standup (posted by a set time, covering yesterday/today/blockers) supplemented by a shorter, more focused live sync two to three times a week, reserved specifically for blockers and decisions rather than status recitation. This costs less attention from everyone involved while actually surfacing blockers faster, because a blocker buried in a live standup rundown is easy to under-emphasize, while a blocker flagged in writing is harder to let slide.

## Build in a Structural Reason to Talk After the Honeymoon Ends

Cadence decay isn't usually a decision — it's what happens by default when there's no structural forcing function keeping the frequency up once the initial motivation fades. Build one in deliberately: a recurring, calendar-fixed retro or planning session that isn't optional and isn't rescheduled around convenience, a monthly cadence review where both sides explicitly discuss whether the current communication structure is still working (not just the delivered work), and a standing agenda item in sprint planning for anything that's been ambiguous or under-communicated in the prior sprint. None of this is exotic — it's standard agile ceremony discipline — but the discipline is exactly what erodes first once a vendor relationship starts feeling routine, and product managers who protect these recurring touchpoints from being the first thing cut under deadline pressure are the ones whose vendor relationships stay healthy at month six.

## Separate Escalation Channel From Routine Channel

A common cadence failure: routine updates and genuine blockers flow through the same channel, which means urgent issues get buried in volume once routine traffic increases, or routine updates get treated with false urgency because they're in the "important" channel. Establish two explicitly separate channels from the start — one for routine async updates and questions, and a distinctly named, lower-volume channel or tag reserved specifically for blockers requiring same-day attention. This separation is cheap to set up and disproportionately valuable exactly at the moment the relationship has enough history that Slack channels are full of normal noise — the moment a real blocker is most likely to get lost without a dedicated signal.

## Decide Who Owns Cadence Health, Not Just Delivery Health

Delivery health (are sprints shipping, is velocity stable) usually has an owner by default — whoever is running sprint planning. Communication cadence health frequently doesn't, which is exactly why it degrades quietly: nobody is explicitly responsible for noticing that standups have become perfunctory or that response times have crept up. Assign this explicitly, usually to the product manager or the internal engineering lead managing the relationship, with a standing prompt (part of the monthly cadence review above) to ask directly: is the current communication structure still serving the team, or has it drifted into something that technically happens but doesn't actually transfer information anymore?

## Making the Final Call

The communication cadence that matters isn't the one you experience in week two — it's the one still functioning honestly in month six, after the initial motivation to impress has worn off on both sides. Design for the timezone gap rather than around it, build in structural forcing functions that don't depend on ongoing enthusiasm, and assign explicit ownership of cadence health separate from delivery health. A cadence engineered this way degrades far more slowly, and when it does start slipping, someone notices before it costs a missed deadline.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much timezone overlap is actually needed for a Netherlands-Vietnam engagement?
A focused two-hour overlap window, typically late morning CET through early afternoon, is generally sufficient for synchronous standups and blocker resolution when the rest of the day defaults to async-first communication with clear same-working-day response norms.

### Are live video standups better than async written ones?
Live standups build rapport well in the first month but often decay into status theater by month three. A written async standup supplemented by two to three focused live syncs a week for blockers and decisions tends to hold up better over the life of a longer engagement.

### Why does communication cadence tend to erode even when delivery stays on track?
Because cadence health rarely has an explicit owner the way delivery health does. Nobody is specifically responsible for noticing standups have become perfunctory, so the drift happens quietly and is usually only caught after it's already caused a missed handoff.

### Should routine updates and urgent blockers share the same communication channel?
No. Mixing them means blockers get buried in routine volume or routine updates get treated with false urgency. A separate, lower-volume channel reserved specifically for same-day blockers keeps genuine urgency visible.

### What's a practical way to catch cadence decay before it causes a real problem?
A recurring, calendar-fixed monthly cadence review, separate from delivery-focused retros, where both sides explicitly discuss whether the current communication structure is still working — not just whether the work is shipping.
