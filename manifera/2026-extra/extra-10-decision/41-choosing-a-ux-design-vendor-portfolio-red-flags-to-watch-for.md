Every UX agency deck opens the same way: a grid of gorgeous mobile screens, a case study title like "Reimagining Fintech Onboarding," and a client logo you're supposed to recognize. None of it tells you whether the person who made those screens can actually solve your product's problem. The portfolio review is the single highest-leverage 45 minutes in the entire vendor selection process, and most product leaders spend it admiring pixels instead of interrogating process.

This decision matters because a UX design vendor's output becomes your product's foundation for years — the information architecture, interaction patterns, and design logic they hand off get built into code your engineering team will maintain long after the contract ends. If you're a Head of Product evaluating agencies for a redesign, a new product line, or a design system kickoff, you are not buying screens. You are buying a way of thinking that will shape every sprint after the contract ends. Getting the portfolio review wrong means discovering three months into the engagement that the "senior UX team" you hired is actually one visual designer with a Dribbble account and a template library.

## The Same Three Flows, Re-Skinned

The fastest tell in any portfolio is structural repetition. Look past the color palettes and typography and check the actual flow: onboarding with a 3-step progress indicator, a dashboard with a left rail and card grid, a checkout with the same field order and validation pattern. If six "different" case studies from six different industries — fintech, healthcare, logistics, SaaS — all resolve to the same underlying flow logic, you're not looking at six solved problems. You're looking at one template re-skinned six times with a new client logo pasted on top. Ask the agency directly to walk you through why the healthcare onboarding flow differs structurally from the fintech one, given that the regulatory constraints, user anxiety levels, and task complexity are completely different domains. A vendor with real product thinking will have a specific, defensible answer rooted in user research. A template shop will pivot to talking about the color system.

## Process Artifacts: What's Missing Tells You More Than What's Shown

A polished final screen is the least informative artifact in a case study, because it's the output every agency — good or bad — can produce. What separates genuine UX practice from a visual design service is the trail of intermediate work: low-fidelity wireframes, user flow diagrams, information architecture maps, and evidence of iteration between them. Ask to see the wireframes that preceded the final screens for a specific case study, not a curated "process" slide with three arbitrary boxes labeled Discover, Design, Deliver. A vendor who genuinely ran a design process can pull up an actual FigJam or Miro board with real dated iterations, sticky notes from a workshop, and versions that look meaningfully different from each other — because real design work is messy before it's polished. If every "sketch" phase looks suspiciously clean and suspiciously close to the final output, it was probably drawn after the fact to fill a slide, not used to drive a decision.

## No Evidence of Testing Means No Evidence of Thinking

Ask any vendor: what did you change after testing this with real users, and why? A UX practice that runs usability testing — moderated sessions, unmoderated tools like Maze or UserTesting, even five-person guerrilla tests — will have a specific story about a decision that testing overturned. Something like "we assumed users would scan the pricing table top-down, testing showed they anchored on the middle column, so we restructured the visual hierarchy." That kind of story is nearly impossible to fabricate convincingly because it requires naming a wrong assumption. Vendors who cannot produce this story, and instead describe testing in the abstract ("we always validate our designs with users"), most likely treat testing as a compliance checkbox mentioned in the sales deck rather than a practice embedded in the workflow. This matters enormously for a Head of Product because a vendor who doesn't test is a vendor who is guessing at the same rate you would guess internally — you're paying agency rates for opinion, not expertise.

## Pixels-Only Agencies vs. Product-Minded Design Partners

There's a meaningful category difference between a visual design shop and a product design partner, and portfolios blur it deliberately. A pixels-only agency will show you beautiful screens and describe their contribution entirely in aesthetic terms — clean, modern, delightful. A product-minded partner will describe trade-offs: why they chose a single-column checkout over a multi-step one given the target user's device context, or why they recommended cutting a feature from the MVP scope because usability testing showed it added cognitive load without adding conversion. Ask the vendor to describe one decision from a past project where they pushed back on the client's initial request and explain the reasoning. An agency that only ever agreed with the client, or can't recall ever disagreeing, has never been treated as anything more than a production resource — and that is exactly the role they will play for you.

## Who Actually Did the Work? Ownership and Subcontracting

Agency portfolios routinely display work done primarily by a freelancer or subcontractor who is no longer affiliated with the agency, or by a different team than the one that would staff your project. This is one of the most common and hardest-to-catch red flags. Ask specifically: which named individuals on your current team worked on this case study, in what role, and are they available to be staffed on our engagement? A vendor who gets vague about attribution, or who can only speak generally about "our team's approach" without naming who did the actual research, wireframing, and visual design, is very likely showing you someone else's work, or work from a team composition that has since changed. This is especially important for boutique agencies of under 15 people, where a single strong senior designer's work can carry the entire portfolio while the team you'd actually be assigned is two juniors.

## Outcomes: Before/After Data or It Didn't Happen

A UX case study that ends at "and here's the final design" without any measurement of what changed for the business is a portfolio piece, not evidence of impact. Strong vendors show before/after metrics: conversion rate on a redesigned checkout, task completion time on a redesigned dashboard, drop-off rate on a redesigned onboarding flow, support ticket volume tied to a confusing feature that got redesigned. These numbers don't need to be dramatic — a genuine 8% lift in activation rate is more credible and more useful than an implausible "300% increase in engagement" with no defined baseline. If a vendor cannot produce a single case study with a real before/after number, across their entire portfolio, that tells you they either don't track outcomes or don't get invited back long enough to measure them.

## Reading Between the Lines: NDA Excuses and Reference Refusals

NDAs are real and some clients genuinely restrict what an agency can show publicly. But there's a difference between "we can't show the screens, but I can walk you through the problem, our process, and the measured outcome verbally" and a flat refusal that shuts down the conversation entirely. If NDA becomes the answer to every substantive question — not just "can we see it" but "can you describe what changed" — treat it as an evasion, not a legal constraint. The same logic applies to references: a design vendor of any real standing should be able to connect you with at least two former clients willing to speak for fifteen minutes about how the engagement actually went, including what didn't work well. A vendor who stalls on references, offers only marketing testimonials, or provides a reference who turns out to be a personal friend of the founder is telling you something important about how their client relationships actually end.

## The Interview Question That Beats the Portfolio Itself

If you only have time for one part of the vetting process, weight the live interview over the portfolio review, because a curated deck is optimized to hide exactly the weaknesses you're trying to find. During the interview, describe a real, specific problem from your own product — not a hypothetical — and ask the vendor to think through it out loud: what questions would they ask your users first, what would they want to test before committing to a direction, and where do they see risk in the brief as you've described it. A vendor with genuine product design capability will ask you clarifying questions you hadn't considered and will be comfortable saying "I don't know yet, that's what research would tell us." A vendor coasting on a strong portfolio will jump straight to a confident-sounding solution, because selling certainty is what closes deals, even when the honest answer is that nobody knows the right flow until it's tested with real users.

## Making the Final Call

No portfolio review will fully de-risk a UX vendor decision — even strong agencies have a range of case study quality, and the best portfolio piece is not always the most relevant one to your problem. What the review should do is separate agencies who can show their reasoning from agencies who can only show their output. A vendor with three excellent, well-documented case studies and honest gaps elsewhere is a safer bet than one with twenty polished screens and no process trail behind any of them. Weight the interview as heavily as the portfolio itself: the questions a vendor asks you about your users and constraints often reveal more about their actual practice than anything in the deck.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many case studies should a UX vendor be able to show in detail?
Three to five well-documented case studies with process artifacts and outcome data are more valuable than twenty polished screens with no depth behind them. Ask the vendor to go deep on two or three projects rather than skim a long portfolio — depth reveals whether their process holds up under questioning.

### What's a reasonable request to make during a portfolio review?
Ask to see intermediate artifacts (wireframes, user flow diagrams, research notes) for a specific case study, not just final screens, and ask what a usability test changed about the design. Both requests are reasonable, standard practice, and any legitimate UX vendor should be able to satisfy them without hesitation.

### Is it a red flag if a vendor's portfolio spans very different industries?
Not by itself — strong UX practices transfer across industries. The red flag is when the underlying flow structure, information architecture, and interaction patterns are identical across industries with genuinely different user needs and regulatory constraints, which suggests templating rather than research-driven design.

### How do I verify that the team in the portfolio is the team I'll actually work with?
Ask for the named individuals who worked on specific case studies and request that those same people be included in the proposed staffing plan for your engagement, ideally with a short call before signing. A vendor unwilling to confirm this, or who reassigns junior staff after the contract is signed, is a common source of quality drop-off mid-project.

### What should I do if a vendor cites NDAs for every past project?
Ask them to describe the problem, process, and outcome verbally without showing screens — a legitimate NDA restricts visual disclosure, not the ability to discuss reasoning and results. If a vendor cannot speak to outcomes even in general terms across their entire portfolio, treat NDA citations as an evasion rather than a legitimate constraint.
