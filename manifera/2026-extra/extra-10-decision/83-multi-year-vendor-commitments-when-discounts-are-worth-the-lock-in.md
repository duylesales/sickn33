A vendor offers 15% off if you sign for three years instead of renewing annually. On a €600,000-a-year engagement, that's €270,000 over the term — real money, the kind of number that gets a CFO's attention in a budget review. The question that actually matters isn't whether the discount is real. It's whether you're confident enough in this specific vendor relationship, eighteen months from now, that locking in today's terms is worth giving up the annual re-evaluation point that's currently your best leverage and your best exit.

This is a recurring decision for any CFO managing outsourced engineering spend, because multi-year proposals show up constantly, discount percentages are always framed as the headline, and the actual trade-off — cost certainty and vendor investment in exchange for reduced flexibility and negotiating leverage — gets less scrutiny than it deserves. The right answer depends heavily on relationship maturity and market conditions, not just the size of the discount on offer.

## The Discount Math Vendors Lead With

Multi-year discounts typically run 10-20% off the equivalent annual rate, and vendors offer them for a real reason, not just as a sales tactic: committed multi-year revenue lets a vendor invest confidently in dedicated headcount, reduces their own sales and account-management overhead, and de-risks their bench planning. That's a genuine value exchange, not a trick — but the discount percentage alone is an incomplete way to evaluate the offer, because it says nothing about what you're giving up in exchange, which is the annual decision point where you'd otherwise reassess performance, renegotiate rate against current market conditions, or walk away cleanly if the relationship isn't working.

## What Lock-In Actually Costs You

The real cost of a multi-year commitment isn't visible in the contract's pricing table — it's the option value you're giving up. An annual term gives you a credible walk-away point every twelve months, which is itself a form of leverage even if you never exercise it, because the vendor knows renewal isn't guaranteed and behaves accordingly on service quality and responsiveness. A multi-year commitment removes that annual pressure point for the vendor, and underperformance that would trigger a real conversation at annual renewal can instead drift for another year or two under a locked term, with the only recourse being an early-termination clause that's usually expensive or restrictive by design. Price that option value explicitly against the discount before deciding — a 15% discount is a bad trade if it removes your only real lever on a vendor relationship still proving itself.

## When Multi-Year Commitments Make Sense

Multi-year terms are genuinely the right call under specific conditions: the relationship has a track record, typically 12-18 months of demonstrated performance under a shorter term first, the scope of work is stable and unlikely to shift dramatically, and the discount meaningfully exceeds what you'd expect from typical market rate movement over the same period. Locking in a strong, proven vendor relationship at a good rate before a market correction pushes rates upward is a legitimately smart move — Vietnam's software engineering labor market has seen steady wage growth in recent years, and a locked multi-year rate can outperform market by the contract's later years even without the headline discount factored in.

## When Rolling Annual Terms Are the Safer Bet

Annual or even shorter rolling terms are the better structure when the relationship is new, the scope is likely to evolve significantly, or the market itself is unsettled enough that locking in current rates risks looking expensive rather than smart in eighteen months. A new vendor relationship, however promising the pilot engagement looked, hasn't yet been tested against a real crisis, a scope change, or a difficult renegotiation — the moments that actually reveal vendor character. CFOs who commit multi-year before that track record exists are trading real flexibility for a discount on a relationship they haven't finished evaluating.

## Exit Ramps: The Clause That Makes Lock-In Tolerable

The clause that determines whether a multi-year commitment is reasonable or reckless is the exit provision, and it deserves as much negotiating attention as the discount itself. A workable structure includes an annual performance review with defined, objective triggers — SLA breaches, quality metrics, delivery timeliness — that permit early exit without the punitive penalties a straight early-termination clause usually carries. Ask specifically what it costs, in both notice period and financial penalty, to exit in year two of a three-year term versus honoring it to completion; if that number is prohibitively high regardless of vendor performance, you haven't negotiated a discount, you've negotiated a trap with a discount attached.

## Benchmarking Against a Moving Market

A multi-year rate locked today is a bet on where the market goes, and that bet deserves an explicit hedge. Negotiate a rate review or benchmarking clause tied to a defined market index or comparable-engagement data at each contract anniversary, with adjustment triggered only if the market moves beyond an agreed threshold in either direction — protecting you if rates fall and the vendor if rates rise. Without this clause, a multi-year lock-in is a one-directional bet: you benefit if the market rises past your locked rate, and you simply overpay with no recourse if it falls, since the vendor has no incentive to volunteer a rate reduction on a contract that's already signed.

## Making the Final Call

Multi-year commitments are worth the lock-in when the relationship has a proven track record, the discount clearly exceeds expected market movement, and the exit provisions are genuinely usable rather than punitive in name only. They're the wrong call on a new or unproven relationship, or when the discount is being used to paper over flexibility you'll need within the term. Price the option value you're giving up as explicitly as you price the discount you're gaining — that comparison, not the headline percentage, is the actual decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How large a discount should a vendor offer for a multi-year commitment?
Multi-year discounts typically run 10-20% off the equivalent annual rate. A discount at the low end of that range with weak exit provisions is a worse deal than a discount at the higher end paired with a genuine, usable performance-based exit clause, so evaluate the two together rather than the percentage alone.

### How much of a track record should I have with a vendor before signing a multi-year contract?
A reasonable minimum is 12-18 months of demonstrated performance under a shorter initial term. Committing multi-year before a vendor relationship has been tested against a real scope change, a difficult renegotiation, or a service issue means trading flexibility for a discount on a relationship you haven't finished evaluating.

### What exit terms should be negotiated into a multi-year vendor contract?
Negotiate an annual performance review with defined, objective triggers — SLA breaches, quality metrics, delivery timeliness — that permit early exit without punitive penalties. Ask explicitly what exiting in year two of a three-year term costs in notice period and financial penalty before signing.

### Should a multi-year contract include a market rate review clause?
Yes. Without a rate benchmarking clause tied to a market index or comparable-engagement data at each anniversary, a locked-in rate is a one-directional bet: you benefit if market rates rise past your locked number, but simply overpay with no recourse if rates fall, since the vendor has no incentive to volunteer a reduction.

### What's the real cost of vendor lock-in beyond the contract price?
The real cost is the option value of the annual walk-away point you give up. That annual leverage keeps a vendor motivated on service quality even if you never exercise it; removing it under a multi-year term means underperformance can drift for a year or more before your only recourse is an expensive early-termination clause.
