Nobody wakes up one morning to discover a vendor relationship has failed. It erodes in increments small enough that any single one is easy to excuse — a sprint demo that slips from Thursday to "early next week," a status update that gets slightly vaguer, a response time that creeps from same-day to next-day and nobody quite notices when. By the time the erosion is obvious enough to act on, it's usually been compounding for two or three quarters, and what would have been a fifteen-minute course-correcting conversation early on has become a formal escalation with a paper trail. A structured health check exists specifically to catch the fifteen-minute version of that problem before it becomes the formal one.

Most VPs of Engineering monitor vendor performance reactively — they notice a problem when it's already affecting delivery, not before. A deliberate health check cadence, run on metrics chosen to surface drift rather than just measure output, catches the pattern while it's still cheap to fix. This article covers the cadence that works, the specific leading indicators worth tracking, and how to run the conversation itself so it reads as genuine partnership maintenance rather than a surprise audit that puts a vendor on the defensive.

## Why Vendor Relationships Fail Gradually, Not Suddenly

The failure pattern in most struggling vendor relationships isn't a single dramatic incident — it's a series of individually minor degradations that compound because nothing forces an explicit, structured look at the trend line until something breaks. A vendor's best engineers rotate onto a more strategically important account for that vendor, and the replacement takes longer to ramp than anyone flags directly. Status updates get slightly less specific because the team is quietly under more pressure than they're admitting. None of these, in isolation, would trigger an escalation — and that's exactly the problem, because a relationship monitored only for escalation-worthy events misses the entire window where correction is cheap and reversible.

## The Health Check Cadence That Catches Drift Early

A two-tier cadence works better than either a single annual review (too infrequent to catch drift while it's small) or constant informal monitoring (too unstructured to reliably surface a trend). A monthly pulse check — a short, structured fifteen-to-twenty-minute conversation covering a small fixed set of questions, not a full status meeting — tracks the leading indicators below and takes almost no additional time from either side since it can often be folded into an existing recurring meeting. A quarterly deep review — a longer, more deliberate session comparing the current quarter's metrics against the trailing baseline, revisiting the original engagement goals and KPIs, and explicitly asking both sides whether anything has changed that the other side should know about — catches slower-moving drift the monthly pulse might individually miss but that becomes visible once three months are compared side by side.

## The Metrics That Predict a Relationship Problem Before It's a Delivery Problem

The most useful health check metrics are leading indicators of relationship quality, not just lagging indicators of delivery output — by the time delivery output visibly degrades, the relationship problem has usually existed for a while already. Track response time drift: how long does it take the vendor to respond to a written question or escalation, and is that number trending up over successive months. Track sprint commitment reliability: not just whether velocity is stable, but whether the vendor consistently commits to roughly what they deliver, since a growing gap between commitment and delivery — even with stable raw velocity — signals either overcommitment or a reluctance to flag risk honestly. Track escalation pattern: are the same categories of issue resurfacing repeatedly despite being "resolved" in a prior conversation, which indicates a root-cause fix isn't actually happening. And track proactive disclosure: is the vendor surfacing risks and problems before you ask, or only after you've noticed and raised it yourself — a relationship where the vendor has stopped proactively flagging risk has usually already started managing you rather than partnering with you.

## Running the Health Check Conversation Without It Feeling Like an Audit

How the health check is framed and delivered matters as much as what's measured. Present it from the outset, at kickoff, as a standing mutual practice rather than something introduced later in response to a specific concern — a health check that only appears after trust is already shaken reads as suspicion, while one built into the engagement structure from day one reads as normal partnership hygiene. Make it genuinely two-directional: ask the vendor what, from their side, would help the engagement run better, not just what they're doing wrong from your side — a vendor who experiences the health check as collaborative is more likely to surface an honest concern (a team member's bandwidth is stretched, a requirement was genuinely ambiguous) than one who experiences it as being graded. And keep the monthly pulse genuinely short and low-ceremony; a health check that turns into an hour-long formal review every month will get resented and eventually deprioritized by both sides, undermining the entire point of catching drift while it's still small.

## What to Do When the Health Check Surfaces a Real Problem

When a health check does surface a genuine issue, the response should match the size of what's actually been found — most drift caught early is resolvable through a direct, specific conversation and a defined correction window, not an escalation to contract remedies. Name the specific trend observed (response time has moved from same-day to two-day average over the past six weeks, for example), ask the vendor's own read on the cause, and agree a specific, time-bound correction with a follow-up check built in — not a vague request to "do better." If the same issue resurfaces after a defined correction window despite this conversation, that's the point where the pattern shifts from a health check finding to a genuine structural concern worth the more formal escalation covered in our piece on [when to switch software vendors mid-project](https://www.manifera.com/blog/when-to-switch-software-vendors-mid-project) — but most drift caught at the health check stage never needs to go that far.

## Making the Call

Run a two-tier health check cadence — a short monthly pulse and a deeper quarterly review — tracking leading indicators like response time drift, commitment reliability, recurring escalation patterns, and proactive risk disclosure rather than only lagging delivery output. Frame it as standing mutual practice from kickoff, keep it genuinely collaborative and low-ceremony, and respond to what it surfaces with a specific, time-bound conversation rather than an immediate leap to formal escalation. The relationships that erode into a crisis are almost always the ones nobody was structurally checking on until the crisis was already visible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a vendor relationship health check actually happen?
A two-tier cadence works best: a short fifteen-to-twenty-minute monthly pulse check tracking a small fixed set of leading indicators, plus a deeper quarterly review comparing the current quarter against the trailing baseline. A single annual review is too infrequent to catch drift while it's still small and cheap to correct.

### What metrics matter most in a vendor health check?
Leading indicators of relationship quality matter more than lagging delivery metrics: response time drift, sprint commitment reliability (the gap between what's committed and what's delivered), recurring escalation patterns, and whether the vendor proactively surfaces risk before being asked. By the time delivery output visibly degrades, the underlying relationship issue has usually existed for a while.

### How do I run a health check without it feeling like an audit to the vendor?
Introduce it at kickoff as a standing mutual practice rather than something added later in response to a specific concern, and make it genuinely two-directional by asking what would help the engagement run better from the vendor's side too. A health check framed as collaborative surfaces more honest disclosure than one experienced as being graded.

### What should I do when a health check surfaces a real problem?
Name the specific trend observed, ask the vendor's own read on the cause, and agree a specific, time-bound correction with a follow-up check built in, rather than a vague request to improve. Most drift caught at this stage resolves through a direct conversation and doesn't need to escalate to a formal contract remedy.

### When does a health check finding become a bigger structural concern?
If the same issue resurfaces after an agreed, defined correction window despite a direct conversation about it, that's the point where a health check finding shifts into a genuine structural concern worth a more formal escalation. Most drift caught early through a regular health check cadence never reaches that point.
