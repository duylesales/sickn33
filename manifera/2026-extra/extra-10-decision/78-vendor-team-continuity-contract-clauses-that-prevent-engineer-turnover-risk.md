Your lead backend engineer on the vendor team gives two weeks' notice. Does your contract obligate the vendor to backfill with someone at equivalent seniority, hand over documented context, and absorb the ramp cost — or does it simply let them swap in whoever's available and bill you the same rate while you re-explain your architecture from scratch? Most master service agreements are silent on this, which means the answer defaults to whatever the vendor finds convenient, not what protects you.

Engineer turnover is not a hypothetical risk in offshore and outsourced development — it's a normal operational fact, and pretending otherwise at contract signing is how CTOs end up renegotiating leverage they no longer have once a key engineer has already left. The vendors worth signing with don't have zero turnover; they have contractual structures that make turnover a manageable, bounded event instead of an open-ended risk the client absorbs entirely. This is written for the CTO negotiating or renewing a dedicated-team or staff-augmentation contract who wants continuity protections written into the paper, not promised verbally in the sales cycle.

## The Turnover Number Vendors Don't Volunteer

Annual attrition in Vietnam's software engineering market has historically run in the 15-25% range industry-wide, driven by a competitive hiring market for mid-to-senior talent across Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi — not a red flag in itself, but a number you should ask for directly and compare against a specific vendor's actual figure, not the industry average. A vendor with account-level attrition meaningfully below that range on engagements over 18 months is signaling something real about internal retention practices; one that can't produce a number, or gives you a vague "very low" without data, hasn't earned the benefit of the doubt. Ask specifically about attrition on engagements resembling yours in size and duration — a vendor's overall number can look fine while attrition on long-running dedicated teams tells a different story.

## Key Person Clauses: Naming Names in the Contract

The single highest-leverage clause available is a key person provision that names specific engineers — by role or by name, ideally both — as material to the engagement, with contractual consequences if they're reassigned or leave without a defined replacement process. A well-drafted key person clause specifies: advance notice before a named engineer is pulled off the account for internal reasons (as opposed to resignation, which can't be controlled), a minimum overlap period between an outgoing and incoming engineer, and — critically — that the client has input into, not just notification of, the replacement's qualifications. Without this clause, "your dedicated team" is a marketing phrase, not a contractual guarantee; the vendor is free to rotate staff across accounts as their own utilization needs dictate.

## Bench Depth and Backfill Guarantees

A key person clause only has teeth if the vendor actually has a bench to draw from. Ask vendors directly what their backfill timeline commitment is — a serious dedicated-team provider should commit to a specific window, typically 2-4 weeks for a like-for-like replacement, written into the SLA rather than left as an aspiration. Push further: ask what happens to your billing during a backfill gap. Contracts that let a vendor continue billing full rate for an unfilled seat, or that don't specify a ramp-discount period for a replacement engineer's first few weeks, quietly shift the full cost of turnover onto you. A fair structure discounts the replacement engineer's rate by 25-50% for the first two to four weeks, reflecting the reality that they aren't yet delivering full value.

## Knowledge Continuity: Documentation as a Contractual Deliverable

The most expensive part of turnover usually isn't finding a replacement engineer — it's the tribal knowledge that leaves with the departing one. Contracts rarely make documentation a deliverable in its own right, treating it instead as background hygiene that happens if there's spare time. Flip that: require architecture decision records, onboarding documentation, and runbooks as explicit, reviewable deliverables tied to milestone payments, not an unenforced best practice. Some CTOs go further and require a documentation audit at defined intervals — quarterly or at each major release — specifically to catch the gap between what's actually documented and what exists only in one engineer's head, before that engineer is the one who resigns.

## Notice Periods and the Transition Window

Standard notice periods in Vietnamese employment contracts run 30-45 days depending on seniority and tenure, which sets a rough ceiling on what a vendor can contractually guarantee for planned departures — but your MSA should specify a minimum overlap window that the vendor is responsible for delivering regardless of what an individual engineer's personal notice looks like, because the vendor employs a bench precisely to absorb this variability. A two-week hard minimum overlap between outgoing and incoming engineers, with a documented handoff checklist as a deliverable, is a reasonable floor to negotiate; anything shorter tends to produce replacement engineers who are debugging your codebase live in production rather than shadowing before takeover.

## Retention Incentives: What Good Vendors Do Internally

Contract clauses manage the consequences of turnover; the better lever is understanding what a vendor does to prevent it in the first place. Ask about internal retention mechanics directly: career progression paths for engineers staying on long-running accounts, compensation review cadence relative to local market rates, and whether engineers on dedicated accounts get cross-project variety or are locked into narrow scope indefinitely — a common driver of attrition on long engagements. Vendors with structured internal mobility and transparent comp benchmarking tend to show it in their attrition numbers, which is one more reason to ask for that number rather than accept a qualitative assurance.

## Making the Final Call

Turnover isn't preventable through contract language alone, but its cost to you is almost entirely a function of what the contract requires when it happens. A key person clause with real teeth, a bounded backfill window with billing protections, documentation as a contractual deliverable, and a defined transition overlap turn an unpredictable risk into a bounded, budgetable one. A vendor unwilling to commit to any of these in writing is telling you, indirectly, how they'll handle it when it actually happens.
