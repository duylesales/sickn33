If you view software as an expense to be minimized, you will hire the cheapest agency available. You will receive cheap code. And within two years, that cheap code will become a massive financial liability that requires a total, catastrophic rewrite.

**The Pain:** To save money, a European logistics firm hires a bottom-tier offshore agency to build their custom routing engine. The initial price is incredibly low. 
**The Agitation:** The agency cuts every architectural corner to hit the budget. They do not write automated tests, they hard-code database credentials, and they use deprecated open-source libraries. The initial "cheap" build wasn't cheap; it was a high-interest loan on Technical Debt, and the firm just defaulted.

## What the Research Says About the Price of Cutting Corners

The Consortium for Information & Software Quality (CISQ), in its 2022 Cost of Poor Software Quality in the US report (co-sponsored by Synopsys), estimated that accumulated software technical debt in the United States alone had grown to approximately $1.52 trillion — a figure roughly equal to the entire US IT labor budget for that year. That is not abstract; it is the aggregate bill for exactly the kind of corner-cutting described in the Pain scenario above: skipped tests, hard-coded credentials, deprecated dependencies, all quietly compounding interest until something breaks.

McKinsey's research on technology debt, based on a survey of 50 CIOs at financial-services and technology companies with revenues over $1 billion, found that CIOs estimate tech debt amounts to 20–40% of the value of their entire technology estate before depreciation, and that 10–20% of the technology budget nominally allocated to new products is instead being diverted to resolving tech-debt issues — with 30% of CIOs reporting that figure exceeds 20%. Perhaps most damning: 92% of the CIOs McKinsey surveyed reported spending less than a fifth of their current IT budget on reducing tech debt, even as 60% said their organization's tech debt had visibly worsened over the prior three years. In other words, most enterprises know the debt is growing and are still not funding the fix.

The Standish Group's long-running CHAOS Report, the most cited longitudinal study of IT project outcomes, found that only around 31% of software projects are classified as fully "successful," roughly 50% are "challenged" (over budget, late, or missing scope), and about 19% fail outright and are cancelled — with large, complex projects performing dramatically worse than small ones. A rushed, cost-minimized bespoke build is statistically more likely to land in the "challenged" or "failed" bucket than a governed one, which is precisely the failure mode Manifera's Amsterdam-governed architecture is designed to engineer out of the process from day one.
