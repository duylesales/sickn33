Most small and mid-sized European employers set data salaries by negotiation, one hire at a time. The result after three years is a team where pay reflects who negotiated hardest and when they joined rather than what they contribute, and where nobody can explain the differences. Pay bands for data teams solve this, and the deadline for solving it is no longer discretionary: pay transparency obligations are arriving across the EU and they make inconsistency visible.

## Why This Is Now Urgent

Directive (EU) 2023/970 on pay transparency and enforcement of equal pay is being transposed into national law across member states. Its provisions include giving applicants information about initial pay or its range before an interview, restricting employers from asking about pay history, giving employees the right to information on average pay levels for categories of workers doing the same work or work of equal value, and reporting on gender pay gaps with a joint assessment obligation where unjustified gaps persist.

Implementation details vary by country and organisations should check their national position. The practical direction is unmistakable: pay differences you cannot justify will become visible to the people affected by them.

For data teams this is uncomfortable, because the field has been characterised by rapid market movement, wide variation and heavy reliance on individual negotiation.

## Pay Bands for Data Teams: A Simple Framework

You do not need an elaborate system. Most teams under fifty people are served by five or six levels and a clear description of each.

**Define levels by scope and autonomy, not by years.** A workable progression: works on defined tasks with review; owns a component independently; owns a system and its outcomes; influences across teams and sets technical direction; shapes the function.

**Write two or three sentences per level** describing what someone at that level does, decides and is trusted with. Avoid inventories of technologies.

**Attach a salary range to each level**, with overlap between adjacent levels so that a strong person at one level can earn more than a new arrival at the next.

**Apply the same framework across roles.** Data engineer, data scientist, machine learning engineer and analytics engineer should sit on the same ladder. Different pay for equivalent scope is precisely what the directive targets.

**Have a parallel technical and management track** at senior levels, with equivalent pay.

## Benchmarking Honestly

Use several sources and treat all of them sceptically: published salary surveys for your country and sector, recruitment agency data, publicly advertised ranges, and what candidates tell you during processes.

Two common errors. Benchmarking against the wrong market — a regional manufacturer competing with Amsterdam technology salaries will fail, while a regional manufacturer competing with other regional employers will not. And benchmarking once and never revisiting, which produces the drift described below.

## Writing Level Descriptions That Work

The most common failure in levelling frameworks is describing levels by experience or technology. Both age badly and neither predicts contribution.

Describe instead what a person at each level does without help, what they decide, what they are trusted with, and how far their influence reaches.

A workable set for a data team:

**Level one.** Completes defined tasks with regular review. Learning the domain and the systems. Asks for help readily.

**Level two.** Works independently on defined problems. Owns a component. Reviews others' work. Knows when to escalate.

**Level three.** Owns a system and its outcomes in production, including when it breaks. Frames ambiguous problems into workable ones. Works directly with stakeholders.

**Level four.** Sets technical direction for an area. Influences work beyond their own. Makes trade-offs that others follow. Develops people.

**Level five.** Shapes what the function does and how it operates. Represents it credibly to senior leadership. Accountable for outcomes across multiple systems or teams.

Three sentences each is enough. The purpose is not to classify perfectly but to make conversations about progression concrete, and to give you a defensible basis for pay differences under equal pay scrutiny.

## Drift, Compression and How to Fix Them

Two problems appear in every growing team, and both are consequences of an external market moving faster than internal increases.

**Drift** is an individual falling behind. Someone hired at a fair rate receives modest annual increases while the market rises. After three years they are materially below what they could earn elsewhere, and they usually discover this from a recruiter rather than from you.

**Compression** is structural. Because new hires are recruited at current market rates while existing staff receive percentage increases, recent arrivals earn as much as more experienced colleagues. This is corrosive precisely because it is visible: people know roughly what new colleagues earn, and under transparency obligations they will know more precisely.

The fixes are straightforward but require budget.

Benchmark annually and adjust proactively rather than waiting for a resignation. Budget for correction separately from the general increase pool, so that fixing a lagging salary does not consume someone else's rise. Review at promotion and at significant scope change rather than at the next annual cycle. And when hiring above an existing colleague's salary, address it at the time rather than hoping nobody notices.

The cost of correction is almost always lower than the cost of replacement, and considerably lower than the cost of a pay equity dispute.

## What to Include in the Comparison

Base salary is only part of what people compare, and in Europe the surrounding elements are substantial.

**Pension.** Employer contributions vary enormously between countries and employers, and the difference over a career is significant. Candidates increasingly understand this.

**Holiday.** Statutory minimums differ across member states and many employers exceed them. Extra days have real value and low cost.

**Bonus and variable pay.** Be honest about realism. A bonus that has paid out twice in five years should not be presented as part of expected compensation.

**Equity.** Common in startups, rare elsewhere in Europe, and frequently misunderstood. If you offer it, explain the mechanics, the tax treatment in your country and the realistic scenarios.

**Working arrangements.** Hybrid flexibility, four-day arrangements, parental provisions and training budgets are valued and comparatively cheap.

**Allowances.** Travel, home office, meals and other arrangements differ by country and can be material.

Under pay transparency rules, the relevant comparison is pay for equal work or work of equal value, which includes complementary or variable components. Documenting how each element is determined, and applying that consistently, is the practical protection.

It is also the honest thing to give candidates, who cannot compare offers sensibly without it.

## Publishing Ranges in Vacancies

Employers resist this for predictable reasons, and most of the fears do not survive contact with practice.

*"Competitors will see what we pay."* They already know, from candidates and recruiters.

*"Everyone will ask for the top of the range."* They will, and you say what distinguishes the top from the bottom. A defined framework makes that conversation straightforward.

*"Our existing staff will see it."* This is the real concern, and it is an argument for fixing internal inconsistency rather than for concealing it, particularly when employees will soon have a right to comparative information.

What publishing gains is substantial. Applications from people whose expectations match, rather than three interviews ending in a decline. Higher application rates overall, since candidates increasingly filter out postings without a range. And an advantage in attracting candidates who have been underpaid elsewhere, who are disproportionately from groups that negotiate less assertively.

Practical advice: publish a genuine range, not a span so wide it conveys nothing. Say what determines position within it. Include the main non-salary elements. And ensure the range is one you will actually pay, because a candidate offered below the published minimum will tell people.

## Equal Pay for Work of Equal Value

The legal concept extends further than most employers assume, and data teams are exposed in a specific way.

The principle covers not only identical roles but work of equal value, assessed on objective criteria such as skills, effort, responsibility and working conditions. Two roles with different titles can constitute work of equal value.

For a data team this raises an uncomfortable question. If a data analyst and a machine learning engineer require comparable skill, effort and responsibility, a systematic pay difference between them needs an objective justification. Market rate alone is a weaker defence than employers hope, particularly where the lower-paid role is predominantly occupied by one group.

The same applies to adjacent functions: a business analyst and a data scientist doing comparable work, or a support engineer and a data engineer.

The practical response is to apply one levelling framework across the whole function, assess roles against the same criteria, and document the basis for any differences. Where a genuine market premium exists for a scarce skill, record the evidence and the reasoning.

None of this requires paying everyone the same. It requires being able to explain the differences in terms other than who negotiated hardest, which is a standard most organisations would want to meet regardless of the law.

## Running the Annual Cycle

A structure only works if it is maintained, and a light annual process is sufficient.

**Re-benchmark.** Update your view of market rates for each level using current sources. Rates for data roles have moved unevenly in recent years, and some specialisms have moved much faster than others.

**Place everyone.** Confirm each person's level against the framework. Where someone has grown into a higher level, the promotion and the pay adjustment should happen together rather than sequentially.

**Identify outliers.** Anyone below the range for their level, anyone unusually high, and any pattern by group. Look at the pattern by gender, and by any other characteristic where you have data, before anyone else does.

**Separate budgets.** General increases, corrections and promotions should be funded distinctly, or corrections will be made from the pool intended for everyone.

**Communicate the framework.** People should know the levels, what distinguishes them and roughly where the ranges sit. Opaque systems generate more suspicion than the numbers usually justify.

**Record the reasoning.** Decisions documented at the time are defensible later; reconstructed explanations are not.

This takes a manager two or three days a year and removes the most common source of unnecessary resignations in technical teams.

## Competing Without Matching

Not every employer can pay the top of the market, and pretending otherwise wastes everyone's time. Being explicit about the trade-off works better than being vague.

Organisations that recruit successfully below market rates tend to be clear about what they offer instead: genuine ownership of systems rather than a slice of one; a short commute to somewhere people want to live; predictable hours and respected boundaries; stability; a training budget that is real; flexibility that is written down rather than discretionary; and work whose purpose people find meaningful.

State this plainly in the vacancy alongside the range. Candidates for whom the trade-off works will apply; those for whom it does not will not, which saves three interviews.

What does not work is competing on salary while paying below market, which produces a stream of offers declined at the final stage and a reputation among recruiters.

## Real example

### The audit that took a day and changed six salaries

A mid-sized European software company had grown its data team from two to eleven people over four years without a pay structure. A new head of data ran a simple analysis before any obligation required it.

Plotting salary against scope of responsibility produced an incoherent picture. Two people hired during a competitive period earned more than colleagues with greater responsibility. The two most recent hires, both junior, had been recruited at rates close to people two levels above them, because the market had moved. One person who had been promoted twice had never had a salary review, and earned less than someone doing equivalent work who had joined a year earlier.

She built a five-level framework, placed everyone against it, and identified six people below the range for their level.

The correction cost roughly four per cent of the team's payroll. Two of the six had been actively interviewing elsewhere, and both stayed.

Her comment afterwards was that the cost of fixing it was less than replacing one of them, and that the conversation would have been far worse if the directive had forced it.

## Key Takeaways

- Pay transparency obligations are arriving across the EU and will expose unjustifiable differences.
- Five or six levels defined by scope and autonomy are sufficient for most teams.
- Apply one framework across data engineering, data science and machine learning roles.
- Benchmark against the market you actually compete in, and revisit annually.
- Correcting drift proactively is cheaper than losing people or defending it later.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: small employer) Do we need this with six people?
Yes, and it is easier now than at twenty. The structure prevents the incoherence rather than correcting it.

### (Scenario: employer worried about cost) What if we cannot afford to correct everyone?
Prioritise the clearest inequities, plan the rest over two cycles, and be honest with the people affected about the timeline.

### (Scenario: employer competing for scarce skills) Can we pay above band for a hard-to-fill role?
Yes, if the reason is documented and objective — scarcity, specific scope — rather than negotiating ability. That distinction matters under equal pay rules.

### (Scenario: recruiter asked about ranges) Must we publish salary in vacancies?
Requirements depend on national implementation, and providing range information to applicants is central to the directive. Publishing now is simpler than retrofitting later.

### (Scenario: employer with international staff) Do we pay by location?
Either approach is defensible, but be consistent and explain it. Ad hoc location adjustments create exactly the inconsistency the rules target.
