The question arrives around the fourth or fifth year. You are competent across several areas and expert in none, and colleagues who chose a niche appear to be advancing faster — or more precariously, depending on which niche. Whether to be a specialist or generalist in an AI career has no universal answer, and the useful framing is not which is better but what each buys you and what it costs.

## What Each Strategy Actually Buys

**Depth buys demand and price.** A person who is genuinely expert in forecasting, retrieval systems, computer vision or causal inference is scarce, and scarcity commands both interesting problems and better compensation. It also makes you findable: people seek you out for the thing you are known for.

**Depth costs optionality.** Your area may decline, be absorbed into tooling, or become commoditised. You are also less useful in a small team that needs one person to do everything.

**Breadth buys adaptability.** A generalist can move between problems, sectors and roles, and is the natural fit for small organisations where one person covers the whole stack. Breadth also positions you for lead and product roles, which require understanding how the parts connect.

**Breadth costs distinctiveness.** Being adequate at many things makes you replaceable and hard to recruit for, because nobody searches for a generalist. It can also mean being outperformed in every specific comparison.

The honest summary is that depth is the better strategy for being hired for something and breadth is the better strategy for being useful anywhere, and most durable careers combine them.

## Specialist or Generalist in an AI Career: The Shape That Works

The pattern most experienced European practitioners converge on is a T: broad working competence across the applied stack, with genuine depth in one or two areas.

The broad part is not optional. Anyone working in applied AI needs to handle data, write maintainable code, evaluate honestly, deploy something and explain it to a non-specialist. These are the entry conditions rather than a specialisation.

The deep part is what makes you memorable. It can be a technical area, a class of problem or a domain — and the strongest position is usually a combination: forecasting in energy, retrieval in regulated documents, computer vision in manufacturing.

The combination matters because it is far harder to replicate than either element alone. Many people know forecasting; fewer know forecasting and the operational reality of a distribution network, and those people are recruited rather than filtered.

## Which Specialisms Have Proved Durable

Rather than predicting the future, it is more useful to look at which areas have retained value through several shifts in the field.

**Forecasting and time series.** Every operational business needs it, the methods have been stable for decades, and demand spans energy, retail, logistics, manufacturing, transport and finance.

**Optimisation and operations research.** Scheduling, routing, allocation and planning. Mathematically stable, commercially valuable and consistently short of practitioners.

**Evaluation and experimentation.** Arguably the most durable of all, because every generation of technology needs someone to determine whether it works.

**Data platform and infrastructure.** Organisations with models need people to operate them, regardless of what the models are.

**Information retrieval and search.** Predates the current interest in it by decades and has been renewed rather than replaced by it.

**Computer vision in physical settings.** Manufacturing, agriculture, infrastructure and medicine, where the constraints are physical rather than fashionable.

**Causal inference.** Increasingly valued as organisations distinguish prediction from decision.

What has proved less durable: expertise tied to a specific framework, a specific vendor's platform, or a model architecture. These are worth knowing and not worth building an identity around.

## Domain Knowledge as the Third Axis

The depth-versus-breadth framing usually omits the dimension that matters most in practice.

Technical skill is widely distributed and increasingly assisted by tooling. Understanding of a particular industry — its processes, constraints, vocabulary, economics and failure modes — is not, and it cannot be acquired in a weekend.

The practical consequence is that a person with moderate technical depth and deep domain understanding is frequently more employable, and more effective, than a stronger technologist without it. They know which questions matter, whether an answer is plausible and what will actually be adopted.

Domain knowledge also compounds. Five years in energy, healthcare or manufacturing makes the sixth year more valuable, whereas five years with a particular framework makes the sixth year less so.

And it transfers more than people expect. Someone who understands one regulated industry understands the shape of regulated work generally: documentation, validation, human oversight and the pace that comes with them.

For people worried about specialising too narrowly, this is the reassuring observation. Choosing a sector is a safer commitment than choosing a technology, because industries persist and their data problems outlast every methodological fashion.

The strongest position remains the combination of both, which is why it is worth choosing a domain deliberately rather than by accident of employment.

## How the Calculation Changes by Career Stage

The right answer differs depending on where you are, which is why generic advice conflicts.

**Years zero to three.** Breadth, without question. Learn to handle data, write maintainable code, evaluate honestly, deploy something and explain it. Specialising before you know what the work involves means choosing on the basis of what sounded interesting in a course.

**Years three to six.** Begin to develop depth in whatever you find yourself drawn to and repeatedly good at. Take the projects that require it. This is when a reputation begins to form, and it forms around what you actually do rather than what you intend.

**Years six to twelve.** Depth pays. This is when the market seeks you out for a specific capability, compensation reflects scarcity, and you are trusted with problems rather than tasks. Maintain the broad base so that you remain employable outside your niche.

**Beyond twelve.** Most people either deepen further into a recognised expert position, broaden into leadership where breadth is the requirement, or combine depth with a domain and become the person an industry calls.

The transitions are not irreversible. People move between these patterns repeatedly, and a period of deliberate breadth after years of depth — or the reverse — is a normal and often refreshing part of a long career.

## Signs You Have Specialised Too Narrowly

A few warning signs are worth watching for, because narrowing happens gradually and is easier to correct early.

**Your skills are tied to one employer's stack.** If your expertise is in an internal platform, a customised vendor system or a proprietary toolchain, it may not transfer. Deliberately learn the open equivalents.

**You cannot describe your work without naming a product.** Expertise expressed as a tool name rather than a problem class is a signal.

**You would struggle in a small team.** If you could not handle the data engineering, deployment or stakeholder conversation yourself, you are dependent on an organisational structure that may not exist elsewhere.

**Your area has a single industry.** Some specialisms are tied to one sector's regulatory moment or technology cycle. Check whether the skill exists elsewhere.

**You have stopped learning outside your niche.** Three years of depth with no breadth produces a person who is excellent at one thing and unable to evaluate whether it is still the right thing.

The correction is not to abandon depth. It is to maintain a working competence in the surrounding areas: enough data engineering to build your own datasets, enough deployment to ship, enough statistics to evaluate, and enough communication to explain. That base is what makes depth safe.

## Signs You Have Stayed Too Broad

The opposite failure is less discussed and equally limiting.

**Nobody seeks you out.** Recruiters and colleagues approach people for a recognised capability. If you are never approached, it may be because there is nothing specific to approach you about.

**Your CV reads as a list of technologies.** Breadth described as exposure rather than as delivered outcomes suggests you have done a little of everything and finished none of it.

**You are always the second-best option.** In every specific comparison there is someone better suited, and you are hired only when the requirement is vague.

**Interviews go three questions deep and stop.** Depth in an interview comes from having stayed with a problem long enough to have opinions about it.

**You have changed area every eighteen months.** Enthusiasm for the new can prevent anything compounding.

The correction is to choose. Pick the area you enjoy most and are already best at, and spend two years going deeper rather than wider: take the projects that require it, read seriously, and build a track record in it.

Two years is enough to be genuinely good at something in this field. The people who never choose spend a decade at the same level of depth in six different areas, which feels productive and is not.

## Building Depth Deliberately

Depth rarely accumulates by accident, and the mechanisms are straightforward.

**Take the projects.** The fastest route is doing the work repeatedly on real problems with consequences. Volunteer for the ones in your chosen area even when they are less glamorous.

**Read properly.** Not announcements — the foundational material. Most specialisms have two or three books and a handful of papers that repay careful study, and few practitioners have read them.

**Build the thing nobody else will.** An evaluation harness, a benchmark, a tool for a recurring problem. Owning a piece of infrastructure in your area makes you the person who understands it.

**Teach it.** Explaining something to colleagues, writing it up or presenting at a meetup exposes the gaps in your understanding faster than anything else.

**Work with someone better than you.** A year alongside a genuine expert is worth several years alone, and it is a legitimate reason to choose one job over another.

**Accumulate failures.** Depth is largely a catalogue of ways things go wrong, and you acquire it by being responsible when they do.

**Stay long enough.** Depth requires seeing the consequences of your own decisions, which takes years in the same area rather than months.

## A Practical Way to Decide

If the question is live for you, a short exercise resolves it more reliably than reflection.

Write down the last five pieces of work you enjoyed most, and the last five you were best at. The overlap is usually obvious and usually surprising to nobody except you.

Then read twenty vacancies for roles you would want in three years. Note what they consistently require, and whether that intersects with the overlap above. Where it does, you have a direction that is both enjoyable and marketable, which is the only combination worth committing to.

Then ask what you would need to do this year to move toward it: a project, a role change, a specific piece of learning, a conversation with your manager.

And accept that this is revisable. Almost nobody's career follows the plan they made at thirty, and the value of the exercise is in directing the next two years rather than the next twenty.

## Real example

### The specialist who was not narrow

An engineer in Belgium spent seven years working on demand forecasting, first in retail and then in energy. Colleagues occasionally suggested he was limiting himself.

What he had actually accumulated was broader than the label. Forecasting had required time series methods, hierarchical modelling, data engineering for large series, evaluation discipline, optimisation for the decisions that consumed his forecasts, and enough domain understanding of both sectors to know what drove demand in each. He had deployed and operated systems and negotiated with planners who disagreed with him.

When his employer restructured, he was approached within a fortnight by three organisations in different sectors. None of them was looking for a forecasting specialist as such; each had a problem involving prediction under uncertainty and operational consequences, and his record answered it.

His own view was that he had not specialised in forecasting. He had specialised in a class of problem, and that class exists in every industry.

## Key Takeaways

- Depth makes you findable and valuable; breadth makes you adaptable and useful in small teams.
- The durable shape is broad competence with genuine depth in one or two areas.
- Combining a technical depth with a domain is harder to replicate than either alone.
- Some specialisms are narrow in method and broad in sector, which reduces the risk considerably.
- Fundamentals underlie everything and do not expire; tooling does.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: engineer in their first years) Should I specialise early?
No. The first three to five years are for breadth and fundamentals. Specialisation without a broad base produces a fragile career.

### (Scenario: generalist feeling replaceable) How do I build depth from here?
Choose the area you already enjoy most, take the projects that require it, and deepen deliberately over two years rather than switching fields.

### (Scenario: specialist worried about decline) What if my area becomes obsolete?
Most specialisms transfer more than they appear to. Evaluation, systems thinking and domain knowledge move with you; only the tooling is discarded.

### (Scenario: candidate choosing a niche) Which specialisms are safest?
Those narrow in method and broad in sector — forecasting, optimisation, evaluation, retrieval, data platform — because the demand is not tied to one industry.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we hire specialists or generalists?
Generalists for small teams and early capability, specialists once you have a problem that recurs and justifies depth.
