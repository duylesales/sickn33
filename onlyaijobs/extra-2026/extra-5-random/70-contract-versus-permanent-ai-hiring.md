A vacancy has been open five months, a project is slipping, and someone suggests hiring a contractor instead. It is a reasonable response and frequently the right one, and it is also how organisations end up two years later with critical systems nobody on the payroll understands. Contract versus permanent AI hiring is a decision about what kind of value you need, and getting it right saves both money and institutional knowledge.

## When Contracting Is the Right Answer

**Defined projects with an end.** A migration, a first production system, a specific model build, an audit of something existing. Work with a boundary suits a boundaried arrangement.

**Specialist skills needed briefly.** Deploying on edge hardware, designing an evaluation framework, setting up a data platform. You need the capability for four months, not forever.

**Capacity during a peak.** An existing team with a deadline and no headroom.

**Cover for absence.** Parental leave, long-term sickness, a gap between a departure and a replacement.

**Testing whether a capability is worth building.** Before committing to a permanent team, find out whether the problem is tractable.

**Speed.** A contractor can often start in weeks where a permanent hire takes months, and for time-critical work this alone justifies the premium.

## When Permanent Hiring Is the Right Answer

**Ongoing ownership.** Systems that run need someone responsible for them next year. Contractors leave; the model keeps producing predictions.

**Accumulated domain knowledge.** Knowing why a field is unreliable and what happened in 2024 takes years to build and is the most valuable asset a data person holds.

**Building capability.** If you want a team, you need people who will still be there to mentor the next hires.

**Work that cannot be specified in advance.** Ambiguous, exploratory work that evolves is poorly suited to a contract with defined deliverables.

**Cost over time.** For continuous work, employment is substantially cheaper.

## Contract Versus Permanent AI Hiring: The Cost Comparison People Get Wrong

A contractor's day rate looks like a large premium over an equivalent salary, and comparing them directly is misleading in both directions.

Against the contractor's rate, set the true cost of an employee: gross salary, employer social contributions, pension, holiday, sickness, equipment, training, recruitment cost and management overhead. Depending on the country, the loaded cost is considerably above the headline salary — the difference is substantial in much of continental Europe.

Against the employee, set the contractor's lack of holiday and sickness cost, their immediate availability, and the absence of termination obligations.

Then add the factor nobody prices: knowledge that leaves. If the contractor built something nobody else understands, the cost of their departure is paid later by whoever has to maintain it.

For a six-month project, contracting is often cheaper in total. For three years of ongoing work, it rarely is.

## Employment Classification Across Europe

This is the area where employers most often create problems for themselves, and the rules differ by country.

Broadly, authorities across Europe assess the substance of a relationship rather than what the contract calls it. Factors typically examined include whether the person works under your direction and control, whether they are integrated into your organisation, whether they bear commercial risk, whether they have other clients, whether they use their own equipment, whether they could send a substitute, and whether the arrangement resembles the work of your employees.

Where a relationship is found to be employment in substance, consequences can include liability for social contributions, tax, and employment rights including protection against dismissal.

The Netherlands has a well-known framework addressing this question, with enforcement practice that has changed over time; Germany has its own established tests; and several other member states have tightened rules in recent years, particularly where long single-client engagements are involved.

Practical guidance for hiring managers: take national advice rather than assuming; be cautious about multi-year single-client engagements with one individual; do not treat contractors identically to employees in scheduling, management and integration; and prefer engaging through a properly constituted company or an agency where that is the local norm.

## Scoping a Contract Engagement Well

The difference between a contract that delivers and one that disappoints is almost entirely in the scoping.

**Define the deliverable, not the time.** "A production classification service with documented evaluation, monitoring and handover" is a deliverable. "Six months of a data scientist" is an arrangement that drifts.

**Include a discovery phase.** One or two weeks, paid, before the main scope is fixed. This is where you both find out whether the data exists and whether the problem is tractable. Contracts written before discovery are frequently renegotiated after it.

**Write the acceptance criteria.** What must be true for the work to be complete, including documentation and handover.

**Name the internal counterpart.** Someone who will be there afterwards, who pairs with the contractor and can maintain what is built.

**Specify what happens if the answer is no.** A feasibility engagement that concludes the approach will not work has delivered value, and the contract should say so rather than treating it as failure.

**Agree intellectual property and data handling explicitly.** Who owns the output, what they may reuse, and the data processing arrangements required under GDPR.

**Set a review point** partway through, with the option to stop.

## Protecting Institutional Knowledge

The recurring failure of contract engagements is not quality; it is that the understanding leaves.

Six practices prevent it, and none of them are expensive.

**Pair from day one.** An internal engineer working alongside the contractor, not receiving a handover at the end. This is the single most effective measure, and it is the one most often skipped because the internal person is busy.

**Documentation as a deliverable.** Written during the work, reviewed by the internal counterpart, and required before final payment. Documentation promised at the end is documentation written in a rush.

**Code in your repositories, on your infrastructure.** Not on the contractor's machine, not in their account.

**Decision records.** Why this approach, why these features, what was excluded and why. This is the knowledge that disappears most completely and matters most later.

**A recorded walkthrough.** An hour of the contractor explaining the system, recorded, is worth more than fifty pages of documentation and takes an hour.

**A maintenance test.** Before the engagement ends, have the internal person make a small change and deploy it, with the contractor available but not doing it. If they cannot, the handover is not complete.

These conditions also attract better contractors, because competent professionals prefer engagements where their work will survive.

## The Blended Model That Usually Works

Most European organisations that use both arrangements well converge on a similar structure.

A permanent core owns the systems, the domain knowledge and the relationships. These are the people who know why the data behaves as it does and who will still be there in three years. Even a small permanent core — two or three people — changes everything, because there is someone to receive knowledge and someone accountable for what runs.

Contractors supplement this for defined pieces: a specialist capability, a project with a deadline, a peak in demand, or cover for absence. They work on bounded deliverables alongside permanent staff.

Consultancies and agencies fit a third role: larger pieces of work requiring a team, or capability the organisation does not want to build at all.

The arrangement that reliably fails is the inverse — a permanent manager with no permanent technical staff, and a rotating cast of contractors building things. The organisation accumulates systems and no understanding, and each new contractor spends a month learning what the last one knew.

If you are starting from nothing, the sequence that works is usually a contractor to prove the problem is tractable, then a permanent hire to own what follows, then further contract help as needed.

## Finding and Selecting Contractors

The market differs from permanent hiring and rewards different behaviour.

**Sources.** Former employees and colleagues are the best channel; people who have worked with you need no assessment. Specialist agencies and brokers in most European markets; professional networks; and referrals from other contractors, who know each other.

**Assessment.** Do not run a four-stage interview process for a three-month engagement; experienced contractors will decline. A technical conversation about their previous work, a conversation about how they scope and hand over, and two references from recent clients is usually sufficient and more informative.

**What to look for.** Evidence of delivery in comparable organisations, willingness to discuss what went wrong, a clear view on documentation and handover, and the ability to say when they think an approach will not work. The best contractors decline unsuitable engagements, which is a positive signal.

**What to be wary of.** A CV listing every technology, reluctance to name clients or provide references, resistance to a discovery phase, and anyone who agrees to your proposed scope without questioning it.

**Rates.** Vary widely by country, specialism and whether an agency is involved. Ask what is included and whether the rate covers expenses and travel.

## Managing Contractors Differently

Once engaged, the management approach should differ from how you manage employees, both legally and practically.

Focus on deliverables rather than on hours and presence. This is both how you get value and, in several European jurisdictions, part of what distinguishes a genuine contracting relationship from disguised employment.

Give them what they need immediately. Access, data, introductions and a clear brief on day one. A contractor waiting two weeks for a database account is an expensive way to test your onboarding process, and the cost is entirely yours.

Be available for decisions. Contractors cannot resolve organisational questions, and an engagement stalls quickly when the sponsor is unreachable.

Do not use them as general capacity. Adding ad hoc requests erodes the scope, delays the deliverable and moves the relationship closer to employment in substance.

Be clear about integration. Contractors should attend the meetings relevant to their work and not necessarily everything else; in some jurisdictions full integration into team rituals is itself a classification factor.

And treat them decently. The European contracting market is small and reputational: contractors talk to each other, and organisations known for poor briefs, late payment or chaotic sponsorship find their choices narrowing.

## Converting a Contractor to an Employee

This works well and is worth planning for rather than improvising.

The advantages are substantial on both sides. You have months of evidence about how someone works, which no interview process produces. They know your data, your domain and your people, so the expensive part of onboarding is already done. And the risk of a poor hire is close to zero.

Practical points. Check any agency agreement first; many include conversion fees or notice periods, and discovering this after an offer is awkward. Discuss it openly rather than allowing it to become an unspoken possibility. Be realistic about compensation: a contractor's day rate does not convert into a salary directly, and the conversation is easier when you present the whole package including pension, holiday, security and development.

Be honest that the work will change. Employment brings meetings, longer horizons, maintenance and organisational responsibilities that contracting does not. Some people prefer contracting for exactly these reasons and will decline, which is a legitimate answer.

## Real example

### The system that left with the contractor

A European insurer engaged a contractor to build a claims triage model. He was excellent: the system went live in five months and performed well.

His contract ended. Eight months later the model's accuracy declined noticeably. The remaining team could run it but could not diagnose it. The code was competent and sparsely documented, the feature definitions lived in a notebook, and the decisions about which claims were excluded from training had been made in conversations nobody had recorded.

They re-engaged the contractor at a higher rate for three weeks to explain his own system, and then spent four months rebuilding parts of it so that internal staff could maintain it.

The head of claims analytics changed the policy rather than the approach. Contractors remained welcome, with three conditions written into every engagement: an internal engineer paired with them throughout, documentation delivered as part of the work rather than at the end, and a handover session recorded before the final invoice.

Their next two contract engagements produced systems the team still maintains.

## Key Takeaways

- Contract for defined projects, scarce skills, capacity and speed; employ for ownership and accumulated knowledge.
- Compare loaded employment cost against day rate, not salary against day rate.
- Knowledge that leaves with a contractor is a real and deferred cost.
- Pair every contractor with an internal person from day one.
- Employment classification rules across Europe are enforced; take advice on arrangements that resemble employment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: manager with an unfilled vacancy) Should we contract while we keep searching?
Often yes, provided the contractor works on bounded deliverables and an internal person is involved. Do not give them ownership of anything permanent.

### (Scenario: employer worried about classification) Is there a legal risk?
Yes, and it varies by country. Arrangements where the person works under your direction, on your schedule, integrated into your team, resemble employment and are scrutinised. Take national advice.

### (Scenario: employer comparing rates) Why are AI contractor rates high?
Scarcity, short engagements, unbilled periods between them, and the fact that contractors fund their own pension, insurance and equipment.

### (Scenario: small company) Can we afford either?
A short, well-scoped contract engagement is often the most affordable way to find out whether the capability is worth building permanently.

### (Scenario: employer wanting to convert) Can a contractor become an employee?
Frequently, and it works well because both sides have evidence. Check any agency contract for conversion fees before you start.
