The reference stage is where candidates relax and occasionally where offers unravel. It is also poorly understood: practice differs substantially across Europe, several things employers ask for are legally constrained, and candidates frequently hand over contacts without thinking about what will be said. Handling references and background checks deliberately takes an hour and removes a category of avoidable risk.

## What Actually Happens at This Stage

In most European AI and data hiring, the process is modest: one or two conversations with previous managers, confirmation of employment dates and sometimes verification of a degree. For roles involving finance, critical infrastructure, children or government work, more formal screening may apply.

Timing matters. References are normally taken after a conditional offer, not during the process, and a request to contact your current employer before you have resigned should be declined without hesitation. Reputable employers understand this and expect it.

What referees are asked is usually narrow: what you did, how you worked with others, whether they would work with you again, and occasionally whether there were performance or conduct concerns. In several countries, former employers are cautious about saying anything beyond dates and role, either as company policy or from concern about liability.

## References and Background Checks Across Europe

Practice varies enough to matter if you are moving between countries.

In **Germany and Austria**, employees are entitled to a written employment reference on leaving, and these documents follow established conventions where seemingly neutral phrasing carries recognised meaning. If you work there, obtain and read yours carefully.

In the **Netherlands and Scandinavia**, references are usually informal conversations, often with the candidate's former manager directly, and are treated as one input among several.

In **France, Italy and Spain**, written certificates of employment confirming dates and position are standard, with substantive references handled informally.

In **Ireland and international companies**, practice resembles the Anglophone model with structured reference requests.

Across the EU, GDPR applies to reference and screening data: it must be collected for a defined purpose, be proportionate, be retained only as long as necessary, and you have rights of access to it.

## Choosing Referees Well

The instinct is to offer the most senior person who will say yes. The better choice is the person who saw the most of your actual work.

Rank candidates for the role by three criteria: how closely they observed you working, how recently, and how well they can speak about the specific things this employer cares about. A team lead who reviewed your code weekly is a far stronger reference than a director who saw two presentations.

Aim for coverage rather than repetition. One manager who can speak to delivery and judgement, one peer or senior colleague who can speak to collaboration and technical depth, and if relevant one stakeholder or client who experienced the outcome.

Avoid: people you have not spoken to in years, anyone who left the organisation before your most significant work, friends whose relationship to you is personal, and anyone who might be surprised to be contacted.

For career changers and early-career candidates, academic supervisors, internship managers, open source maintainers you have contributed alongside, and clients from freelance work are all legitimate.

Keep three referees ready at any time, refreshed as you change roles. Assembling them under time pressure after an offer is how people end up nominating someone unsuitable.

## Preparing Your Referees

A referee who is caught off guard gives a vague reference, and a vague reference reads as reservation.

Contact each one before you give their details. Ask directly whether they would be comfortable giving a positive reference — the phrasing matters, because it gives someone with reservations an easy way to decline, which is far better for you than a lukewarm call.

Then give them what they need. Which company, which role, roughly what it involves, and a short reminder of what you worked on together: the projects, your contribution and the outcome. Two or three sentences each. People remember less than you assume, particularly about work from several years ago.

Mention what the employer is likely to focus on. If the role emphasises production ownership, remind them of the system you operated. If it emphasises stakeholder work, remind them of the presentations.

Tell them when to expect contact, and thank them afterwards regardless of outcome.

This is not coaching a referee to say something untrue. It is ensuring that an honest person has the information needed to be accurate, which is in everybody's interest and is standard practice across European professional hiring.

## What Employers May and May Not Check

Screening in the EU must have a lawful basis and be proportionate to the role, and several common practices sit closer to the line than employers assume.

**Employment and education verification** is generally accepted, and in a field where credentials are occasionally embellished it is reasonable.

**Criminal record checks** are restricted in most member states to roles where they are justified — work with children or vulnerable people, financial services, security-sensitive positions — and rules on what may be requested and by whom vary considerably by country.

**Credit checks** are used in some financial roles and are not generally appropriate elsewhere.

**Social media screening** is common in practice and legally uncomfortable. Data protection authorities across Europe have advised that examining a candidate's private social media is generally not justified, and that any screening should be limited to professional information relevant to the role and disclosed to the candidate.

**Health information** may not normally be requested, with narrow exceptions where specific fitness is a genuine requirement, and disability-related questions are constrained by equal treatment law.

**Salary history** is increasingly restricted, and pay transparency rules under Directive (EU) 2023/970 are moving further in that direction.

If something seems disproportionate, asking why it is needed is entirely legitimate, and a well-run employer will have an answer.

## Security Clearance and Sensitive Sectors

Some AI and data roles in Europe require vetting that goes well beyond an ordinary reference check, and candidates should know what they are agreeing to before starting the process.

Defence contractors, national security bodies, some critical infrastructure operators and certain government roles require national security clearance. Processes differ by country but typically involve detailed personal history, financial information, foreign connections, and interviews with you and sometimes with people who know you.

Practical implications worth considering in advance: clearance usually requires citizenship or long residency in the country concerned, which may exclude otherwise qualified candidates; the process takes months, which affects start dates; dual nationality and family abroad are not disqualifying but are examined; and the information required is genuinely personal.

Some organisations will sponsor clearance for a new hire, others require it already held, which makes existing clearance a valuable asset in those markets.

Beyond formal clearance, some sectors apply their own vetting: financial services regulators require fitness and propriety assessments for certain roles, and organisations handling health data or children's data apply sector-specific checks.

None of this should deter you from these sectors. It is simply worth knowing that the timeline and the intrusiveness differ from ordinary hiring.

## Handling a Difficult History

Most careers contain something awkward: a role that ended badly, a dismissal, a manager you clashed with, a short tenure, a gap. The handling matters more than the fact.

**Be first.** If something will surface, mention it yourself, briefly and factually, before references are taken. A candidate who explains a difficult exit calmly is credible; one whose explanation arrives after the employer heard something else is not.

**Keep it short and non-blaming.** "The role changed direction after a reorganisation and we agreed it was not working" is sufficient. Extended criticism of a former employer damages you rather than them.

**Provide alternatives.** If one previous employer will not give a substantive reference, offer two others and say why.

**Know what they can say.** In several European countries, written references must be truthful and, in some, may not be deliberately damaging; German practice in particular has established case law on this. If you believe a former employer is misrepresenting your employment, it is worth taking advice.

**Show the trajectory.** What you have delivered since matters more than what happened three years ago, and employers assess risk rather than history.

Most hiring managers have encountered all of this before. What they are assessing is your judgement in how you present it.

## Your Rights Over the Data

Screening produces personal data about you, and you have rights over it that few candidates exercise.

Under GDPR you may ask an employer or recruitment agency what data they hold about you, why, how long they will keep it, and with whom they have shared it. You may ask for inaccurate data to be corrected. In many circumstances you may ask for it to be deleted, particularly after a process has concluded and you were not hired.

Employers should tell you in advance what checks will be performed, and consent obtained under the pressure of a job offer is a weak basis, which is why most rely on other lawful bases and on proportionality.

Practical steps worth taking: ask at offer stage what screening will be done and by whom; if a third-party screening provider is used, ask for their privacy information; and if you are not hired, ask for your data to be deleted rather than retained indefinitely in a talent pool, unless you want to be contacted again.

If something is recorded about you that is wrong, correct it early. An error in a screening record can follow you between employers when the same provider is used.

None of this requires confrontation. A single polite email at the right moment is usually sufficient.

## For Employers: Doing This Properly

Reference and screening practice is one of the places where hiring processes quietly become unfair or unlawful, and the fixes are straightforward.

Take references only after a conditional offer, with the candidate's knowledge, and never contact a current employer without explicit written permission. Breaching this can cost someone their job and will cost you your reputation as an employer.

Ask the same questions of every referee, recorded against the same criteria. Unstructured conversations invite impressions that are neither comparable nor defensible.

Keep screening proportionate to the role and document why each check is necessary. A criminal record check for a data analyst role will be difficult to justify to a regulator or a candidate.

Tell candidates in advance what will be checked, retain the results only as long as needed, and delete them when the process ends.

And treat what you hear proportionately. A single lukewarm reference is weak evidence, particularly when the referee is distant from the work. Ask the candidate about it rather than withdrawing quietly.

## Real example

### The reference that nearly cost an offer

A machine learning engineer accepted a conditional offer and supplied two referees: his current team lead, whom he had asked, and a manager from a previous role three years earlier, whom he had not contacted in two years.

The second reference was lukewarm. The manager remembered him working on a project that had been cancelled and, asked what he had delivered, could not recall specifics. Nothing negative was said, and the absence of substance was itself a problem.

The hiring manager raised it. The candidate was able to explain the project and why it had been cancelled, and offered a third referee — a colleague who had worked with him daily on the system that actually shipped that year.

That conversation resolved it and the offer stood.

His conclusion was that he had chosen a referee by seniority rather than by what they had actually seen him do, and that a five-minute call beforehand would have avoided the whole episode.

## Key Takeaways

- References are normally taken after a conditional offer; never permit contact with a current employer before you resign.
- Choose referees who saw your work closely, not those with the most senior titles.
- Ask every referee in advance and remind them what you worked on together.
- Practice differs across Europe; written references are standard in German-speaking countries.
- Screening must be proportionate and lawful under GDPR, and you have rights over the data.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employed candidate) Can I refuse to give my current employer as a referee?
Yes, and you should until you have resigned. Offer a former manager or a senior colleague instead and explain why.

### (Scenario: candidate who left badly) What if a previous employer will say something negative?
Choose other referees, be honest with the new employer about the situation before they hear it elsewhere, and focus on what you have delivered since.

### (Scenario: candidate asked for payslips) Can employers ask for proof of my previous salary?
Several European countries restrict questions about salary history, and pay transparency rules are tightening this further. You can decline and state your expectations instead.

### (Scenario: candidate facing a criminal record check) Is this normal?
In specific sectors, yes — finance, children, security and some public roles. Outside these, a blanket criminal record check may not be proportionate.

### (Scenario: employer) How should we take references properly?
After a conditional offer, with the candidate's consent, using the same questions for every candidate, and recording only what is relevant to the role.
