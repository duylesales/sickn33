Drug development is slow, expensive and mostly unsuccessful, which is precisely why the industry invests heavily in anything that improves the odds. Europe has a dense life sciences landscape — large pharmaceutical companies, biotech clusters, contract research organisations and academic medical centres — and a persistent shortage of people who can combine computational skill with biological and clinical understanding. AI jobs in pharma and biotech are varied, stable and unusually well suited to people arriving from scientific backgrounds.

## Where AI Jobs in Pharma and Biotech Are Found

**Target identification and early discovery.** Analysing genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic data to identify and validate biological targets. Heavy bioinformatics and statistics, often close to academic research.

**Molecule design and screening.** Predicting properties, prioritising compounds, and designing candidates. Chemistry-aware machine learning, structure prediction and generative approaches all appear here.

**Preclinical and translational research.** Imaging analysis, biomarker discovery, animal study data and in vitro assay analysis.

**Clinical development.** Trial design, site selection, patient recruitment, data quality monitoring and analysis of trial and real-world data.

**Manufacturing and quality.** Process control, yield optimisation, batch release analytics and deviation analysis in highly regulated production environments.

**Commercial and medical affairs.** Forecasting, market analysis, safety signal detection in pharmacovigilance, and literature and document retrieval systems.

Pharmacovigilance and regulatory document work deserve particular mention: they are large, growing and frequently overlooked by candidates chasing discovery roles.

## The Regulatory Environment

Life sciences is a validated-systems industry. Software used in regulated activities must be qualified, changes must be controlled, and evidence must be retained. Clinical data handling follows good clinical practice; manufacturing follows good manufacturing practice; both assume documentation, audit trails and defined responsibilities.

On top of this, personal health data is a special category under GDPR, requiring specific legal bases and careful handling. Systems that support clinical decisions may fall under medical device regulation, and the EU AI Act adds further obligations for certain uses. Companies in this sector are generally well practised at regulatory change, which makes them less chaotic than the rules sound.

For engineers, the implication is familiar from banking and automotive: reproducibility, documentation and traceability are part of the work, not an obstacle to it.

## The Data Realities of Life Sciences

Life sciences data has a characteristic shape that catches out people arriving from consumer technology: wide, small and expensive.

A transcriptomics experiment may measure twenty thousand genes across forty samples. A clinical trial may have three hundred patients and hundreds of recorded variables. Each observation cost real money and took months to obtain, so discarding data is not an option and overfitting is a constant danger.

This inverts the usual instincts. Regularisation, careful feature selection, cross-validation done correctly, and honest reporting of uncertainty matter far more than model capacity. Pre-specification of analyses — deciding what you will test before you look — is standard practice in clinical work and increasingly expected elsewhere, precisely because the risk of finding spurious patterns in wide data is so high.

Batch effects are a second recurring trap. Samples processed on different days, by different technicians, on different instruments, carry systematic differences that models happily learn. Detecting and correcting for them is a core competency, and interviewers frequently ask about it because candidates who have not worked with experimental data usually have not encountered the problem.

## Clinical Development: The Largest Practical Employer

More data roles exist around clinical trials than around molecule design, and they are less contested.

The work includes designing trials and calculating the statistical power needed; selecting countries and sites based on historical recruitment performance; predicting enrolment and flagging sites at risk of falling behind; risk-based monitoring, which uses statistical signals to focus quality oversight where it matters; managing and cleaning trial data; and analysing safety signals as they emerge.

Increasingly, real-world data — from health registries, insurance claims and hospital records — is used to design trials, contextualise results and support regulatory submissions. Working with it requires understanding both the statistics of observational data and the governance of health information across different European jurisdictions, where access rules differ substantially between countries.

Contract research organisations, which run trials on behalf of sponsors, are significant employers in this space and hire data professionals continuously. They are often overlooked by candidates who only search pharmaceutical company names, and they offer exposure to many therapeutic areas quickly.

## Discovery: What Has Actually Changed

Computational methods in early discovery have advanced substantially, and it is worth being precise about what that means for jobs.

Protein structure prediction moved from a research problem to a routine tool, which shifted work toward downstream questions: interaction prediction, binding affinity, design of binders and enzymes. Generative approaches now propose candidate molecules and proteins at scale, which has moved the bottleneck from generation to selection and experimental validation.

The practical consequence is that discovery teams need people who can integrate computation with laboratory reality. A model proposing a thousand candidates is only useful if someone can rank them against synthesisability, toxicity risk, intellectual property position and the throughput of the assay that will test them.

This is why employers repeatedly emphasise collaboration with medicinal chemists and biologists. Candidates who present themselves purely as modellers are less attractive than those who can describe how their output fed a real experiment and what the experiment showed — including when it contradicted the model, which it frequently does.

## Manufacturing and Supply in Regulated Production

Pharmaceutical manufacturing is a rich and under-appreciated area for data work, particularly as biologics and cell and gene therapies expand across Europe.

Biological processes are variable by nature, so understanding which process parameters drive yield and quality has direct financial and patient-supply consequences. Typical projects include multivariate process monitoring, predicting batch outcomes, investigating deviations, optimising cell culture conditions and forecasting supply.

The regulatory frame is strict. Changes to a validated process require justification and often regulatory notification, so models tend to support decisions rather than make them automatically. Data integrity requirements — attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate records — shape how systems are built and logged.

Skills that transfer directly from industrial analytics apply here: multivariate statistics, time series, design of experiments and a comfort with sensor data. What must be learned is the vocabulary of validated environments and the discipline of change control.

For candidates in general manufacturing analytics looking for a more resilient sector, pharmaceutical production is a realistic move, because the underlying analysis is familiar and the domain knowledge is learnable on the job.

## Language Models in Life Sciences

Text is everywhere in this industry, which has made retrieval and document processing one of the fastest-growing application areas.

Scientific literature is the obvious case: keeping up with publications in a therapeutic area is impossible manually, and systems that search, summarise and link findings are in active development. Regulatory affairs is another: submissions run to thousands of pages assembled from many sources, and drafting support with strict traceability to source documents is valuable.

Pharmacovigilance handles large volumes of adverse event reports arriving as free text in many languages, which must be coded, assessed and reported within regulatory timelines. Classification and extraction here have measurable operational value and clear quality requirements.

Medical information, clinical protocol drafting, and internal knowledge retrieval over standard operating procedures round out the picture.

What distinguishes these projects from general enterprise work is the evidentiary standard. An answer without a verifiable source is not usable, hallucination is a patient safety and regulatory issue rather than an inconvenience, and human review is mandatory in most workflows. Engineers who understand grounding, citation and evaluation are well placed here, and the domain can be learned.

## Entering the Sector From Outside

Three routes work reliably.

**From academia.** A PhD or postdoc in a relevant field — biology, chemistry, physics, statistics, bioinformatics — is the traditional path into discovery and translational roles. What academics usually need to add is software engineering discipline, an understanding of project timelines and the ability to present to non-specialists.

**From engineering.** Data engineers, platform engineers and ML engineers are hired for their skills, not their biology. Life sciences organisations have large, fragmented data estates and consistently struggle to recruit people who can build reliable infrastructure. Domain learning happens gradually and nobody expects it on day one.

**From adjacent regulated industries.** People from medical devices, food production, chemicals or clinical software transfer well because they already understand validated environments.

Contract research organisations, clinical software vendors, laboratory instrument companies and health data platforms all hire continuously and are easier to enter than the best-known pharmaceutical brands. A year in any of them changes how the sector reads your CV, and internal mobility within large life sciences employers is generally well supported.

## Where the Work Is in Europe

Life sciences activity concentrates in recognisable clusters, which is useful for planning a search.

Switzerland around Basel and Zurich hosts major pharmaceutical headquarters and research sites. The Netherlands has Leiden and the Amsterdam region, with the European Medicines Agency in Amsterdam. Belgium's corridor around Leuven, Ghent and Brussels is dense in biotech. Denmark and southern Sweden form the Medicon Valley cluster around Copenhagen and Lund. Germany has Munich, Heidelberg, the Rhine-Main area and Berlin. France has Paris-Saclay and Lyon. Ireland concentrates pharmaceutical manufacturing, and Cambridge and Oxford anchor activity in the UK. Spain, Italy and Poland host substantial manufacturing and growing clinical operations.

Remote work is partially possible for computational and clinical data roles and rarely for laboratory-adjacent or manufacturing positions. Many employers run hybrid arrangements with expectations of regular on-site presence for collaboration with experimental teams.

## Interviews and What Employers Probe

Hiring processes in life sciences tend to be thorough and slower than in software, often involving a scientific presentation as well as technical and panel interviews.

Prepare for four areas. First, statistical rigour: expect questions about multiple testing, confounding, batch effects, sample size and why a result might not replicate. Answering "I would check whether the effect survives correction" is better than describing a sophisticated model.

Second, reproducibility: how you version data and code, how someone else could rerun your analysis in two years, how you record decisions. This is asked far more often here than elsewhere.

Third, collaboration with scientists or clinicians: describe a time you changed your approach because a domain expert told you something the data did not show.

Fourth, judgement about limitations. Saying clearly what your analysis could not conclude is treated as a strength, not a weakness, by interviewers who spend their working lives distinguishing signal from wishful thinking.

If you are presenting a project, choose one with a negative or ambiguous result and explain how you handled it. In an industry where most programmes fail, the ability to reason honestly about disappointing data is the most valuable trait a candidate can demonstrate.

## Real example

### The biomarker model that survived audit

A computational biologist at a European biotech built a model predicting which patients were likely to respond to a therapy, using data from an earlier trial.

The modelling took six weeks. Preparing it for use in planning the next trial took considerably longer, because everything had to be reproducible: exact data versions, environment specification, every preprocessing decision recorded, analysis pre-specified rather than chosen after seeing results.

During an internal audit, a reviewer asked why two samples had been excluded. The answer was documented in the analysis log with the date and the reason, agreed in advance with the study statistician. That single fact, he said afterwards, was the difference between a model that informed a trial and a model that would have been discarded.

He now tells new hires that in this industry the analysis is not the deliverable. The evidence that the analysis was done properly is the deliverable.

## Skills That Get You Hired

Statistics, especially survival analysis, mixed models and multiple testing; comfort with high-dimensional data and small sample sizes; reproducible computational practice; and enough domain literacy to talk to biologists, chemists or clinicians. Python and R both remain common, and R is genuinely important in clinical statistics.

For discovery roles, a scientific background is usually expected. For data engineering, platform and clinical operations roles, it is often not.

## Key Takeaways

- AI jobs in pharma and biotech span discovery, preclinical, clinical, manufacturing and commercial functions.
- Reproducibility and documentation are the deliverable as much as the model is.
- Health data is a special category under GDPR, with strict handling requirements.
- Statistics matters more than deep learning in most roles, particularly in clinical work.
- Non-discovery areas such as pharmacovigilance, trial operations and manufacturing are accessible entry points.
