Consulting is one of the most common starting points for AI careers in Europe, and one of the most common things people leave. The reasons are consistent: travel, billing pressure, projects that end before anything is deployed, and the frustration of handing over work you will never see again. Moving from consulting to in-house AI solves several of these and introduces others. This guide describes what actually changes, which of your skills transfer, and how to make the move without being undervalued in the process.

## What Genuinely Changes

**You live with your decisions.** In consulting, a shortcut taken in month two is someone else's problem in year two. In-house, it is yours. This single fact reshapes how you work, usually for the better.

**The timescale extends.** Projects that would have been a twelve-week engagement become eighteen-month programmes with maintenance attached. You will spend a large share of your time on systems that already exist.

**Your influence comes from relationships, not from a contract.** Consultants borrow authority from the engagement and the sponsor. Internally you build it slowly, through delivery and through knowing people.

**Nobody counts your hours.** Utilisation targets disappear. For many people this is the largest quality-of-life improvement; for a few it removes a structure they found motivating.

**Depth replaces breadth.** You will know one organisation's data extremely well instead of six superficially. Whether that is a gain or a loss depends entirely on your temperament.

## From Consulting to In-House AI: Skills That Transfer

Consultants arrive with real advantages, and should say so plainly in interviews.

They have seen many organisations, which gives them pattern recognition about what works and what stalls. They can run a workshop, write a clear document and present to executives — skills that are scarce in internal teams. They are used to entering an unfamiliar domain and becoming useful in weeks. They handle ambiguity and unreasonable deadlines without drama. And they have usually worked across several industries, which makes them good at spotting when an organisation believes its problem is unique when it is not.

These are genuine strengths, and internal teams value them once a consultant stops presenting them as consulting skills and starts presenting them as delivery skills.

## The Gaps to Close

Three gaps recur, and interviewers probe all of them.

**Production ownership.** Have you operated something you built, for a year, including the incidents? Many consultants have not, and the honest answer is better than an evasive one.

**Depth in one system.** Consulting rewards moving on; internal work rewards understanding why a field has three possible meanings.

**Maintenance and incrementalism.** Much internal work is improving something that exists, not building something new. Enthusiasm for greenfield projects only is a warning sign to hiring managers.

## Why People Leave, and Whether Moving Fixes It

It is worth being precise about the reason, because different reasons point to different destinations.

**Travel and location.** Moving in-house usually fixes this completely, and it is the most common driver in Europe, particularly for people with young families.

**Never seeing the outcome.** In-house fixes it, provided you join a team that operates what it builds rather than one that hands work to a separate platform group.

**Utilisation pressure.** Gone, though replaced by other pressures such as roadmaps and stakeholder demands.

**Shallow technical work.** Not automatically fixed. Plenty of internal teams do shallow work too. Diagnose this during interviews rather than assuming.

**Poor management.** Bad managers exist everywhere. Moving for this reason without checking the new manager carefully often reproduces the problem.

**Wanting more money.** Consulting at senior levels can pay well, and some in-house moves reduce earnings. If pay is the main driver, verify the numbers before assuming.

Writing down the real reason matters, because it determines the questions you should ask in interviews. A person leaving because of travel should ask about site expectations; a person leaving to see outcomes should ask who runs the system after launch.

## Choosing the Right Internal Team

Internal environments vary enormously, far more than consultancies do, and the same job title can mean completely different lives. Diagnose before you accept.

Ask what is currently in production and who maintains it. A team with three running systems is a different proposition from one with a proof of concept and an ambition.

Ask who decides what the team works on. If priorities are set entirely elsewhere and arrive as fixed requests, you have moved from serving external clients to serving internal ones, which is a smaller change than you hoped.

Ask about the data platform. Whether reliable pipelines exist determines whether you will spend your first year modelling or plumbing.

Ask how long the team has existed and what happened to the last three projects. Cancelled projects are not disqualifying, but the answer tells you how the organisation behaves.

Ask about the manager's background and how long they have been there. In-house, your manager matters more than in consulting, because they are your route to resources, scope and visibility.

Finally, ask to speak to an engineer without the manager present. Confident teams arrange it readily.

## Positioning Your Consulting Experience

Consultants often undersell themselves by describing engagements rather than outcomes.

Rewrite each project around what changed for the client. "Six-month engagement delivering a demand forecasting solution" says nothing. "Replaced spreadsheet-based forecasting for a retailer with eleven thousand product-store combinations; reduced stockouts on slow-moving items; the system has run daily since 2025 and is maintained by their internal team, whom I trained" tells a hiring manager exactly what you can do.

Name your personal contribution explicitly. Consulting work is collective and CVs often blur this. Say what you built, what you decided and what someone else did.

Address the two predictable objections before they are raised. Explain what you know about operating systems in production, even if that knowledge came from watching clients struggle. And say plainly that you want to own something long term, with a reason that sounds like you rather than like an interview answer.

Finally, translate the vocabulary. Words like engagement, workstream, deliverable and client stakeholder read as consulting language and quietly signal that you have not yet made the shift. Say project, team, system and colleague.

## What the First Six Months Feel Like

Most former consultants report a similar arc, and knowing it in advance helps.

**Months one and two: unexpected slowness.** Nobody assigns you a workstream. Access takes weeks. Meetings have no defined outcome. Consultants find this disorienting because they are trained to be productive within days of arriving somewhere new.

**Months two to four: the depth surprise.** You discover the data has twelve years of history and eight system migrations behind it, and that the colleague who understands the 2019 change is the most valuable person in the building. Your consulting instinct to move fast collides with a genuine need to understand.

**Months four to six: influence begins.** You have delivered something small, people start asking your opinion, and you begin to see the organisation's real decision-making structure, which is never the one on the chart.

Two habits shorten the arc. First, deliver something visible and useful within the first two months, even if it is modest. Second, meet people deliberately across functions rather than staying within the data team, because in-house influence is built horizontally and consultants are unusually good at it once they remember to try.

## Skills You Will Need to Build

Three areas separate a consultant who becomes a strong internal engineer from one who plateaus.

**Operating software.** Deployment, monitoring, alerting, incident response and the discipline of on-call if your systems warrant it. If you have never been woken by something you built, you have not yet had the education. Volunteer for it early.

**Legacy and incremental work.** Improving a system built by someone who has left, without rewriting it, is a core internal skill. It requires reading code sympathetically, making small safe changes and resisting the urge to start again. Consultants often arrive with a bias toward greenfield and have to unlearn it.

**Long-horizon prioritisation.** With no engagement scope to contain you, you must decide what not to do over a period of years, and defend it. This is harder than it sounds and is what distinguishes senior internal engineers.

Alongside these, keep the consulting habits worth keeping: writing things down, structuring a discussion, presenting clearly to non-technical audiences, and finishing what you start. Internal teams frequently lack all four, and a former consultant who retains them becomes disproportionately influential within a year.

## Negotiating the Move

A few practical points that recur in European moves out of consulting.

**Level.** Consultancy grades rarely translate. Anchor on responsibility: how many systems, how many people, what decisions are yours. Ask what distinguishes the level above the one offered, and whether the gap is bridgeable within a year.

**Total package.** Consulting compensation often includes bonus and allowances that mask the base. Compare like with like: base, pension contribution, holiday, bonus realism, travel time no longer unpaid, and any equity. In several European countries pension arrangements differ substantially between employers and are worth real money.

**Notice period and handover.** Consultancies often have long notice periods and may hold you to client commitments. Confirm the date you can realistically start before agreeing one.

**Non-solicitation clauses.** Some consulting contracts restrict joining a client. If the employer you are joining is a current or recent client, check this carefully and early; it is a common and avoidable problem.

**Scope in writing.** Ask for the first year's expectations in the offer or in a written summary. Ambiguity that seems harmless at signing becomes the reason people leave after a year.

## When Staying Is the Better Choice

Not everyone should leave, and consultancies in Europe have changed in ways worth considering before you go.

Many now run managed services and long-term platform engagements where teams operate what they build for years, which removes the main frustration while keeping variety. Some have internal product groups. Several offer genuinely good training, structured progression and exposure to a range of industries that no single employer can match.

If your dissatisfaction is with a specific project, a specific manager or a specific travel pattern, an internal move within the firm may solve it faster than a job change, and you keep the seniority you have built.

Consulting also suits certain people permanently: those who become bored after eighteen months on one problem, those who like variety and pace, and those who enjoy the commercial side. There is no career hierarchy in which in-house is the mature destination; it is simply a different job.

## Real example

### The consultant who was asked what happened next

A data scientist at a European consultancy interviewed for a machine learning engineer role at a logistics company. He presented a demand forecasting project he had led for a retail client, with a clear result and a strong technical story.

The hiring manager asked a simple question: what happened to it afterwards? He did not know. The engagement had ended at handover.

He answered honestly, and then said what he would have done differently — the monitoring he would have insisted on, the documentation he would have written for the client's team, the decision he now believed was wrong because it assumed a data feed that the client could not maintain.

That answer got him the job. The manager later said that every consultant they interviewed had a polished delivery story, and that he was the only one who had thought seriously about what his work required after he left.

## Key Takeaways

- In-house work means living with your decisions and maintaining what you build.
- Consulting skills in communication, ambiguity and rapid domain entry transfer well.
- The gaps to close are production ownership, depth and appetite for maintenance.
- Answer the "what happened next" question honestly; it is the one that matters.
- Choose the team carefully; internal environments vary far more than consultancies do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: consultant worried about pay) Will I take a salary cut?
Sometimes, though less often than assumed. Compare total conditions including travel, hours and bonus structure rather than base salary alone.

### (Scenario: consultant with no production experience) Will that disqualify me?
Rarely, if you are honest and show you understand what production requires. Teams hire for judgement and trajectory as well as track record.

### (Scenario: consultant considering seniority) Will my title carry across?
Consulting grades often do not map cleanly. Focus on scope and responsibility rather than on matching a title.

### (Scenario: consultant missing variety) Will I be bored?
Some people are. Large organisations with several domains, or product companies with many surfaces, retain variety better than a single-process business.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we hire from consultancies?
Often yes. Screen specifically for ownership and maintenance appetite, and value the communication skills that internal teams frequently lack.
