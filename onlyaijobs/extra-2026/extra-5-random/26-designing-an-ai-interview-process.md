Most technical hiring processes are inherited rather than designed. A team copies the stages it experienced elsewhere, adds a round after a bad hire, and ends up with five interviews that measure the same thing three times and miss the things that matter. Designing an AI interview process deliberately takes about a day and pays back immediately, because it shortens time to hire, improves consistency and stops strong candidates leaving mid-process. This playbook sets out how to build one for machine learning, data science and data engineering roles.

## Start From the Job, Not From the Stages

Write down what the person will actually do in their first year. Not the job advertisement — the real work. Then list the four or five capabilities that determine whether they will do it well.

For a machine learning engineer building production systems, those might be: writing maintainable code, framing a problem and choosing an appropriate method, evaluating honestly, deploying and operating systems, and working with non-technical stakeholders. For a research-oriented role the list looks different. For a data engineer, different again.

Each capability gets assessed once, by someone qualified to assess it, with an agreed standard. Anything not on the list does not get its own stage. This single discipline removes most of the redundancy in typical processes.

## Designing an AI Interview Process: A Four-Stage Default

**Stage 1 — Screening call (30 minutes, recruiter or hiring manager).** Confirm mutual interest, explain the role honestly, check practical constraints such as location, availability and salary range. Salary should be discussed here; under the EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970 member states are introducing requirements to provide pay range information to applicants, and early transparency prevents wasted processes.

**Stage 2 — Technical conversation (60 minutes, engineer).** Go deep on one project the candidate has done. Ask why they made each decision, what they would do differently, what went wrong. This is the highest-signal hour in most processes.

**Stage 3 — Applied exercise (90 minutes live, or a short take-home with a live discussion).** Realistic, scoped and related to your actual work. Evaluate reasoning as much as output.

**Stage 4 — Team and stakeholder round (60 minutes).** Collaboration, communication, and a real conversation with someone from the business side the person will support.

Four stages, ideally within two to three weeks. Add a fifth only if you can name the specific capability it measures.

## What Each Stage Should Actually Measure

Ambiguity about purpose is what makes interviews redundant. Assign each stage a single primary question.

**Screening call: should we both invest further time?** It is not a technical filter. It confirms the role matches what the candidate wants, checks practical constraints and sells the job honestly. Recruiters should be briefed well enough to describe the technical work in two sentences without jargon.

**Technical conversation: has this person done real work, and do they understand why it worked?** Depth over breadth. One project, thirty minutes of questions, following the candidate's decisions rather than a checklist.

**Applied exercise: can they do the work with their normal tools?** Realistic constraints, access to documentation and search, and permission to use the assistants they would use on the job.

**Team round: can they work with us, and can we work with them?** Collaboration, disagreement, communication with non-specialists.

If two stages would answer the same question, merge them. If no stage answers a capability on your list, you have a gap that will surface as a bad hire in six months, which is the far more expensive outcome.

## Designing an Applied Exercise Worth Doing

The practical stage is where most processes go wrong, either by testing something irrelevant or by demanding unreasonable effort. A good exercise has six properties.

**It resembles your work.** A small extract of a real problem, sanitised. Candidates learn about the job while you learn about them.

**It is scoped.** Ninety minutes live, or three hours at home. Say clearly what you expect within that time and that you are not looking for completeness.

**It is open-book.** Documentation, search and coding assistants allowed, because that is how the work is done. If you want to see reasoning, ask about the decisions afterwards.

**It has no single right answer.** The discussion of trade-offs is the signal.

**It is graded against criteria written in advance.** Problem framing, code clarity, evaluation approach, communication of limitations.

**It respects the candidate's data and effort.** Never use candidate submissions in your product. Delete them after the process in line with your retention policy, and say so.

Offering a choice — live session or take-home — accommodates people with caring responsibilities or current jobs, and costs nothing.

## Questions That Produce Signal

Certain questions separate candidates reliably; others produce rehearsed answers.

High-signal questions:

- "Walk me through a project where the first approach did not work. What did you do?"
- "How did you know the system was performing well after deployment?"
- "What would break first if usage increased tenfold?"
- "Describe a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about what to build."
- "What did you decide not to do on that project, and why?"
- "If you had two more weeks on it, what would you fix?"

Low-signal questions: puzzle-style brainteasers, algorithm trivia unrelated to the role, questions about the internals of a framework the team may replace next year, and anything the candidate can answer by reciting a blog post.

The most informative follow-up in any technical interview is simply "why?" repeated three times. Candidates who have genuinely done the work keep producing reasons; candidates who have watched someone else do it run out at the second or third level. This works across seniority, across specialisms and across languages, and it requires no preparation from the interviewer beyond attention.

## Calibrating Interviewers

A structured process still fails if interviewers apply different standards. Three practices fix most of it.

**Write the rubric together.** For each stage, agree what a weak, adequate and strong answer looks like. Half an hour with the team produces a shared standard and surfaces disagreements about the role itself, which is useful before rather than after hiring.

**Shadow before assessing.** New interviewers sit in on two interviews and write an assessment that is compared with the experienced interviewer's, without affecting the decision.

**Review outcomes.** Every few months, look at who was rejected and why, and whether the stage that rejected them was measuring what it claimed. Teams frequently discover that one interviewer rejects almost everyone, or that a stage has drifted into testing a personal preference.

Also agree what the bar is in advance. "Would I be pleased if this person joined the team" is a workable standard; "would I hire them over the best person I have ever worked with" is not, and produces processes that never conclude.

Write assessments before the debrief. Discussion after independent scoring surfaces disagreement; discussion before it produces consensus around whoever spoke first.

## Candidate Experience Is Part of the Design

In a market where experienced AI engineers have options, the process itself is a recruiting argument. Candidates infer how you run projects from how you run interviews.

The elements that matter most are simple. Tell candidates the full process at the first contact, including the number of stages and the expected timeline. Reply within a week at every stage. Give a decision when you said you would, even when it is negative. Provide at least one sentence of specific feedback to anyone who reached a technical stage.

Make scheduling easy for employed candidates by offering early, late and flexible slots. Confirm who they will meet and in what format. Send the practical exercise details in advance so nobody is surprised.

And be honest about the role. Describing a maintenance-heavy position as greenfield research gets people through the door and out again within a year, which costs far more than the vacancy would have.

Candidates talk to each other. In the relatively small European AI community, a reputation for a respectful, decisive process brings referrals, and a reputation for silence after four rounds follows an employer for years.

## Legal and Fairness Considerations in Europe

Hiring in the EU carries obligations that shape process design, and a structured process satisfies most of them naturally.

Equal treatment law prohibits discrimination on protected grounds, and consistent criteria applied to every candidate are both fairer and easier to evidence. Many employers now remove photographs, dates of birth and other personal details from applications at screening.

Data protection applies throughout: candidate data must have a lawful basis, be retained only as long as necessary, and be handled transparently. Tell candidates how long you keep their application and ask before retaining it in a talent pool.

Automated tools deserve particular care. Under the EU AI Act, AI systems used in recruitment and selection are treated as high-risk, bringing obligations around risk management, data governance, transparency, human oversight and record keeping. GDPR separately restricts decisions based solely on automated processing with significant effects. If you are considering CV screening tools, video assessment or ranking systems, involve legal advice early rather than after procurement.

Pay transparency is the other moving piece. Under Directive (EU) 2023/970, member states are implementing rules on providing pay information to applicants and restricting questions about salary history; publishing a range in the vacancy is the simplest way to stay ahead of it.

## Measuring Whether the Process Works

You cannot improve a hiring process you do not measure, and the useful metrics are few.

Track time from application to decision, and the drop-off at each stage. A stage where half of candidates withdraw is a design problem, not a quality filter. Track the offer acceptance rate; repeated declines usually indicate a pay or process problem rather than a market problem. Track how long candidates wait between stages, which is the most common reason for losing people to faster employers.

The harder and more valuable measurement is predictive validity: do the people who scored well in your process perform well in the job? Six to twelve months after each hire, ask the manager which stage best predicted what they now see. Over a handful of hires, patterns emerge — and teams are often surprised to find that the stage they were proudest of predicted nothing.

Review rejected candidates occasionally too. If a person you rejected is thriving in an equivalent role elsewhere, that is information about your bar.

## Real example

### Five rounds, no hires, and the fix

A European scale-up spent seven months trying to hire two machine learning engineers. The process had five technical stages including an algorithm interview, a take-home requiring around fifteen hours, a system design round, a live coding session and a panel.

Two offers were made in seven months. Both were declined. Analysis of the pipeline showed that most candidates withdrew during the take-home, and that the algorithm round rejected several people the hiring manager had rated highly in conversation.

They rebuilt the process: the take-home was replaced by a ninety-minute live exercise using a small sample of real data, the algorithm round was removed, and the system design round was merged with the technical conversation. Total candidate time dropped from about twenty hours to four.

Three hires followed within ten weeks. The head of engineering noted that the removed stages had not been protecting quality; they had been screening for availability of free time.

## Scoring and Decisions

Define what each stage measures and what good looks like before you run it. Interviewers write their assessment before discussing with others, to avoid anchoring. Decisions are made against the capability list, not by general impression. A structured process is also easier to defend under equal treatment obligations, and easier to improve, because you can see which stage is rejecting people.

## Key Takeaways

- Derive the process from the real job and assess each capability once.
- Four stages within two to three weeks suits most AI and data roles.
- A deep conversation about the candidate's own work is the highest-signal stage.
- Long take-homes screen for free time, not ability.
- Write assessments independently and decide against defined criteria.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: hiring manager under time pressure) Can we hire in two interviews?
Yes for junior roles and small teams, provided both are structured and one involves practical work. Speed is a genuine competitive advantage.

### (Scenario: team debating take-homes) Should we use a take-home assignment?
Only if it takes under three hours, is optional, and is discussed live afterwards. Otherwise use a live exercise.

### (Scenario: employer hiring across countries) Do the same stages work everywhere in Europe?
The structure travels; expectations about formality, notice periods and salary discussion differ, so brief candidates clearly.

### (Scenario: employer using AI screening tools) Can we use AI to screen candidates?
Take advice first. Recruitment systems are treated as high-risk under the EU AI Act, and GDPR rules on automated decision-making apply.

### (Scenario: small company) We hire once a year — is this worth the effort?
Yes, and more so. Infrequent hiring means no practised instincts, which is exactly when structure helps most.
