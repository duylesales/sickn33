Most AI vacancies are written the same way: a paragraph of company boilerplate, a bulleted wish list of twenty technologies, a line about a dynamic environment, and no salary. Then the hiring manager wonders why the applications are irrelevant. Writing an AI job posting well is not a marketing exercise; it is a filtering exercise. Every sentence should help the right person recognise themselves and help the wrong person move on. This guide sets out what experienced data and machine learning professionals look for, how to structure a posting so they read it, and which details decide whether they apply.

## Start With the Problem, Not the Company

Engineers scan for the work. The first two sentences after the title should describe what the person will build and why it matters to the business. Compare:

*"We are a leading organisation on an exciting digital transformation journey, looking for a talented Data Scientist to join our dynamic team."*

*"You will build the forecasting models that decide how much stock our 180 stores order each week. Today this runs on a spreadsheet maintained by one planner, and it is the largest source of waste in our supply chain."*

The second version tells a candidate the domain, the scale, the maturity level and the impact in one breath. It also tells unsuitable candidates to stop reading, which is exactly what a good posting should do.

## Choose a Title That Matches the Work

Titles in this field are inconsistent, and a vague one costs you applications. Use the term the market uses for the work, and add a qualifier if it helps: "Machine Learning Engineer — demand forecasting", "Data Engineer (Azure, analytics platform)", "Applied Scientist — computer vision".

Avoid invented titles such as "Data Ninja" or "AI Evangelist", and avoid "Data Scientist" as a catch-all when the role is really analytics or engineering. Candidates who arrive expecting modelling and find dashboards leave within a year, which costs far more than the extra thought it takes to name the job correctly.

## Describe the Data and the Stack Honestly

Experienced candidates want to know what they will actually work with. Include:

- **The data**: what it is, roughly how much, where it lives, how clean it is.
- **The stack**: languages, cloud, orchestration, storage, and what is already in place versus what they will build.
- **The maturity**: is there a platform, are models in production, who maintains them.
- **The team**: size, composition, who they will learn from, who they will report to.

Honesty here is not a weakness. "Our data is spread across three systems and needs consolidation" attracts people who like building foundations and repels people who would be miserable doing it. Both outcomes are wins.

## Write Requirements You Actually Mean

The standard wish list — five years of everything, a PhD, ten technologies — filters out strong candidates and attracts the overconfident. Research on job adverts consistently finds that long requirement lists deter well-qualified applicants, particularly women and career changers, more than they deter unsuitable ones.

A better pattern:

- **Must have** (three to five items): the things without which the person genuinely cannot do the job.
- **Helpful** (three to five items): experience that would accelerate them.
- **Not required**: say explicitly what you are not asking for, such as "no PhD required" or "no prior experience in our sector required; we will teach you".

Keep language plain and avoid gendered or exclusionary phrasing such as "rockstar", "aggressive self-starter" or "young dynamic team" — the last of which is also legally risky in several European countries.

## Publish Pay, Location and Practicalities

Three details decide whether many candidates apply at all:

**Pay.** Publish a range. The EU Pay Transparency Directive will require employers to give pay information to candidates before interviews and prohibits asking about pay history. Beyond compliance, a range saves everyone time and signals confidence.

**Location.** Give the exact office address, not just the city, plus the hybrid policy in days per week. Candidates increasingly filter by commute, and "Amsterdam" can mean anything from a canal-side office to a business park 40 minutes away.

**Practicalities.** Contract type, working language, visa sponsorship, and the interview process with a realistic timeline.

## A Structure That Works for Writing an AI Job Posting

A reliable structure for writing an AI job posting, in the order candidates read:

1. **Title** — precise, market-standard, with a qualifier.
2. **The problem** — two or three sentences on what they will build and why.
3. **What you will do** — four to six bullets describing real activities, not responsibilities in the abstract.
4. **The data and stack** — what exists, what they will build.
5. **The team** — size, composition, manager, who they learn from.
6. **Must-haves** — three to five.
7. **Helpful, not required** — three to five, plus what you explicitly do not require.
8. **What we offer** — salary range, benefits, learning budget, flexibility, equipment.
9. **Practicalities** — exact address, hybrid policy, contract, language, sponsorship.
10. **Process** — steps and timeline, with a named contact.

Keep paragraphs short. Most candidates read on a phone, often in the evening, and scan before they commit to reading.

## Words That Cost You Candidates

Certain phrases reliably reduce response quality: "fast-paced environment" (often read as chaotic), "wear many hats" (read as understaffed), "work hard, play hard" (read as long hours), "rockstar" or "ninja" (read as unserious), "competitive salary" with no range (read as below market), "we are like a family" (read as weak boundaries). None of these are fatal on their own, but a posting full of them signals that nobody senior reviewed the text.

Replace them with specifics. If the environment really is fast-paced, say what that means: "we ship weekly and expect models to reach production within a quarter."

## Writing for Different Seniority Levels

**Junior roles.** Emphasise mentoring, structure and learning. State what the first six months look like, who reviews their work and what training exists. Keep requirements minimal and explicitly welcome career changers and recent graduates. Avoid asking for years of experience in tools that have existed for two years.

**Mid-level roles.** Emphasise ownership and interesting problems. Candidates at this level are usually employed and selective; they respond to specifics about the systems they would own and the team they would join.

**Senior and lead roles.** Emphasise scope, influence and context: how the team fits the organisation, what decisions they would make, what is broken that they would fix, and what support they would have. Senior candidates read for constraints as much as opportunities — budget, mandate, reporting line.

Using the same text for all three levels is the most common cause of mismatched applications.

## Inclusive Language and Legal Boundaries

European equal treatment law prohibits discrimination on grounds such as age, gender, disability, religion, nationality and sexual orientation, and job adverts are one of the most visible places where this is tested. Phrases such as "young team", "native speaker" or "recent graduate only" can create legal exposure as well as narrowing your pool.

Practical substitutes: describe the language level required rather than nativeness ("professional working proficiency in Dutch for stakeholder meetings"); describe the experience level rather than age; state flexibility and accessibility positively ("we support part-time arrangements and flexible hours"). If your organisation uses AI tools in recruitment, note that the EU AI Act treats certain employment uses as high-risk, and GDPR governs how candidate data is processed and retained.

## Describing AI Work Without Hype

Many postings promise more than the role delivers: "you will build cutting-edge generative AI systems" for a job that mostly involves cleaning data and maintaining dashboards. Candidates discover the gap in week three, and the cost is a resignation within a year plus a reputation that spreads through a small professional community.

A more effective approach is to describe the mix honestly, including the unglamorous parts. Something like: "Roughly half your time will go to data pipelines and quality, a quarter to modelling and a quarter to working with the operations team on how forecasts are used." Candidates who accept that description arrive with the right expectations, and those who need a purely modelling role select themselves out — saving both sides months.

The same applies to generative AI. If the work is building retrieval systems over internal documents with careful evaluation, say that. It is genuinely interesting work, and describing it accurately attracts people who will do it well rather than people chasing a buzzword.

## Practical Details That Get Overlooked

- **Working language**, stated plainly, including whether documentation and meetings differ.
- **Visa sponsorship**: whether you can sponsor, and whether you are a recognised sponsor where that applies.
- **Equipment and tooling**: hardware, cloud budget, GPU access for roles where it matters.
- **On-call**: if models run in production and the team carries a pager, say so.
- **Travel**: to client sites, plants or other offices, and how often.
- **Contract type and probation**, and the path to permanent employment where relevant.

A posting's reach depends on where it appears and how it is presented. ## Measuring Whether Your Posting Works

Track three numbers per vacancy: how many applications arrive, how many are relevant enough for a first conversation, and how many candidates withdraw and why. A low relevance rate usually means the title or the first paragraph is wrong. Withdrawals at the offer stage usually mean pay, location or hybrid policy were unclear in the text. Refine the posting between rounds rather than repeating the same advert for months.

## A Reusable Checklist

Before publishing, check that your posting:

- Names the problem and the data in the first paragraph.
- Uses a title candidates actually search for.
- Distinguishes must-haves from helpful extras and states what is not required.
- Publishes a salary range and the main benefits.
- Gives the exact address, hybrid policy and working language.
- States the interview process and timeline with a named contact.
- Describes the unglamorous parts of the job honestly.
- Avoids exclusionary phrasing and unnecessary degree requirements.
- Can be read on a phone in three minutes.

If a posting meets all nine criteria, you will usually receive fewer applications and hire faster — which is the point. A vacancy is not an advertising campaign; it is the first filter in a process, and every hour spent making it precise saves several hours of screening later.

## Real example

### Rewriting one vacancy

A logistics company advertised for a "Data Scientist" for three months. The posting listed Python, R, SQL, Spark, TensorFlow, PyTorch, Kubernetes, Tableau, Power BI, AWS and Azure; required a master's degree and five years' experience; described the company as a market leader; and gave no salary or address. It attracted 60 applications, mostly from candidates with no relevant background, and produced no hires.

The hiring manager rewrote it as "Machine Learning Engineer — delivery time prediction". The first paragraph explained that the company promises delivery windows to customers and that the current estimates are wrong often enough to generate thousands of support calls each month. It described the data — three years of shipment records, route data and weather — and the stack, noted that there was no ML platform yet and that the first year would involve building one, listed three must-haves and three nice-to-haves, published a salary range and the exact address, and set out a four-step interview process completed within three weeks.

The rewritten posting attracted 23 applications. Eleven were relevant, four were interviewed and two offers were made. The hire started six weeks later.

## Key Takeaways

- Lead with the problem and the data, not company boilerplate.
- Use a market-standard title that matches the actual work.
- Separate genuine must-haves from helpful extras, and say what you do not require.
- Publish a pay range, the exact location, hybrid policy and the interview process.
- Honest descriptions filter candidates better than aspirational ones.

### (Scenario: recruiter) How long should an AI job posting be?
Long enough to describe the problem, data, team, requirements and practicalities — usually 500 to 800 words. Detail helps; padding does not.

### (Scenario: hiring manager) Should we publish a salary range?
Yes. It improves the quality of applications and prepares you for EU pay transparency requirements.

### (Scenario: employer with a messy data landscape) Should we admit our data is not ready?
Yes. It attracts builders and prevents early resignations from candidates who expected a mature platform.

Three to five genuine must-haves, plus a short list of helpful extras. Long lists deter strong candidates.
