Nearly everyone who works in AI in Europe was rejected repeatedly before they got there, and almost nobody talks about it. The result is that people in the middle of a search believe they are uniquely unsuccessful, when in fact the numbers are ordinary. Dealing with rejection in an AI job search is partly emotional and partly analytical: the emotional part is about protecting your capacity to keep going, and the analytical part is about reading where you are losing so you can change the right thing.

## What the Numbers Actually Look Like

For a graduate or career changer applying to AI and data roles in Europe, a realistic pattern is something like twenty to forty applications producing five to ten first conversations, three to five processes that reach a technical stage, and one or two offers. For experienced engineers the ratios are better but rarely dramatic.

That means most applications end in rejection or silence, by design. The process is a filter, not a verdict on your worth, and the person reading your application is comparing you to a specific role rather than assessing your potential in general.

It also means a run of five rejections is statistically unremarkable, even though it feels like evidence.

## Dealing With Rejection in an AI Job Search: Read the Stage

Where you are rejected tells you far more than the rejection itself.

**Silence or rejection after applying.** Your application is not clearing the first read. The issue is usually presentation, relevance or targeting — not ability. Look at whether your CV shows outcomes, whether your experience is visible in the first third of the page, and whether you are applying to roles that plausibly match.

**Rejection after a screening call.** Usually mismatch on practicalities — level, salary, location, availability, language — or an unclear explanation of what you do. Ask these questions earlier yourself.

**Rejection after a technical stage.** A genuine skills gap, or a communication gap where you knew the answer and did not show your reasoning. Ask which.

**Rejection after the final round.** Usually a close comparison with another candidate. Frustrating and not a signal to change anything. Ask to be kept in mind; repeat candidates are hired more often than people expect.

**Offer withdrawn or role cancelled.** Business circumstances. Nothing about you.

If you are being rejected at the same stage repeatedly, that is the stage to work on. If rejections are spread evenly, your approach is probably fine and you need more shots.

## Reasons You Were Rejected That Have Nothing to Do With You

It helps to know how often the decision was settled before you were involved.

**The role was filled internally.** Many European employers are required by policy or works council agreement to advertise externally even when an internal candidate is the likely choice.

**A referral arrived first.** A colleague's recommendation reached the hiring manager in week one, and your application arrived in week three.

**The budget changed.** Hiring freezes, reorganisations and cancelled projects end processes at every stage, including after offers.

**The level was wrong.** Teams sometimes advertise a senior role and hire a mid-level candidate, or the reverse, after meeting a few people.

**A previous hire shaped the criteria.** A team burned by a bad hire often over-indexes on whatever went wrong last time, which may be something you cannot influence.

**Notice period or start date.** A team needing someone in four weeks cannot wait three months, however much they liked you.

None of this means you should assume every rejection was external. It means you should not build a theory about your inadequacy on a sample of one, because a large share of decisions are made for reasons you will never be told.

## Asking for Feedback in a Way That Gets an Answer

Most requests for feedback fail because they ask for too much. "Could you tell me why I was not selected?" invites a careful non-answer from anyone worried about legal exposure.

A better message is short and specific:

*"Thank you for letting me know. If you have a moment, I would find it useful to know whether the gap was mainly in the practical exercise or in the depth of my production experience — it would help me focus my preparation. Either way, I enjoyed the conversation about your evaluation setup."*

One question, easy to answer, no argument implied. This gets a reply perhaps a third of the time, which is enough to be worth sending.

Two rules. Never argue with the feedback, even when you think it is wrong; you are asking for a favour and you may apply again. And never ask for feedback more than once from the same employer.

If you reached a final round, add one sentence asking to be considered for future roles. Hiring managers keep informal lists, and people are regularly contacted months later for a position that did not exist at the time.

## Keeping a Record That Actually Helps

A simple tracker turns an emotional experience into a diagnosable one. Columns worth having:

- Company and role
- Date applied, and how you found it
- Stage reached, and the date of each step
- Outcome and any feedback
- Who you spoke to
- One line on what you would do differently

Review it every two weeks, not every day. You are looking for three things: where you lose people, which kinds of roles respond to you, and whether your response rate changes when you change something.

The tracker also protects you from two distortions. It stops a recent rejection from feeling like the whole picture, because you can see the interviews you did get. And it stops the opposite error, drifting through months of applications without noticing that nothing is working.

One further benefit: when a recruiter contacts you about a company you applied to six months ago, you will know what happened and who you spoke to, which prevents the awkward duplicate-submission problem and lets you reference the earlier conversation confidently.

## Fixing the Most Common Problems

If the pattern points at the first read, the usual causes are fixable in an evening.

**The CV buries the evidence.** Projects and outcomes should be visible in the top half of the first page. Education and language certificates rarely need more than three lines.

**Skills are listed rather than demonstrated.** Star ratings and long technology lists carry no information. Replace with two or three projects described in terms of problem, approach, result.

**Targeting is too broad.** Applying to research scientist roles, data engineering roles and analyst roles with the same document signals uncertainty. Pick one direction for a month and measure.

**The applications are generic.** A tailored opening paragraph referencing the actual work changes response rates noticeably, especially at smaller employers.

If the pattern points at technical stages, the fix is slower but equally concrete: identify which questions you handled badly, prepare those specific areas, and practise saying your reasoning out loud, because many rejections at this stage are about invisible thinking rather than absent knowledge.

If the pattern points at final rounds, usually nothing is broken. Keep going; that stage is a coin toss between good candidates.

## Protecting Your Confidence While You Search

The practical advice above only works if you are still applying in month four, so the emotional side is not an indulgence.

**Separate identity from outcome.** A rejection says a company chose someone else for one role. It does not say you are not an engineer, a scientist or a capable person. This sounds obvious and stops being obvious around the twelfth rejection.

**Measure effort, not results.** Results are outside your control and slow. Effort — applications sent, hours prepared, projects advanced — is within your control and visible weekly.

**Keep building something.** A project that progresses regardless of the search gives you evidence of competence that no rejection touches, and it gives you something to talk about in the next interview.

**Tell someone.** Isolation is what makes searching corrosive. A friend, a former classmate, a peer group, a former colleague; anyone who knows what you are doing.

**Keep the rest of your life intact.** Exercise, sleep, people. Candidates who cancel everything to focus on the search interview worse, not better.

**Set an end point for each week.** Two or three focused sessions, then stop. A search that expands to fill all available time produces exhaustion, not offers.

## When to Change Strategy Rather Than Tactics

Sometimes the problem is not the CV or the interview technique; it is the plan.

Consider a strategic change if, after two to three months of consistent effort, you are reaching almost no first conversations despite a rewritten CV and well-targeted applications. Options that work:

**Widen the role type.** Data analyst, analytics engineer, data engineer, ML-adjacent software roles and domain-specific analytical positions all lead to AI work within a year or two, and are far easier to enter than machine learning research roles.

**Widen the geography.** Some regions have thin markets. Opening to a neighbouring city, or to hybrid roles with weekly travel, can transform your options.

**Consider a different entry door.** Internships, traineeships, junior consulting roles and graduate programmes exist precisely for people without experience, and several large European employers run them annually.

**Add one concrete piece of evidence.** An end-to-end project deployed and documented, or a contribution to an open source tool, changes how a CV reads more than another course does.

**Ask whether the target is realistic yet.** Wanting a research role with no publications, or a senior title with two years of experience, may simply need a step in between.

## What Rejection Looks Like From the Other Side

Understanding the hiring manager's position removes much of the sting.

A vacancy for a mid-level data role at a recognisable European employer can attract well over a hundred applications, many from people with no relevant background. The first pass is fast and imperfect, often done by someone with limited technical knowledge against a written brief. Strong candidates are missed regularly, and hiring managers know it.

At interview stage, teams are usually choosing between people who could all do the job. The decision then turns on small things: one candidate's experience matched the immediate project, another had worked with a similar data problem, a third interviewed more confidently on the day. None of these is a judgement of overall ability.

And decisions are frequently constrained by things nobody mentions: a budget band, a team's need for a specific gap to be filled, a manager's preference for someone who can start sooner.

This is not a reason to stop improving your applications. It is a reason to take each individual outcome less personally, and to keep sending the next one.

## Real example

### Twenty-three rejections and the pattern nobody saw

A graduate in applied mathematics in Belgium applied for data science roles for five months. She kept a spreadsheet, which turned out to be the most useful thing she did.

After twenty-three applications she had four screening calls and one technical interview. The pattern was clear once written down: she was not getting past the first read.

She asked a friend working in the field to look at her CV. His reaction was immediate. Her thesis, a substantial piece of statistical modelling on real hospital data, appeared in a single line under education on the second page. The first half of page one listed her school, her language certificates and a skills matrix of twenty technologies rated with stars.

She rewrote it: the thesis as a project with the problem, method, result and tools; the skills matrix deleted; education compressed to three lines.

Her next eleven applications produced six conversations and two offers. Nothing about her ability had changed in three weeks. The rejections had been telling her something specific, and only the record made it legible.

## Key Takeaways

- Most applications end in rejection; the ratios are ordinary even for strong candidates.
- The stage at which you are rejected tells you what to fix.
- Keep a written record — patterns are invisible without one.
- Ask for feedback politely and briefly; some employers give it.
- Separate the analytical response from the emotional one, and give both their place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: applicant receiving no replies at all) Is silence a rejection?
Usually yes, though not always quickly. Follow up once after two weeks, then assume it has moved on and keep going.

### (Scenario: candidate rejected after a final round) Can I apply to the same company again?
Yes, and you should. Reapplying after six months, especially for a different role, works more often than people assume.

### (Scenario: candidate asking for feedback) Will employers tell me why?
Some will, briefly. Ask one specific question rather than requesting general feedback, and accept a non-answer gracefully.

### (Scenario: applicant losing confidence) How do I know when to change approach versus persist?
Change if you see a repeated pattern at one stage. Persist if rejections are varied and you are still reaching interviews.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we give rejection feedback?
For candidates who reached a technical stage, one or two specific sentences cost little and are remembered.
