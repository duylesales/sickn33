Independent work is common in European technology, and in some markets — the Netherlands and the Nordics particularly — it is a mainstream career rather than an interlude. Freelancing in AI can mean higher earnings, control over what you work on and genuine variety. It also means being a business, with the unpaid hours, uncertainty and administration that implies. This guide describes what the work actually is, the economics behind the headline rates, and how to decide whether it suits you.

## Who Hires Independent AI Specialists

**Organisations with a capability gap.** They have data and a problem and no internal expertise, and they want someone to build the first version and often to train their staff.

**Teams needing capacity.** An existing data team with more work than people, hiring for a defined period or project.

**Companies between hires.** A vacancy open for months and a project that cannot wait.

**Specialist needs.** A specific skill required briefly: a retrieval system, a forecasting rebuild, an evaluation framework, an audit of an existing model.

**Agencies and consultancies.** Subcontracting independents onto client work, which is the most common route to a first engagement.

**Startups.** Pre-hire technical work, or fractional senior involvement.

Note what is largely absent: research roles and long-horizon platform work, which organisations generally keep in-house.

## What the Work Actually Involves

Independent engagements skew toward well-defined, deliverable work. A typical contract is two to six months, often part-time, to build something specific or to assess something existing.

The composition differs from employment. You will spend more time on scoping, expectation setting and handover, and less on long-term system ownership. You will frequently work in unfamiliar organisations with unfamiliar data, which rewards people who become useful quickly. And you will be asked to justify your decisions more explicitly, because you are visibly expensive.

The work most in demand across Europe has been unglamorous: getting data into a usable state, building a first production system where none existed, evaluating whether a proposed approach is feasible, and rescuing projects that stalled.

## Freelancing in AI: The Economics Behind the Rate

Headline rates look impressive next to salaries and are not comparable. Working through the arithmetic honestly is the most important thing a prospective freelancer can do.

From a day rate, subtract: unbilled days between engagements, which for most independents is a meaningful share of the year; holiday and sick leave, which nobody pays you for; pension, which you fund entirely; insurance; accounting and administration; equipment and software; training and conferences; and the unbilled hours spent finding the next engagement.

A widely used rule of thumb is that an independent needs substantially more than the equivalent salary-plus-employer-costs figure to be in the same position, and the exact multiple depends on how many billable days you achieve.

The honest test is not whether the rate is higher. It is whether you will bill enough days at it.

## Legal Status and Tax Across Europe

Independent work is regulated differently in every European country, and the differences are consequential enough that general advice is of limited use. What follows is orientation, not guidance; consult an accountant in your own country.

Most countries distinguish genuine self-employment from arrangements that are employment in substance. Authorities look at factors such as whether you control how the work is done, whether you bear commercial risk, how many clients you have, whether you use your own equipment, and whether you could send a substitute. Arrangements that resemble employment — a single client, fixed hours, integrated into a team, working under direction — attract scrutiny in several member states, and the consequences of reclassification can fall on both parties.

The Netherlands has a particularly well-known framework around this question, and enforcement practice has varied over time; anyone working there should check the current position rather than relying on what was true a few years ago.

Beyond classification, the practical matters are: registering a business, value added tax registration and invoicing, income tax and social contributions, pension arrangements you must fund yourself, and cross-border rules if you work for clients in other countries.

Budget for an accountant. The cost is small relative to the cost of getting this wrong.

## Contracts and What to Read Carefully

Independent work makes you responsible for terms an employer would normally handle, and a few clauses matter disproportionately in AI engagements.

**Intellectual property.** Who owns what you produce. Most clients expect to own deliverables; you may want to retain rights to general tools and methods you bring with you. Say so explicitly rather than assuming.

**Liability.** Look for unlimited liability clauses and indemnities. Negotiate a cap, typically related to the contract value, and check what your insurance actually covers.

**Data protection.** If you process personal data for a client you are likely a processor under GDPR, which requires a written agreement covering security, sub-processing, breach notification and deletion. Clients often supply this; read it.

**Confidentiality.** Standard and reasonable. Check the duration and whether it prevents you describing the engagement in general terms for future work.

**Payment terms.** Thirty days is common; sixty is not unusual with large organisations and affects your cash flow materially. Negotiate, and consider partial payment on milestones.

**Termination.** How much notice either side must give. A contract terminable on one week's notice is much riskier than the day rate suggests.

**Scope.** The single most useful thing you can write is a clear statement of what is included and what is not.

## Finding Work Reliably

Independents who thrive treat finding work as an ongoing activity rather than an emergency response.

**Former colleagues and employers.** The dominant source of first and repeat engagements. People who have seen you work do not need convincing. Keeping in touch with a few dozen former colleagues is the single highest-return activity available.

**Subcontracting.** Agencies, consultancies and larger independents subcontract regularly. The rate is lower and the work is more reliable, which makes it a sensible base load.

**Referrals from clients.** Deliver well, then ask. Most independents are embarrassed to ask and should not be.

**Specialist intermediaries.** Brokers and platforms exist in most European markets, with varying quality and margins.

**Visibility.** A clear profile describing what you do, occasional writing about your work, and presence in professional communities. This generates inbound enquiries slowly and steadily.

**A defined specialism.** "Machine learning" is too broad to be memorable. "Forecasting for retail and logistics" or "retrieval systems in regulated environments" is what people remember when a relevant problem appears.

The discipline that matters most is doing this while busy. Independents who only look for work when an engagement ends spend a lot of unbilled time.

## Scoping and Delivering Engagements Well

The commercial skill that distinguishes successful independents is scoping, and it is learnable.

**Insist on a discovery phase.** A short, paid assessment — one or two weeks — before committing to a delivery scope. This protects both sides: you find out whether the data exists, and the client gets a realistic plan rather than an optimistic one.

**Write down what success means.** Specific, measurable, agreed in advance and in the contract. In AI work this must include what happens if the result is that the approach does not work, which is a genuine possible outcome and must not be treated as failure to deliver.

**Manage scope actively.** Requests will expand. Handle it by agreeing a change explicitly, not by absorbing it silently and resenting it.

**Communicate more than feels necessary.** A weekly written update prevents the most common source of dispute, which is a client who is unsure what is happening.

**Plan the handover from day one.** Documentation, a walkthrough, a maintenance note and, where possible, training a member of their team. Clients remember whether the work survived your departure, and that determines referrals.

**Deliver something early.** Value in week three buys patience in week ten.

## The Parts People Underestimate

Independent work has costs that are invisible from employment.

**Isolation.** No colleagues, no team, nobody to ask when you are stuck. Many independents find this the hardest part, and the successful ones deliberately build peer networks, shared workspaces or regular contact with other freelancers.

**No development.** Nobody pays for your learning, reviews your code or mentors you. Your skills can stagnate while you bill, and you must invest deliberately.

**Administration.** Invoicing, chasing payment, bookkeeping, insurance, contracts, tax returns. Plan for a day a month at least.

**Uncertainty.** Pipelines evaporate, projects are cancelled, clients reorganise. This is manageable with a buffer and unpleasant without one.

**Saying no.** The pressure to accept unsuitable work when the pipeline is thin is real, and bad engagements cost more than empty weeks.

**No collective protections.** Sick pay, parental provisions, redundancy protection and pension contributions are yours to arrange. In much of Europe these are substantial employment benefits that independents replace at their own expense.

None of this is a reason not to freelance. It is a reason to enter it deliberately rather than as an escape from a job you dislike.

## Who It Suits, and Who It Does Not

After a few years, patterns are clear about who does well independently.

It suits people who enjoy entering unfamiliar organisations and becoming useful quickly; who are comfortable with commercial conversations; who like variety more than depth; who can tolerate income that varies by quarter; and who are disciplined about administration and pipeline.

It suits less well people who want to build one system over years; who are early in their careers and need development; who find uncertainty genuinely stressful; who dislike selling; or whose circumstances require predictable income — a mortgage application, for instance, is treated differently for independents in most European countries.

A useful middle path exists. Many experienced people alternate: a few years employed, a few years independent, then employed again. Each direction is possible, and employers generally read freelance experience positively provided it shows delivered work rather than a series of short unexplained engagements.

Another is fractional work: a long-term part-time arrangement with one or two clients, which gives continuity and relationship depth while retaining independence. This has become common for senior people in European technology markets and combines several of the advantages of both models.

## Making the Transition Safely

If you decide to go independent, a few practical steps materially reduce the risk.

**Secure the first engagement before you resign.** Starting with a signed contract changes the experience entirely, and it is often available from your own network or even, in some cases, your current employer.

**Build a buffer.** Three to six months of personal expenses. This is what lets you decline bad work, which is the main determinant of whether the first year goes well.

**Do the arithmetic before, not after.** Required billable days, target rate, fixed costs, pension contribution, tax provision. If the numbers only work at ninety per cent utilisation, the plan is fragile.

**Set up the administration properly from the start.** Business registration, separate bank account, accountant, insurance, invoice template, contract template.

**Check your current contract.** Non-competition and non-solicitation clauses may restrict working for your employer's clients, and this catches people out regularly.

**Tell your network.** Most first engagements come from people who already know you, and they cannot refer you if they do not know you are available.

## Real example

### The freelancer who counted her days

A data scientist in the Netherlands left employment to work independently, encouraged by a day rate that looked like a substantial increase.

Her first year was good: two long engagements, high utilisation, strong earnings. Her second was not. One client paused a project after a reorganisation, another delayed a start by three months, and she billed considerably fewer days.

At the end of the second year she did the calculation properly across both years, including pension contributions she had underfunded and the unbilled weeks she had not counted. Averaged over twenty-four months, her position was similar to her previous salary, with more variance and more administration.

She continued freelancing, but changed how she worked: she kept three months of expenses as a buffer, accepted one longer engagement per year as a base, raised her rate, and spent one day a fortnight on finding work regardless of how busy she was.

Her conclusion was that freelancing was a good career and a bad way to avoid thinking about money.

## Key Takeaways

- Independent AI work skews toward defined, deliverable projects rather than long-term ownership.
- The headline rate is not comparable to a salary; billable days are what matter.
- Most first engagements come through former colleagues and subcontracting.
- Legal classification and tax treatment differ by country and matter.
- Freelancing suits people who like variety and can tolerate uncertainty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: engineer considering the move) How much experience do I need?
Realistically five or more years. Clients pay independent rates for judgement and speed, and juniors are better served by employment where they are developed.

### (Scenario: candidate asking about work) Where do first engagements come from?
Former colleagues and employers, subcontracting through agencies and consultancies, and referrals. Cold marketing rarely produces the first one.

### (Scenario: freelancer worried about legal status) Am I really self-employed?
That depends on how the work is structured, and several European countries scrutinise arrangements that resemble employment. Take national advice; the rules and enforcement differ.

### (Scenario: freelancer considering insurance) Do I need professional liability cover?
Many clients require it, and it is sensible regardless. Check what your contracts make you liable for.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we hire freelancers for AI work?
Useful for defined projects, capacity and specialist skills. Less suitable where the value is long-term ownership and accumulated domain knowledge.
