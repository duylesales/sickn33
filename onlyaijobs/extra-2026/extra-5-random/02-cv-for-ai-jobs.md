A CV for AI jobs has one job: to convince a busy hiring manager, in well under a minute, that talking to you is worth an hour of their time. Most CVs from data and machine learning professionals fail not because the person is unqualified but because the document hides the evidence. It lists tools instead of outcomes, describes team achievements without saying what the candidate did, and buries the one project that would have made the case. This guide explains how hiring managers actually read CVs for AI roles, how to structure yours, how to describe models and impact honestly, and what to do about gaps, career changes and language barriers.

## How Your CV Is Actually Read

In most European companies, a CV passes through three quick filters.

**The recruiter scan (20–40 seconds).** Someone checks whether you plausibly match the role: right kind of experience, right seniority, work authorisation, location or willingness to relocate, and obvious red flags such as unexplained gaps or a mismatch with the vacancy.

**The hiring manager scan (1–3 minutes).** A technical person looks for evidence: what you built, at what scale, with what responsibility, and whether the claims feel credible. They read your most recent role first and often skip everything older than five to seven years.

**The interview preparation read (later).** If you are invited, someone reads more carefully to prepare questions. This is where inconsistencies get noticed.

Write for all three. The first two rounds are won on structure and specificity; the third is won on honesty.

## Structure That Works for a CV for AI Jobs

For most AI roles in Europe, a two-page CV in reverse chronological order works best. One page is fine for graduates; three pages are acceptable only for senior researchers with publication lists, and even then the first page must stand alone.

A structure that consistently performs well:

1. **Name, role title you are targeting, location, contact details, links** (GitHub, personal site, Google Scholar if relevant).
2. **Short summary (2–3 lines)**: who you are professionally, your strongest domain, what you are looking for. No adjectives like "passionate" or "results-driven".
3. **Experience**: for each role, one line of context about the team and product, then three to five bullets of what you did and what changed.
4. **Selected projects** (optional but powerful, especially for career changers and graduates).
5. **Skills**: grouped and honest, not an alphabetical dump.
6. **Education**, and publications if relevant.
7. **Languages** and anything legally relevant such as work authorisation.

## Writing Bullets That Carry Evidence

The single biggest improvement most candidates can make is to rewrite bullets so that each one answers three questions: what was the problem, what did you do, and what changed.

Weak: *"Worked on machine learning models using Python, scikit-learn and AWS."*

Better: *"Built and deployed a churn model for a subscription business (2 million customers); replaced a rules-based system, reduced false positives substantially and handed over a retraining pipeline the analytics team now runs without me."*

The second version says what the model was for, what it replaced, what improved and what you left behind. Note also what it does not do: it does not invent a precise percentage you cannot defend. If you know real numbers and are allowed to share them, use them. If not, describe direction and scale honestly — experienced interviewers respect that far more than suspiciously round figures.

## Describing Models Without Overclaiming

AI hiring managers are unusually alert to inflated claims, because they have seen them often. A few habits protect your credibility:

- Say what part of the work was yours. "Led", "built", "contributed to" and "supported" mean different things; use them accurately.
- Name the baseline. A model is only good compared with something: a heuristic, an old model, a human process.
- Mention evaluation, not just training. Which metric, on what data, and how you knew it generalised.
- Say whether it reached production, and if not, why not. "Prototype that was not deployed because priorities changed" is an honest, common and perfectly acceptable statement.
- Avoid listing every algorithm you have ever imported. Nobody is impressed by a list; they are impressed by a decision you can explain.

## Skills Sections That Help Rather Than Hurt

Group skills into a few meaningful categories — for example languages and frameworks, data and infrastructure, ML areas, domain knowledge — and include only things you could discuss for ten minutes. Rating yourself with stars or percentages rarely helps and often invites scepticism. If a vacancy requires a specific tool that you know, make sure the word appears somewhere in your experience section, not only in the skills list, so that both humans and search filters find it in context.

## Career Changes, Gaps and Non-Linear Paths

European hiring is generally more tolerant of non-linear paths than candidates fear, provided the CV explains them in one line.

- **Career change into AI**: lead with a projects section and any production experience, and make the transferable part explicit (statistics from a research background, engineering rigour from software, domain expertise from your previous industry).
- **Career break**: state it plainly with dates — parental leave, illness, caring responsibilities, study, relocation — and move on. Ambiguity attracts more questions than the break itself.
- **Short stints**: give a one-line reason (project ended, company restructured, fixed-term contract) rather than leaving the reader to speculate.
- **Academic to industry**: translate. "Supervised two master's students" becomes evidence of mentoring; "wrote analysis code used by the group" becomes software delivery.

## Tailoring Without Rewriting Everything

Tailoring a CV for AI jobs does not mean producing a new document for every application. It means keeping one strong master CV and adjusting three things per application:

- **The summary line**: match the role's focus, for example forecasting, computer vision, platform work or analytics.
- **The order of bullets** within your most recent roles, so the most relevant evidence appears first.
- **The vocabulary**: if the vacancy says "time series" and you wrote "sequential data", use their words where they are accurate.

That takes ten minutes per application and has far more effect than a generic document sent everywhere. If you find yourself unable to tailor the CV honestly for a particular vacancy, that is useful information: the role probably is not a good fit.

## Applicant Tracking Systems: What Actually Matters

Candidates worry a great deal about automated filtering. In European companies, most systems used for AI roles are storage and workflow tools rather than aggressive rankers; a human usually reads what arrives. Still, a few habits help:

- Use a simple layout: one column, standard headings, no text inside images.
- Submit a PDF unless another format is requested.
- Use the terms that appear in the vacancy, in context.
- Avoid tables and multi-column designs that scramble when parsed.

Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, employers will have to give candidates pay information before interviews and may not ask about pay history, which should make early conversations more straightforward. Where AI is used in recruitment decisions, the EU AI Act treats certain employment uses as high-risk, and GDPR gives you rights over your personal data — including the right to ask what is stored about your application.

## Country Differences Worth Knowing

Europe is not one hiring culture, and small adjustments help:

- **Netherlands, Nordics, Ireland**: concise, factual CVs; no photo; two pages; cover letters increasingly optional but valued when short and specific.
- **Germany and Austria**: more formal; structured CV; some employers still expect certificates and references attached, though photos are no longer recommended.
- **France**: one to two pages, often with a clear "poste recherché"; motivation letters still common.
- **Spain, Italy, Portugal, Greece**: CVs may be longer; language skills matter; cover letters common.
- **Central and Eastern Europe**: concise CVs in English are standard in tech companies; include a note on work authorisation if relevant.

If you apply across borders, write in the language of the vacancy. An English CV is fine for English-language postings, which cover most AI roles in international teams.

## Publications, Preprints and Open Source

For research-oriented roles, list publications with venue and year, and highlight the two or three most relevant rather than everything. For industry roles, a long publication list can crowd out more useful evidence; summarise instead ("six peer-reviewed papers in computer vision; selected: …").

Open-source contributions are strong evidence when they are real. Link the specific repository or pull requests rather than a bare profile, and say what you contributed. A maintained small library says more than occasional commits to a famous project.

## Confidentiality and What You Can Say

Many data professionals work with confidential or personal data and worry about what they may describe. Safe practice is to describe the shape of the problem rather than the specifics: "a European retailer with several hundred stores" instead of the client name; "a fraud detection system for card payments" instead of the internal project name; relative improvements instead of absolute figures if the numbers are sensitive. Never include customer data, screenshots of internal dashboards or confidential documents in a portfolio. If in doubt, ask your employer what you may share; most are reasonable about generic descriptions.

## The Cover Letter Question

In much of Europe, a short cover letter is still expected, or at least read when provided. Three short paragraphs are enough: why this role and organisation specifically, the single most relevant piece of evidence from your experience, and a practical note (availability, location, work authorisation). Do not restate your CV. If you cannot write a specific first paragraph about the employer, that is a sign you are applying too broadly.

## A Checklist Before You Send

- Does the first half of page one show what kind of AI professional you are?
- Does every bullet name a problem, an action and an outcome?
- Is your own contribution distinguishable from your team's?
- Have you removed adjectives that carry no information?
- Are all dates present and gaps explained in one line?
- Do the tools in your skills list appear somewhere in context?
- Is the file named clearly, for example `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`?
- Would a technical reader be able to ask you three good questions from it?

If the last answer is no, your CV is still describing responsibilities rather than work.

## Keeping It Current

Update your CV every quarter while the details are fresh, even when you are not job searching. Keep a private running log of what you built, what changed and any numbers you are allowed to keep. When an interesting vacancy appears, you will be able to apply the same week rather than spending a weekend reconstructing the last two years from memory.

## Real example

### Two CVs for the same role

Two candidates applied for the same machine learning engineer vacancy at a mid-sized European company. Both had roughly four years of experience.

The first CV listed eleven technologies in a skills matrix, described responsibilities in passive language — "involved in developing models" — and mentioned a master's thesis only in the education section.

The second CV opened with two lines: an ML engineer with a background in physics, focused on forecasting and time series in industrial settings, looking for a production-oriented role. Each job had one context line and three bullets naming the problem, the approach and the outcome, including one honest note that a project had been cancelled after a pilot. A short projects section described a deployed side project with monitoring.

Both candidates were technically similar. Only the second was invited, because the hiring manager could see, in ninety seconds, exactly what she had done and what she would bring. In the interview she was asked about the cancelled project — and her candid account of what went wrong became one of the strongest parts of the conversation.

## Key Takeaways

- A CV for AI jobs must show evidence quickly: problem, action, outcome, and what part was yours.
- Two pages, reverse chronological, with a short factual summary works for most European roles.
- Describe evaluation and production status, not just algorithms.
- Explain gaps and career changes in one line rather than leaving them ambiguous.
- Honest, defensible claims beat impressive but vague ones.

Two pages for most professionals; one page for graduates; longer only for researchers with publication lists, and the first page must still stand alone.

### (Scenario: candidate without production experience) What if none of my models reached production?
Say so honestly and describe what you built, how you evaluated it and what you learned. Many teams hire for judgement, not only deployment history.

### (Scenario: career changer) Should I include a projects section?
Yes. For career changers and graduates, two or three well-documented projects often carry more weight than the experience section.
