The first leadership step in a technical career is the one most people take without preparation and several regret for a year before it starts working. Becoming a tech lead in a data team means your output is no longer what you build; it is what the team is able to build. That is a genuine change in what you are paid for, and the skills that made you a strong individual contributor only partly transfer.

## What Actually Changes

**Your time fragments.** Long blocks of concentration become rare. People who define a good day as six hours of uninterrupted work will find the first months frustrating.

**Your influence is indirect.** You achieve things by helping other people do them, which is slower and less satisfying in the short term and far more effective over a year.

**Decisions become yours.** Which approach, which trade-off, what to stop. Nobody will tell you the answer, and some of your decisions will be wrong in ways that are visible.

**You represent the team.** Upward, outward and to stakeholders who do not understand the work. What you say is treated as the team's position.

**You see the whole board.** Budgets, politics, other teams' plans, the reason a project was really cancelled. This is often the most interesting part.

Note what does not change in most European organisations: a tech lead usually remains technical, still writes code, and typically does not carry formal people management responsibility, which is a separate track.

## Becoming a Tech Lead in a Data Team: The Skills That Matter

**Judgement about what to build.** Choosing the two things worth doing out of nine requests, and defending the choice. This is the core of the role.

**Technical direction.** Setting the approach, the standards and the architecture, and keeping them coherent as several people work in parallel.

**Unblocking.** Noticing when someone has been stuck for two days and intervening without taking the work away.

**Writing.** Proposals, decision records, updates. Leads who write clearly have disproportionate influence because their reasoning travels without them.

**Saying no with a reason.** To stakeholders, to your own manager, and to your own preferences about what would be interesting to build.

**Reviewing without rewriting.** Letting work be done differently from how you would do it, provided it is correct.

## Lead, Manager or Staff Engineer?

European organisations use these titles inconsistently, and understanding the distinctions saves you from accepting a job you did not intend to take.

**Tech lead.** Technical direction and delivery for a team, usually without formal people responsibility. Still hands-on. The most common first step.

**Engineering or data science manager.** Formal responsibility for people: hiring, performance, development, pay conversations and, occasionally, exits. Often little or no coding. A genuinely different job requiring different skills, and in many European countries carrying legal responsibilities around employment that should not be taken lightly.

**Staff or principal engineer.** Senior individual contributor influencing across teams through architecture, standards and difficult technical problems. No direct reports, high leverage, and in many organisations paid equivalently to management.

**Head of data or analytics.** Departmental responsibility including strategy, budget and stakeholder relationships across the organisation.

Ask precisely which of these is being offered, whether people report to you formally, and what proportion of time is expected on technical work. A title alone tells you almost nothing, and people regularly accept a management role believing it to be a technical one, or the reverse.

If you are unsure which you want, a tech lead role is the most reversible way to find out.

## The First Ninety Days as a Lead

A deliberate start prevents most of the common early problems.

**Ask each person what they want.** Individually, in the first fortnight. What they enjoy, what they want to be doing in two years, what frustrates them about how the team works. You will learn more in five conversations than in three months of observation.

**Establish how decisions get made.** Which decisions are yours, which are the team's, which need your manager. Say it out loud, because ambiguity here produces either paralysis or resentment.

**Find out what the team is judged on.** By your manager, by stakeholders, by the wider organisation. Frequently the team's own view differs from the organisation's, and reconciling that is your job.

**Write down the technical direction.** One page: what we build, what standards we hold, what we have decided not to do. It will be wrong in places and it gives everyone something to argue with, which is better than silent divergence.

**Reduce the number of parallel projects.** Almost every new lead inherits too many. Finishing two things creates more goodwill than progressing six.

**Keep one small piece of hands-on work.** Enough to stay credible and to feel the friction your team experiences, not enough to become a bottleneck.

## Mistakes That Cost the First Year

Recurring patterns among new technical leads, all recoverable if noticed.

**Becoming a bottleneck.** Every decision routed through you, every review waiting on you. The fix is delegating decisions, not just tasks: name who owns each area and let them decide.

**Rewriting other people's work.** Correct but demoralising, and it teaches the team that their work will be replaced.

**Protecting the team from everything.** Some shielding is your job; total insulation leaves people unable to understand why priorities change.

**Avoiding difficult conversations.** The engineer whose work is consistently late, the stakeholder who keeps expanding scope. These get harder, never easier, and the team notices what you tolerate.

**Trying to keep your previous output.** Working evenings to remain the top contributor while also leading. This ends badly and predictably.

**Over-correcting into meetings.** Leadership is not attending everything. Decline what you can and protect the team's time as well as your own.

**Not asking for help.** Other leads have solved these problems. Find one inside or outside your organisation and talk to them monthly; it is the cheapest support available and almost nobody arranges it.

## Managing Upward and Outward

A significant share of a lead's value is created outside the team, and it is the part individual contributors least anticipate.

**With your manager.** Establish what they need to know and how often, then be reliable about it. Bring problems early with a proposed response. Ask explicitly what success looks like for the team this year, because you cannot prioritise without it.

**With stakeholders.** Understand what they are measured on. Requests that appear unreasonable usually make sense once you know the pressure behind them, and knowing it lets you propose something that serves both.

**With dependent teams.** Data platform, infrastructure, security, legal. Build these relationships before you need something urgently. Twenty minutes of coffee with the platform lead is worth more than three escalations.

**In writing.** A short monthly summary of what the team delivered, in the organisation's language rather than technical terms, changes how the team is perceived over a year. Most data teams are undervalued simply because nobody outside knows what they do.

**Defending time.** Foundations, documentation and evaluation are invisible to stakeholders and essential to delivery. Making the case for them, repeatedly, is part of the role.

## Growing the People in Your Team

Even without formal management responsibility, a lead shapes careers, and doing it deliberately is among the most satisfying parts of the job.

**Match work to growth.** Assign the project that stretches someone rather than the one they will do most efficiently. Accept that this is slower in the short term.

**Give feedback close to the event.** Small, specific and timely beats a comprehensive assessment months later. This applies to praise as well.

**Make their work visible.** Let people present their own work, name them in updates, put them in front of stakeholders. Leads who take credit for the team's output lose the team.

**Create room to fail safely.** Delegating a decision means accepting a different choice than you would have made, and sometimes a worse outcome. That is the price of people learning to decide.

**Have the career conversation.** Twice a year, ask where they want to be and what would help. Then do one concrete thing about it.

**Notice when someone is struggling.** Withdrawal, missed commitments and silence in discussions are signals. Ask privately and early; the reason is frequently outside work and usually manageable if raised.

## Getting the Role in the First Place

Leadership roles are rarely awarded for technical excellence alone. They are given to people who are already behaving as leads.

Practical preparation, none of which requires permission:

**Own a cross-cutting concern.** Evaluation standards, the path to production, the team's data quality, the onboarding process. Something with no clear owner that everyone benefits from.

**Mentor someone.** Formally or informally. Ability to develop others is the clearest evidence available.

**Write the proposals.** Be the person whose documents people read and refer back to.

**Run the design discussions.** Facilitating a group toward a decision is a visible leadership skill.

**Represent the team occasionally.** Volunteer for the stakeholder meeting nobody wants to attend.

**Ask your manager directly** what would need to be true for you to be considered, and then do those things. This conversation is far more effective than hoping to be noticed, and most managers respond well to it.

If no lead role exists internally, the same evidence supports an external application, where you would typically enter a slightly smaller team. Be prepared to explain how you have led without the title.

## Whether You Should Want It

Not everyone should, and the field would be healthier if that were said more often.

The role suits people who find other people's development satisfying, who enjoy the puzzle of an organisation as much as a technical one, who can tolerate ambiguity and unfinished business, and who do not need to be the one who solved the problem.

It suits less well people whose satisfaction comes primarily from building, who find interruption genuinely draining, or who dislike conflict — because a meaningful part of the job is holding a position when someone senior disagrees.

There is no career ladder in which leadership is the only way up. Senior individual contributor tracks exist in most serious European technology employers and reach comparable seniority and pay. If your organisation has no such track, that is worth knowing before you conclude that leadership is your only option.

The honest test is whether you want the work of the role, not whether you want its status. Ask a lead you respect what their last week actually contained, and see whether it appeals.

## Real example

### The lead who kept the hardest task

A senior machine learning engineer at a European software company was made technical lead of a team of five. For the first six months he kept the most difficult component for himself, because he could do it faster and it was the part he enjoyed.

The team's throughput did not increase. Two engineers said in their reviews that they were not learning. When he took two weeks of leave, work on the core component stopped entirely.

His manager put it plainly: he was still doing the job he had been promoted out of.

He changed approach. He handed the component to the engineer who most wanted it, paired for two weeks, then reviewed rather than wrote. He kept one smaller piece for himself to stay hands-on, and spent the reclaimed time on the things nobody had been doing: a clear technical direction, better relationships with the teams they depended on, and a written record of decisions.

A year later the team delivered more, two engineers had grown visibly, and he described the change as giving away the part of the job he liked most in order to do the job he had.

## Key Takeaways

- Your output becomes what the team can build, not what you build.
- Judgement about what to build is the central skill of the role.
- Keeping the interesting work for yourself is the most common first-year mistake.
- Writing clearly multiplies your influence more than any technical decision.
- Staying partly hands-on keeps credibility; staying fully hands-on prevents the job being done.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: engineer considering the step) Will I stop coding?
Not entirely, but substantially less. Expect twenty to forty per cent of your time, and choose work that can be interrupted.

### (Scenario: engineer worried about reversibility) Can I go back to individual contribution?
Yes, and many do. In most European companies this is treated as a normal choice rather than a demotion, though check how your employer actually behaves.

### (Scenario: candidate asking about pay) Does it pay more?
Usually somewhat, and less than people expect. Senior individual contributor tracks often reach comparable levels.

### (Scenario: engineer without a lead role available) How do I prepare?
Do parts of the job now: technical direction for one area, mentoring a junior, writing the proposals, running the design discussions.

### (Scenario: employer) How do we support new leads?
Explicit expectations, a mentor who has done it, protected time for the non-coding parts, and an early conversation about what they should stop doing.
