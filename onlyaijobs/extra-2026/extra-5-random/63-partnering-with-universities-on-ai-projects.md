Many European organisations want to know whether AI could help them and cannot justify hiring a specialist to find out. Universities, meanwhile, need real problems for students to work on and are rarely short of capable people. Partnering with universities on AI projects is one of the most underused routes available to regional employers, and when it is done well it produces both an answer and, frequently, a future employee.

## Partnering With Universities on AI Projects: The Formats That Exist

**Graduation or thesis projects.** A student spends four to six months on a defined problem, usually supervised jointly by an academic and someone in your organisation. The most substantial format and the one that produces real deliverables.

**Internships and work placements.** Shorter, more operational, often part of a degree requirement. Good for well-defined work and for assessing someone as a potential hire.

**Course projects.** A team of students works on your problem for part of a semester as coursework. Low commitment, variable depth, useful for exploration.

**Applied research projects.** A structured collaboration with a research group over one to three years, often with public co-funding. Appropriate for genuinely difficult problems.

**Traineeships and dual programmes.** Where students alternate study and employment, common in several European countries and an excellent recruitment channel.

**Consortium projects.** Multi-partner programmes funded regionally, nationally or through EU instruments, where several companies and institutions share a research agenda.

## What Makes a Good Student Project

The most common failure is a problem that is either too vague or impossible with the data available. A workable brief has five properties.

**A real question with a decision attached.** "Can we predict which machines will need maintenance, so that planning can be adjusted?" rather than "explore AI for maintenance".

**Data that already exists and can be shared.** Check this before writing the brief. Many projects lose two of their five months waiting for access.

**A scope achievable by one person in months, not years.** Students are capable and inexperienced; ambition should sit in the depth of analysis, not in the breadth of scope.

**A named supervisor inside the organisation.** Someone who meets the student weekly, answers questions and knows the domain. Without this, projects drift.

**A defined deliverable.** A report, a working prototype, an evaluation with a recommendation. Say what you expect at the start.

## Research Universities and Universities of Applied Sciences

Europe's higher education systems generally distinguish between research-oriented universities and institutions focused on professional practice, and the distinction matters for what you will get.

**Research universities** produce students trained in method and theory, often with stronger mathematical foundations. Their graduation projects are expected to contain an element of novelty, which suits genuinely open problems. Academic supervisors are interested in research questions, so a brief framed only as "build this for us" may be declined.

**Universities of applied sciences** — hogescholen in the Netherlands, Fachhochschulen in German-speaking countries, and their equivalents elsewhere — train students for professional practice, with internships built into the curriculum. Their projects are typically more implementation-oriented and more directly useful to a company with a practical problem.

For most organisations investigating whether something is feasible, applied science institutions are the better first contact: the students are oriented toward delivery, the timelines are predictable, and the institutions are accustomed to working with regional employers.

For a hard problem where the answer is genuinely unknown — an unusual sensing challenge, a modelling question with no standard approach — a research group is the right partner, and the collaboration will be slower and deeper.

## Writing the Brief

A well-written one-page brief is what determines whether you attract a good student and whether the project succeeds.

Include: the organisation and what it does, in two sentences a student will understand; the business problem in plain language; the decision that would change if the question were answered; what data exists, in what form, over what period, with approximate volumes; what has already been tried; the expected deliverable; the practical arrangements — location, days on site, supervision, allowance; and what the student will learn.

Avoid: a wish list of several problems, technology requirements copied from a job advertisement, and language implying you already know the answer.

Be honest about the data. "We have five years of production records in a database, plus quality inspection results in spreadsheets that are not linked to them" is far more attractive to a good student than a vague claim of a rich dataset, because it describes a real and interesting problem rather than setting up a disappointment.

Send the brief to a department coordinator or a lecturer in the relevant subject rather than to a general address. Many institutions have a dedicated liaison office for exactly this, and using it saves weeks.

## Data Access, Privacy and Intellectual Property

These three topics cause most of the friction, and all three are manageable if addressed before the student arrives.

**Data access.** Arrange it first. Decide what the student may see, in what environment, and who extracts it. An extract prepared in week one is worth more than a promise of access to production systems that requires three approvals.

**Personal data.** If the data concerns people — customers, patients, employees — GDPR applies to the student's processing as it does to yours. Options include anonymisation, pseudonymisation, working with a subset, or working inside your environment under your controls. Involve whoever handles data protection in your organisation early; this is a short conversation if held at the right time and a project-ending one if held late.

**Intellectual property.** Rules differ by country and institution, and universities often have standard agreements. Typical arrangements give the company rights to use the results while the student retains the right to publish their thesis, with confidential material handled separately. Read what the institution proposes rather than sending your own contract first; their template is usually reasonable and faster to agree.

**Publication and confidentiality.** Theses are generally public documents. Agree in advance what may be published, what belongs in a confidential annex, and whether an embargo period applies.

## Supervising Well

The internal supervisor makes or breaks the project, and the role is smaller than people fear: roughly an hour a week plus availability for questions.

**Meet weekly, at a fixed time.** Students will not chase you, and a fortnight of being stuck is a fortnight lost.

**Explain the domain generously.** How the process works, why a field means what it means, what happened in 2024 that makes the data look odd. This is the knowledge that determines whether the analysis is sensible, and only you have it.

**Introduce them to people.** An operator, a planner, a customer service colleague. Half an hour with each transforms the student's understanding.

**Review the direction, not the code.** You are not expected to assess the technical work; the academic supervisor does that. Your job is to say whether the question being answered is still the right one.

**Be honest about constraints.** If a proposed approach could never be implemented for regulatory or practical reasons, say so early.

**Give feedback on communication.** Students are rarely taught to present to a business audience, and doing so is one of the most valuable things they take away.

Treat the student as a junior colleague rather than as a visitor. It produces better work and it is the reason they will consider joining you.

## Converting Projects Into Hires

The recruitment value of these collaborations is substantial and frequently wasted through inattention.

You have spent months observing someone work on a real problem in your organisation, which is far better evidence than any interview process produces. They have learned your data, your domain and your people, which is the expensive part of onboarding already completed.

To convert this: say early that you would be interested if the project goes well, so the possibility is in their mind. Treat them properly during the project, because students discuss their experiences with each other and with their departments. Make a concrete offer before they graduate, since good students receive other offers in their final months. And if you cannot offer a role, say so honestly and help them find one, which preserves the relationship with the institution.

Over several years, a regional employer running two or three student projects annually builds a genuine pipeline: a reputation in the department, a stream of candidates who have already worked with you, and lecturers who recommend you.

That compounding effect is why the organisations that do this consistently find hiring easier than comparable employers who advertise vacancies and wait.

## Funding and Formal Research Collaboration

For problems larger than a student project, structured funding instruments exist across Europe, and regional employers often do not know they are eligible.

Regional and national innovation agencies in most European countries offer schemes that co-fund collaboration between companies and research institutions, frequently with simplified processes for smaller businesses. Amounts, eligibility and rules vary considerably by country and change over time, so check current conditions with your regional development agency rather than relying on second-hand information.

At European level, framework programmes fund larger consortium research, typically involving multiple countries and partners. These are substantial undertakings with long preparation times, appropriate for organisations with a strategic research interest rather than a specific operational question.

Practical advice for a first approach: talk to the university's business liaison or valorisation office. They know which instruments apply, they have written applications before, and they are motivated to help because these collaborations are part of their mandate.

Be realistic about timescales. Funded research projects take months to arrange and years to run. If you need an answer this year, a student project or a short paid engagement is the better route, and the funded collaboration can follow if the question proves strategically important.

## What to Expect, Honestly

Managing expectations prevents most disappointment.

A student project produces an investigation, not a production system. Even an excellent outcome will be a prototype and a report, and turning it into something operational is separate work that you will need to resource.

Roughly a third of projects produce something directly usable, a third produce a clear negative answer that saves you from a bad investment, and a third produce partial results that inform the next step. All three are reasonable returns for the cost.

Quality varies with the student, as it would with any individual. Interview candidates rather than accepting whoever is assigned, and speak to their supervisor about their working style.

Timelines are academic. Projects start when semesters start and finish when theses are due, so plan around the institution's calendar rather than your own.

And the most common failure is entirely within your control: a project that stalls because data access took three months or because the internal supervisor was unavailable.

## Real example

### The thesis that became a product and a hire

A family-owned packaging manufacturer in the south of the Netherlands suspected that data from its production lines could predict quality problems, but had nobody to investigate.

Through a local university of applied sciences, they took on a graduation student for five months. The brief was narrow: using existing sensor and quality data from one line, determine whether defects could be anticipated early enough to intervene, and quantify the potential saving.

The production manager met the student weekly. The IT manager arranged a data extract in the first week, before the project formally started.

The result was mixed in the best way. Prediction was possible for two of five defect categories and not for the others, and the student explained clearly why — the remaining categories depended on material properties that were never measured. The report included a recommendation to instrument two additional measurements.

They implemented the recommendation, hired the student after graduation, and two years later had a small internal data team.

The managing director's summary: it cost a supervisor's time and a modest allowance, and it told them what to do next.

## Key Takeaways

- Thesis projects, internships, applied research and consortium programmes offer different levels of commitment.
- A good brief has a real decision, available data, achievable scope, a named supervisor and a defined deliverable.
- Arrange data access before the project starts, not during it.
- A negative result that explains why is a valuable outcome, not a failure.
- These collaborations are one of the most effective recruitment channels available to regional employers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: company with no technical staff) Can we host a student without a data specialist?
Yes, provided someone knows the business problem and the data, and the university supervisor provides the technical guidance.

### (Scenario: employer worried about confidentiality) Will our data become public?
Theses are normally published, but confidential appendices, anonymisation and embargo arrangements are standard practice. Agree this in advance in writing.

### (Scenario: employer asking about cost) What does it cost?
Typically a modest allowance for the student plus supervisor time, with amounts and obligations varying by country and institution. It is far cheaper than a consultancy engagement.

### (Scenario: employer wanting results) Will we get something usable?
Sometimes a prototype, usually an evidence-based answer about feasibility. Treat it as investigation rather than as delivery.

### (Scenario: employer thinking about hiring) Can we hire the student afterwards?
Frequently, and it is one of the main reasons to do it. Discuss it openly rather than assuming.
