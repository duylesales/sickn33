Most teams that want a broader range of candidates approach it as an outreach problem and are disappointed. The larger losses usually happen inside the process, where specific and fixable practices exclude people who could do the job. Inclusive hiring for AI teams is mostly a set of unglamorous changes to how vacancies are written and how assessment is run, and each of them improves hiring quality generally rather than trading it away.

## Where Candidates Are Actually Lost

**At the vacancy.** Long requirement lists deter candidates who meet most but not all of them, and this self-selection is unevenly distributed. Unnecessary demands — a specific degree, years of experience with a framework, a driving licence — exclude people for reasons unrelated to capability.

**At application.** Processes requiring an account, an hour of form-filling or a cover letter nobody reads lose people with less time.

**At the take-home.** A fifteen-hour assignment selects for free evenings, which correlates with not having caring responsibilities or a second job.

**At scheduling.** Interviews offered only between nine and five on short notice exclude people who are employed, caring for someone, or travelling.

**In the interview itself.** Unstructured conversations reward cultural similarity to the interviewer. Questions about hobbies and side projects measure available leisure time.

**At the offer.** Salary set by negotiation rather than by band systematically disadvantages candidates who negotiate less assertively, a pattern documented across many labour markets.

None of these require a change in standards. They require removing filters that were never measuring ability.

## Inclusive Hiring for AI Teams: Changes to the Vacancy

Write only the requirements that are genuinely necessary. If someone could learn it in a month, it is not a requirement.

Separate essential from desirable explicitly, and keep the essential list to three or four items.

State that candidates who do not meet everything are encouraged to apply. This measurably widens the pool at no cost.

Publish the salary range. Beyond the transparency obligations arriving under Directive (EU) 2023/970, this removes an information asymmetry that disadvantages candidates without networks.

Describe the work concretely rather than using language about rockstars, ninjas or working hard and playing hard, which signals a specific culture and deters others.

Say what flexibility exists: hours, hybrid arrangements, part-time possibilities. These determine whether large groups of capable people can apply at all.

Remove degree requirements unless the role genuinely needs one. Many do not.

## Assessment That Measures the Job

The largest gains come from changing what you assess and how consistently you assess it.

**Write the criteria before the process.** Four or five capabilities the role genuinely requires, with a description of what adequate and strong look like for each. Every candidate is assessed against the same list.

**Ask about work, not about enthusiasm.** "Walk me through a project where the first approach failed" reveals capability. "What do you build in your spare time?" measures free time.

**Make practical exercises resemble the job.** Open book, with documentation and the tools people actually use, discussed afterwards. Puzzle-style questions and whiteboard algorithm exercises predict poorly and disadvantage candidates who have not spent months rehearsing them.

**Offer a choice of format.** A ninety-minute live session or a shorter take-home. People have different circumstances and different anxieties, and neither format is a better predictor.

**Have interviewers write assessments independently** before discussing. Group discussion before independent scoring converges on whoever spoke first.

**Involve more than one assessor**, ideally with different backgrounds.

**Ban the phrase culture fit** and replace it with specific behaviours you require: handling disagreement, communicating with non-specialists, responding to feedback. Fit is where similarity to the interviewer is smuggled in as a criterion.

## Accessibility and Reasonable Adjustments

A group frequently overlooked entirely: candidates with disabilities, including conditions that are not visible.

Across the EU, employers have obligations to provide reasonable accommodation for disabled candidates and employees, and these apply during recruitment as well as employment. Beyond compliance, most adjustments are trivial and improve the process for everyone.

Practical measures: state in every vacancy that adjustments are available and give a contact for requesting them; send interview questions or the exercise topic in advance, which helps candidates with anxiety, processing differences and non-native language speakers alike; allow extra time on timed exercises when requested, without requiring justification; ensure video interview platforms support captions; and confirm physical accessibility of any on-site location before inviting someone.

For neurodivergent candidates specifically, several common practices are unnecessarily exclusionary: rapid-fire questioning, assessment of eye contact and social ease, ambiguous open-ended questions, and interview panels of five people. Clear structure, advance information and written questions alongside verbal ones address most of it.

Do not ask about health or disability except to offer adjustments. In most European jurisdictions health questions during recruitment are restricted, and they are unnecessary: what you need to know is what adjustment would help, not why.

## The Legal Frame in Europe

Several obligations shape what employers may and must do, and structured hiring satisfies most of them naturally.

**Equal treatment.** EU directives prohibit discrimination in employment on grounds including sex, racial or ethnic origin, religion or belief, disability, age and sexual orientation, implemented through national law. Both direct and indirect discrimination are covered, and indirect discrimination is the relevant risk here: a neutral requirement that disadvantages a protected group and cannot be objectively justified. A fifteen-hour take-home assignment is a plausible example.

**Pay transparency.** Directive (EU) 2023/970 introduces requirements around providing pay information to applicants and restricting questions about pay history, with member states implementing nationally.

**Data protection.** Candidate data requires a lawful basis, proportionate processing, defined retention and transparency.

**AI in recruitment.** Under the EU AI Act, AI systems used for recruitment, screening and evaluation of candidates are classified as high-risk, with obligations on providers and deployers covering risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and transparency. GDPR separately restricts decisions based solely on automated processing.

The practical implication is that a documented, consistent process assessed against written criteria is both the fairest approach and the one you can defend if challenged. Impressionistic hiring is neither.

## Widening the Pool Without Outreach Programmes

Sourcing changes help, and the cheapest ones are structural rather than promotional.

**Advertise where different people look.** Specialist boards, regional channels, university and applied-science institutions beyond the most prestigious, and communities for under-represented groups in technology.

**Reconsider the pipeline you rely on.** Referral hiring reproduces existing networks. Keep it, and balance it with open channels so it is not the dominant route.

**Consider career changers seriously.** People entering data work from other professions bring domain knowledge and professional maturity, and they are systematically screened out by requirements framed around a conventional path.

**Consider returners.** People coming back after a career break are experienced, usually stable, and undervalued.

**Reconsider location requirements.** A rigid on-site policy excludes people with caring responsibilities, disabilities affecting travel and those outside commuting range.

**Reconsider language requirements.** Requiring fluency in the national language for a role conducted in English excludes international candidates for no operational reason. Requiring it where it is genuinely needed is legitimate.

Each of these expands the field of candidates who can do the job. That is the entire mechanism: most under-representation in technical hiring is produced by filters that were never measuring ability in the first place.

## Measuring Whether Anything Changed

Without measurement this becomes an exercise in intentions, and the metrics needed are few.

Track applications per vacancy and drop-off at each stage. If half your candidates withdraw at the take-home, that is a design problem regardless of who they are.

Track offer acceptance and the stated reasons for declining.

Where you collect demographic data, do so carefully: separate it from the application, make it voluntary and anonymous, explain why you are collecting it, and comply with data protection rules, which are strict for special categories. In several European countries collecting certain categories is restricted or unlawful, so take national advice rather than copying practice from elsewhere.

Look at outcomes beyond hiring. Who stays after two years, who is promoted, who receives the interesting projects. A diverse intake that leaves within eighteen months indicates a retention problem, not a hiring success.

Review rejected candidates periodically. If people you declined are thriving in equivalent roles elsewhere, your assessment is measuring something other than capability.

Keep it proportionate. For a team hiring three people a year, a spreadsheet and an honest annual review is sufficient, and it is more than most organisations do.

## After Hiring: The Part That Determines Whether It Works

Recruiting a broader range of people and then losing them is worse than not recruiting them, because the individuals have paid the cost.

The conditions that determine retention are ordinary management: a defined first project, a named person to ask questions, feedback given early and specifically, and work that develops rather than repeats.

Two patterns deserve particular attention in technical teams. Being the only person of a given background on a team is isolating, and hiring in pairs rather than individually changes the experience substantially. And informal knowledge transfer — the conversations where things are actually decided — can exclude people who are not part of the established social group, which is fixed by making decisions in writing rather than in corridors.

Meeting behaviour matters more than policy. Who gets interrupted, whose suggestion is attributed to someone else, who is asked to take notes. Managers who notice and correct these patterns retain people; those who do not lose them for reasons that never appear in an exit interview.

Progression is the longer test. If the same people are promoted regardless of who is hired, the hiring change has achieved nothing durable, and the problem is in how contribution is recognised rather than in recruitment.

## What Not to Do

A few practices are common, well intentioned and counterproductive.

**Adding a diversity statement to an otherwise unchanged process.** Candidates read the requirements and the process, not the statement.

**Unconscious bias training as the only intervention.** Evidence for durable behaviour change from one-off training is weak. Structural changes to process work better.

**Assigning the work to the people most affected.** Asking the one woman on the team to fix the team's gender balance adds unpaid labour to someone who did not create the problem.

**Lowering requirements for some candidates.** This is both unlawful in most European contexts and corrosive, because it undermines the standing of everyone hired. The correct approach is removing requirements that were never necessary, for everyone.

**Photographs and personal details on applications.** Several European countries have moved away from these, and anonymising the first screening stage is straightforward.

**Treating it as a recruitment problem alone.** If people leave within two years, hiring differently changes nothing.

The through-line is that the effective measures are specific, structural and applied to everyone. Anything that depends on individual goodwill, or that singles out particular candidates, tends to produce the appearance of change without the substance.

## Real example

### The change that doubled the applicant pool

A European software company struggled to hire data engineers and attributed it to market scarcity. Applications averaged eight per vacancy and the team was homogeneous.

A recruiter reviewed the process rather than the sourcing. The vacancy listed fourteen requirements including a master's degree and five years with a specific cloud platform. The application required registration on a portal. The assessment was a take-home estimated at eight hours, in practice longer. Interviews were offered within office hours with three days' notice.

They changed all four. The vacancy listed four essential requirements and dropped the degree. Applications were a CV by email. The take-home became an optional ninety-minute live session, with a shorter take-home alternative for those who preferred it. Interview slots included early mornings and evenings.

Applications rose to nineteen per vacancy, with a visibly broader range of backgrounds. They hired two people, one of whom had no degree and eleven years of relevant experience.

The hiring manager's observation was that they had not lowered the bar; they had stopped measuring things that were not the bar.

## Key Takeaways

- Most exclusion happens inside the process, not at sourcing.
- Long requirement lists and long take-homes are the two largest avoidable filters.
- Structured assessment against written criteria is fairer and predicts better.
- Publishing salary ranges reduces an asymmetry that disadvantages some candidates systematically.
- Measure the funnel; impressions about where candidates are lost are usually wrong.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: manager worried about standards) Does this mean lowering the bar?
No. It means assessing the bar rather than proxies for it. Structured assessment against defined criteria is more predictive than unstructured impressions.

### (Scenario: small employer) Can we do this without a recruitment function?
Yes. The changes described here are mostly editing a document and adjusting a calendar.

### (Scenario: employer considering AI screening tools) Will software help?
Take advice first. Recruitment AI is treated as high-risk under the EU AI Act and GDPR restricts automated decisions with significant effects.

### (Scenario: employer asked about quotas) Is positive discrimination allowed?
European equal treatment law permits certain positive action measures under defined conditions, but selection criteria must remain lawful. Take national advice before implementing anything.

### (Scenario: employer wanting evidence) How do we know if it worked?
Track applications per vacancy, drop-off by stage, offer acceptance and, over time, who stays and progresses.
