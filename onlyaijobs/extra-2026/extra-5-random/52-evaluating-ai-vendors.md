Most European organisations buying AI software have no one internally who can assess it, which is precisely why the sales process is designed the way it is. The demonstration is impressive, the case studies are unverifiable and the pricing is structured so that comparison is difficult. Evaluating AI vendors well does not require a data science team. It requires a small number of specific questions, a properly designed trial and a willingness to walk away.

## Start With the Decision, Not the Technology

Before speaking to anyone, write down three things: the decision or process you want to improve, how it is done today, and how you will know in six months whether it improved.

This sounds obvious and is routinely skipped. Organisations that begin with "we should be using AI" end up buying a capability and then searching for a use, which is the most reliable way to waste a budget.

If you cannot state the current cost of the problem — hours spent, errors made, revenue lost, decisions delayed — you are not ready to buy, because you will have no basis for judging whether the product worked.

## Evaluating AI Vendors: The Questions That Matter

**What does it do when it is unsure?** The single most informative question. Good products have a defined behaviour: they decline, flag for review, or express confidence. Products that always produce an answer are hiding their failure mode.

**How was it evaluated, and on whose data?** Ask for the measurement, not the claim. On what data, how many cases, compared with what baseline, and does that data resemble yours?

**What happens with our data?** Where it is processed and stored, whether it is used to improve their models, who can access it, and what happens when the contract ends.

**Who else like us uses it?** Not a logo wall — a reference customer of similar size in a similar sector, whom you can call without the vendor present.

**What does integration actually require?** From us, in staff time, over what period.

**How is it priced as we grow?** Per user, per document, per call, per model invocation. Model the second and third year, not the first.

**What is the exit?** Can you export your data and any configuration, and in what format.

## Run a Proof of Value, Not a Demonstration

A demonstration shows the product working on the vendor's chosen examples. That tells you nothing about your data.

A useful trial has four properties: it uses your data, including the awkward cases; it is scored against criteria you define before it starts; it is compared with your current approach as a baseline; and it is time-boxed with a defined decision at the end.

Include the cases you know are difficult. Vendors sometimes resist this, and the resistance is itself informative.

## Reading a Vendor's Claims

Marketing language in this market has a small number of recurring patterns, and recognising them saves time.

**Accuracy figures with no denominator.** "Ninety-seven per cent accurate" means nothing without knowing on what data, how many cases, which task and compared with what. Ask for all four.

**Benchmarks on public datasets.** Useful as evidence that the technology works at all, irrelevant as evidence that it will work on your documents, your language or your process.

**Case studies with percentages and no absolutes.** "Reduced processing time by sixty per cent" invites the question: from what, for how many cases, measured how, over what period.

**Named large customers.** Check what they actually bought. A pilot in one department is presented identically to an enterprise deployment.

**Proprietary model claims.** Many products are a thin layer over the same commercial models everyone else uses, which is not disqualifying but affects both price and defensibility. Ask directly what runs underneath.

**Self-improving systems.** Ask precisely what improves, from what feedback, and whether your data is used to train models other customers benefit from.

None of this implies dishonesty. Vendors present their strongest evidence, as anyone would. The buyer's job is to ask the questions that make the evidence comparable.

## Designing a Trial That Tells You Something

A proof of value costs a few weeks and prevents years of regret. Design it deliberately.

**Sample properly.** Use a random sample from a real period, not a curated selection. If the population is varied, stratify to ensure the difficult categories are represented. Say in advance how the sample was drawn.

**Define correct in advance.** Have your own experts label the expected outcome before seeing the system's output. This is the step organisations skip, and without it you are assessing plausibility rather than accuracy.

**Measure the baseline.** How accurate is your current process? People are often surprised: human agreement on the same task is frequently lower than expected, which changes what a good result looks like.

**Score the failures, not only the successes.** What kinds of error occur, how would they be detected, and what would they cost?

**Include the workflow.** A product that is accurate but requires three extra steps per case may not save anything. Time the end-to-end process with real staff.

**Set the decision rule before starting.** What result leads to purchase, to renegotiation, or to stopping. Agreeing this in advance prevents the momentum that carries weak pilots into contracts.

## Data, Privacy and Where Processing Happens

For European buyers, data questions are usually the ones that determine whether a purchase is possible at all.

Establish whether personal data is involved and on what lawful basis it would be processed. If the vendor processes personal data on your behalf, you need a data processing agreement under GDPR covering purposes, security measures, sub-processors, breach notification, assistance with data subject rights, and deletion or return at the end of the contract.

Ask where processing physically happens, including any model providers the vendor uses underneath, and whether data leaves the EU. International transfers require an appropriate mechanism, and this is a question your legal colleagues will ask regardless.

Ask explicitly whether your data is used to train models. Many vendors offer a contractual assurance that it is not; some default to the opposite. For confidential or personal data this is usually a hard requirement.

Check retention: how long inputs, outputs and logs are kept, and whether deletion is genuine.

In regulated sectors — health, finance, public administration — additional sector rules and supervisory expectations apply, and procurement rules may require formal tendering. Involve legal and data protection colleagues at the evaluation stage rather than at signature, where they become an obstacle rather than a contributor.

## Regulatory Obligations as a Buyer

The EU AI Act places obligations not only on providers of AI systems but on organisations that deploy them, and this is frequently overlooked during procurement.

Whether obligations apply depends on the use case. Systems used in areas such as recruitment and worker management, access to essential services, creditworthiness assessment, education, law enforcement and certain critical infrastructure functions are treated as high-risk, with requirements that reach the deploying organisation: using the system according to instructions, ensuring human oversight by people with the competence and authority to exercise it, monitoring operation, keeping logs, and informing affected people in defined circumstances.

Transparency requirements also apply more broadly, for example where people interact with an AI system or where synthetic content is generated.

Practically, this means three questions during evaluation. Does this use case fall into a regulated category? Does the vendor provide the documentation and instructions we would need? And do we have the internal capacity to exercise the oversight we would be responsible for?

Getting an answer before signing is straightforward. Discovering afterwards that a deployed system requires oversight nobody has capacity for is expensive, and it is currently one of the more common problems European organisations report.

## Commercial Terms Worth Negotiating

Pricing in this market is less standardised than in ordinary software, which means there is more room to negotiate and more opportunity to misjudge cost.

**Understand the unit.** Per user, per document, per transaction, per model call, per gigabyte. Model your realistic volume in year three, including growth and any seasonal peaks. Consumption-based pricing that looks cheap in a pilot can become the largest line in a departmental budget.

**Cap the increases.** Annual uplift clauses and renewal pricing deserve attention.

**Tie payment to outcomes where possible.** Acceptance criteria met before full payment; phased commitments rather than a three-year contract at signature.

**Service levels that mean something.** Availability is easy to promise. Ask about accuracy expectations, what happens when a model is updated, and what notice you receive of changes that affect output.

**Change of model.** If the vendor switches the underlying model, your results change. Require notice and the right to re-test.

**Exit.** Data export in an open format, assistance during transition, and defined deletion.

**Liability and insurance.** What the vendor is responsible for if their system produces a costly error, and what their cover actually is.

## Build, Buy, or Something in Between

The choice is not binary, and the middle options are often the right ones for organisations without large technical teams.

**Buy** when the capability is not what differentiates you, mature products exist, and your requirements are ordinary. Document processing, transcription, standard forecasting and common security tooling generally fall here.

**Build** when the capability is central to what your organisation does, your data or process is genuinely unusual, and you can employ and retain the people needed. Building without the ability to maintain what you built is the worst outcome of all.

**Buy the platform, build the application.** Use commercial models, cloud services and open components, and build the thin layer specific to you. This is what most European organisations with small teams actually do, and it balances control against effort.

**Buy with a partner.** Engage a consultancy or contractor to build, with an explicit requirement to train your staff and hand over documented, maintainable work. Insist on this in the contract, because the default outcome is a system nobody internally understands.

Whichever route, one internal person must own the capability. Organisations that outsource the understanding of their own processes end up unable to evaluate, negotiate or change anything, which is a more expensive position than any licence fee.

## After the Purchase

Evaluation does not end at signature, and the organisations that get value from AI products are those that keep measuring.

Agree how performance will be monitored in production and who reviews it. Sample outputs periodically and check them against expert judgement, because degradation is usually silent. Record the errors that occur and what they cost.

Watch for process drift: staff developing workarounds, uncertain cases being approved unread, or the system being used for a purpose it was not evaluated for. Each is common and each undermines the original business case.

Keep the baseline alive. If you can still describe how the work was done before, you retain the ability to change vendor or bring the capability in-house. Organisations that dismantle the old process entirely lose that option within a year.

Review annually against the criteria you set at the start, and be willing to conclude that it is not working.

## Real example

### The pilot that was designed to succeed

A regional healthcare organisation evaluated a document processing product for referral letters. The vendor ran a pilot on two hundred letters and reported accuracy above ninety-five per cent. The procurement team were ready to sign.

An analyst asked how the two hundred had been selected. They had been provided by the organisation's own project team, who had chosen clean, typical examples to make the pilot straightforward.

A second round used a random sample from a single week, including handwritten notes, scans of faxes, letters in two languages and documents with missing pages. Accuracy on that sample was substantially lower, and the product had no mechanism for flagging uncertain extractions, so errors would have entered the record silently.

They negotiated differently: a lower initial price, a requirement for confidence scores and a human review workflow, and acceptance criteria measured on random samples quarterly.

The project lead's conclusion was that the vendor had not been dishonest. Nobody had asked how the sample was chosen.

## Key Takeaways

- Define the decision and the current cost before evaluating any product.
- Ask what the system does when it is uncertain; the answer reveals the product's maturity.
- Trial on your own data, including the difficult cases, with criteria set in advance.
- Model pricing over three years, not one.
- Check regulatory obligations early, particularly where the use case may be high-risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: organisation without technical staff) Can we evaluate this without expertise?
Largely yes, using the questions above. For a significant purchase, a few days of independent technical review is cheap insurance.

### (Scenario: buyer facing regulatory questions) Do we have obligations under the EU AI Act?
Possibly. Obligations depend on the use case and on your role as deployer, and some uses are classified as high-risk. Take advice before signing.

### (Scenario: buyer comparing options) Should we build instead?
Build when the capability is core to what you do and you can staff it. Buy when it is a supporting capability and a mature product exists.

### (Scenario: buyer worried about lock-in) How do we protect ourselves?
Insist on data export, avoid exclusive access to your own data, keep contract terms short initially and document your configuration.

### (Scenario: buyer with limited budget) What is the cheapest sensible approach?
A small, time-boxed paid pilot with clear criteria. Free pilots are often designed to be difficult to exit.
