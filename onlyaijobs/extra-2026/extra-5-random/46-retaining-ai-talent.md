A data scientist who leaves after eighteen months takes with them the knowledge of why a field is unreliable, which join produces duplicates and what the model actually does at the boundary. Replacing them costs months of recruiting, months of onboarding and a period of reduced output from whoever supervises the replacement. Retaining AI talent is therefore cheaper than hiring it, and most of what works costs less than the recruitment fee.

## Why People Actually Leave

Exit interviews produce polite answers. The honest reasons, gathered across many European teams, cluster into six.

**Nothing reached production.** The most common reason among engineers. Building prototypes that are praised and then shelved is demoralising in a way that no salary corrects.

**No growth visible.** They cannot see what the next two years look like, or they have stopped learning. This bites hardest around the two-year mark.

**Bad data foundations.** Spending eighty per cent of the time on broken pipelines, with no mandate or budget to fix them properly.

**Being unheard.** Raising concerns about feasibility or data quality and being overruled repeatedly by people who do not understand the constraint.

**Pay that drifted.** Not being underpaid at hire, but being underpaid two years later because the market moved and internal increases did not.

**The manager.** As everywhere, though in data teams it is often a manager who cannot evaluate the work and therefore cannot advocate for it.

Note how few of these are primarily about money, and how many are about whether the work matters.

## Retaining AI Talent Starts With Shipping

The single strongest retention factor for technical people is that their work reaches users. That is a leadership problem rather than an engineering one, and it usually requires three things.

**Fewer projects, finished.** A team running six pilots finishes none. A team running two ships both.

**A route to production that exists.** Someone owns deployment, there is a process, and it has been used before. If every project invents this from scratch, most will not survive the attempt.

**Willingness to stop.** Cancelling a project honestly is far better for morale than letting it drift for a year. People leave over drift, not over decisions.

## What Departure Actually Costs

Managers who treat turnover as an unavoidable background rate usually have not costed it.

A mid-level data scientist leaving a European team typically costs: two to four months of recruitment, including management and interviewing time; a vacancy period during which their work is not done or is done badly by someone else; three to six months before the replacement is productive; a period of reduced output from whoever supervises the new person; and, hardest to quantify, the loss of undocumented knowledge about data quirks, stakeholder history and why certain decisions were made.

Agency fees, where used, are typically a substantial percentage of first-year salary on top of all this.

Conservatively, the total is comparable to a significant fraction of an annual salary, and for a specialist with deep domain knowledge it can exceed it.

Set against that, the measures that retain people — a salary correction, an engineer who owns deployment, protected learning time, a clearer progression conversation — are inexpensive. The asymmetry is large enough that retention deserves the same management attention as hiring, and in most organisations it receives far less.

## Pay Without Illusions

Money is not usually the first reason people leave, and it is reliably the reason they take the call.

The pattern to watch is drift. Someone hired at a fair rate receives modest annual increases while market rates move faster, and after two or three years they are materially below what they would be offered elsewhere. They discover this when a recruiter tells them, and by then the conversation is a resignation rather than a review.

Practical measures: benchmark roles annually against current market data rather than against last year's internal band; adjust proactively rather than waiting for a competing offer; and review after promotion or significant scope change, not at the next annual cycle.

Be aware that transparency is arriving regardless. Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970, member states are implementing rules on pay information for applicants and employees, including access to information about pay levels for comparable work. Internal inconsistencies that were previously invisible will become visible, and organisations that address them in advance will have easier conversations than those that do not.

Where you genuinely cannot match market rates, say so plainly and compete on what you do have. People accept a known trade-off; they resent discovering one.

## Growth That Is Not a Promotion

Most technical people who say they want career development do not primarily mean a title. They mean they want to be better in two years than they are now, and to be able to see how.

Things that provide this cheaply:

**Increasing scope.** Ownership of a larger system, a harder problem, or responsibility for an area rather than a task.

**Technical depth.** Protected time to learn something the role will need — a day a fortnight, used properly, is more than most people get.

**Mentoring others.** Supervising a junior or an intern develops people faster than almost anything else and provides visible seniority without a vacancy.

**External exposure.** Conference attendance, a talk, a paper, participation in a community. Inexpensive and valued.

**Rotation.** A quarter on a different problem within the organisation.

**A stated ladder.** Even a simple written description of what distinguishes levels removes the sense that progression is arbitrary.

The conversation matters as much as the substance. Ask each person once or twice a year what they want to be able to do in two years, and then act on at least part of the answer. Teams where this conversation never happens lose people who would have stayed.

## Fixing the Foundations People Complain About

Technical frustration is an underrated cause of departure, and it is specific enough to address.

The recurring complaints in data teams are consistent: pipelines that break weekly with no time allocated to fix them properly; no access to the data required without a three-week request process; an environment where deploying anything takes a month; tooling chosen years ago and never revisited; and no monitoring, so problems are discovered by stakeholders.

Each of these is a decision about investment, and each is usually cheaper to fix than to tolerate. A recurring engineering allocation — a fixed share of capacity for foundations, defended rather than sacrificed to the next urgent request — is the standard remedy, and teams that hold to it report both better delivery and better retention.

The signal it sends matters as much as the work. A team that is permitted to fix what it complains about concludes that its judgement is trusted. A team that raises the same problem for two years and is always told there is no time concludes the opposite, and the good engineers, who have options, act on that conclusion first.

Ask your team once a quarter what wastes their time. Then fix one of the answers.

## Managers Who Can Advocate

Data teams have a specific management problem: the manager frequently cannot evaluate the technical work and therefore cannot defend it.

The symptoms are recognisable. Unrealistic deadlines accepted because nobody pushed back. Work estimated by people who do not understand it. A team's careful explanation of why something is not feasible overruled by a confident executive. Performance assessed on activity rather than on outcomes nobody can judge.

Engineers tolerate this for a while and then leave, usually saying they wanted a new challenge.

Two remedies work. Promote or hire managers who have done the work, accepting that they will need support in the management craft. Or, where that is not possible, ensure the team has a senior technical person with standing to speak, and that this person is present when commitments are made.

Whichever route, the manager's job in a data team includes translating upward: explaining uncertainty to people who want certainty, defending time spent on foundations, and saying no to requests that cannot be met. A team whose manager does this reliably will forgive a great deal else.

Teams also notice how a manager behaves when a project fails. A manager who absorbs the criticism and protects the team keeps people; one who distances themselves does not.

## Flexibility as a Retention Instrument

In much of Europe, working conditions are a more powerful retention lever than employers assume, and often cheaper than pay.

Hybrid arrangements are now standard for most data work, and imposing a rigid office requirement without a reason that the team accepts is a reliable way to lose people who have arranged their lives around flexibility. Where presence genuinely matters — collaboration with operations, laboratory work, secure environments — say why.

Part-time and compressed arrangements are well established in several European countries and are frequently the difference between a valued colleague staying and leaving after parental leave. Several countries give employees statutory rights to request reduced hours; treating such requests as normal rather than exceptional costs little.

Parental leave, elder care and the practical realities of family life affect a large share of any team over a decade. Employers who handle these periods well acquire remarkably loyal employees.

Also worth attention: respecting boundaries. Teams where evening messages are normal, or where holiday is interrupted, lose people regardless of what the policy says. The manager's behaviour sets this, not the handbook.

These measures cost almost nothing and are visible to every candidate you later try to hire.

## Measuring Retention Honestly

Keep it simple, and act on what you learn.

Track voluntary departures over a rolling period, tenure at departure, and the distribution across the team — losing three people from one sub-team is a different problem from losing three across the department.

Run proper exit conversations, ideally not with the person's direct manager, and ask specific questions: what would have had to change for you to stay, when did you first consider leaving, what did we not notice. The honest answer is usually available if the conversation is genuinely confidential and the person is already leaving.

More useful still are stay conversations: asking people who are not leaving what would make them consider it. The answers are actionable while the person is still there, which is the entire point.

Watch for early signals: disengagement in design discussions, reduced willingness to take on new work, a sudden profile update, and the quiet withdrawal that often precedes a resignation by a few months.

## Real example

### The team that stopped losing people

A European insurer's data team lost four of nine people in eighteen months. The department head assumed it was pay and prepared a case for increases.

The team lead asked to interview the leavers informally first. Three of the four said the same thing: they had built models that were never deployed, because no one owned the integration into the underwriting system and each project stalled at the same point.

The response was structural. They hired one engineer specifically to own the path to production, reduced the number of parallel projects from seven to three, and made deployment ownership part of every project definition from the start.

They also reviewed salaries, and adjusted two that had drifted below market.

Over the following two years, one person left, for reasons unrelated to work. The department head's conclusion was that the money would have helped nobody if the underlying problem had stayed, and that the fix cost less than one recruitment process.

## Key Takeaways

- The most common reason technical people leave AI teams is that nothing they build reaches production.
- Pay drift, not starting pay, is what causes salary-driven departures.
- Visible growth and being heard matter more than perks.
- Cancelling projects honestly is better for retention than letting them drift.
- Most effective retention measures cost less than replacing one person.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: manager who cannot raise salaries) What if pay increases are not possible?
Fix drift where you can and be honest where you cannot. Then compete on scope, shipping, learning and flexibility, which often matter more.

### (Scenario: manager facing a resignation) Is a counteroffer worth making?
Rarely a durable fix unless it addresses the actual reason. Use the conversation to learn what that reason is.

### (Scenario: small team) We cannot offer a career ladder. What then?
Offer growing scope and genuine ownership, which is what a ladder is a proxy for. Say plainly what you can and cannot offer.

### (Scenario: manager worried about a single point of failure) How do we reduce knowledge risk?
Documentation, pairing, rotating ownership and shared on-call. Knowledge concentrated in one person is a risk to them as much as to you.

### (Scenario: manager reviewing pay) How often should we review salaries?
At least annually against current market rates. Pay transparency rules under Directive (EU) 2023/970 will make internal inconsistencies increasingly visible.
