MLOps engineer jobs have become one of the most consistently advertised categories in European AI hiring, and also one of the most misunderstood. Some postings describe a platform role building infrastructure for dozens of data scientists. Others describe a single engineer expected to deploy models, maintain pipelines, manage cloud costs and answer incident calls at night. Both are called MLOps. If you are considering this path — or trying to judge whether a specific vacancy is the right move — you need to look past the title and understand what the work actually involves. This guide explains the reality of MLOps roles, the skills employers test, how to move into the field and how to assess a team before you accept an offer.

## What MLOps Engineer Jobs Actually Involve

MLOps is the practice of getting machine learning models into production reliably and keeping them working. It borrows heavily from DevOps and software engineering, but adds problems that traditional software does not have: models depend on data that changes, their performance degrades silently, their behaviour is probabilistic, and reproducing a result may require the same data, code, parameters and environment.

In practice, MLOps work clusters around several responsibilities:

- **Deployment and serving**: packaging models as services or batch jobs, managing versions, handling latency and scaling requirements.
- **Pipelines**: automating training, evaluation and retraining so they run reliably rather than in someone's notebook.
- **Reproducibility**: versioning data, code, configuration and artefacts so a model can be rebuilt and audited.
- **Monitoring**: tracking data drift, prediction distributions, latency, errors and business metrics, and alerting when something breaks.
- **Infrastructure**: cloud resources, containers, orchestration, GPU scheduling and cost control.
- **Governance**: access control, lineage, documentation and audit trails, increasingly driven by regulation.

## The Three Versions of the Job

When you read a vacancy, try to work out which of these it describes.

**The platform builder.** You build and run an internal ML platform used by other teams: feature store, training orchestration, model registry, serving layer, monitoring. Your customers are data scientists inside the company. Success means other teams ship faster with fewer incidents. This version resembles infrastructure or developer-experience engineering.

**The embedded productioniser.** You sit inside a product or domain team and take models from prototype to production: hardening code, building pipelines, adding tests, deploying, monitoring. You work closely with one or two data scientists and often know the domain well.

**The one-person everything role.** In smaller organisations, one engineer does data engineering, modelling support, deployment, monitoring and sometimes analytics. It can be an excellent learning experience, but it can also mean permanent firefighting with no time to build anything properly.

None of these is inherently better, but they suit different people and offer different career paths. The mismatch between what a candidate expects and which version they join is one of the most common reasons MLOps engineers leave within a year.

## Skills Employers Test

Most MLOps interviews probe four areas:

1. **Software engineering.** Clean Python, testing, packaging, code review habits, Git workflows. Many candidates from data science backgrounds are weakest here, and it is usually the deciding factor.
2. **Infrastructure and cloud.** Containers, Kubernetes or managed alternatives, infrastructure as code, networking basics, IAM and secrets, cost awareness.
3. **ML-specific systems.** Training pipelines, experiment tracking, model registries, feature pipelines, batch versus online serving, drift monitoring, rollback strategies.
4. **Judgement.** When to build a platform versus when to use a managed service; how much automation a small team actually needs; how to make systems debuggable.

You do not need to be an expert in all four, but you need credible depth in at least two and working knowledge of the rest.

## How to Move Into MLOps

**From software or DevOps engineering.** You already have the strongest foundation. Learn the ML-specific parts: how models are trained and evaluated, what drift is, why reproducibility is hard, how feature pipelines differ from ordinary ETL. Offer to take over deployment and monitoring for a model your company already has.

**From data science.** You know models but often not production engineering. Invest deliberately in software engineering: write tested code, learn containers and CI/CD, take responsibility for one model's full lifecycle rather than handing it over.

**From data engineering.** You know pipelines and data quality, which is half the job. Add serving, monitoring and the modelling vocabulary you need to work with data scientists.

In all three cases, the fastest route is usually internal: volunteer for the unglamorous work of putting an existing model into production properly. That experience is far more convincing in interviews than certificates.

## Judging a Team's Maturity Before You Join

Ask specific questions and listen for specific answers:

- How long does it take a new model to go from notebook to production today?
- How many models are in production, and who is on call for them?
- What happens when a model's inputs change upstream?
- How do you know if a model is performing worse than last month?
- What is already built, and what would I be expected to build?
- What did the last production incident look like, and what changed afterwards?

Vague answers usually mean the work will be firefighting rather than engineering. That may be acceptable if you want to build from scratch and the organisation genuinely supports it — but it should be a deliberate choice.

## The Tooling Question

Candidates often worry about tool lists: which orchestrator, which registry, which serving framework. Employers do care, but less than vacancy texts suggest. Tools change every few years; the underlying problems do not. What transfers is understanding why a component exists — why you want a registry at all, what a feature store solves that a database does not, when streaming inference is justified and when a nightly batch job is perfectly adequate.

A useful way to prepare is to be able to explain, for each tool you have used, what would break if you removed it. If you cannot answer that, you know the tool but not the problem. Engineers who can reason from problems to tools adapt quickly to any stack, and interviewers notice the difference immediately.

## Where Regulation Meets MLOps

Compliance used to sit far from infrastructure work. That is changing. The EU AI Act sets requirements for high-risk AI systems that map directly onto MLOps capabilities: technical documentation, record-keeping and logging, data governance, accuracy and robustness, human oversight and post-market monitoring. GDPR adds requirements around personal data, retention and the ability to explain automated decisions.

In practice this means that lineage, versioning, audit logs and monitoring — things MLOps engineers build anyway — become obligations rather than nice-to-haves in sectors such as finance, insurance, healthcare, HR technology and public services. Engineers who can translate a regulatory requirement into a concrete platform feature are unusually valuable, because most organisations have people who understand the rules and people who understand the systems, but few who understand both.

If you are moving into MLOps, reading the high-risk requirements once and thinking about what they imply for your stack is a small investment with disproportionate returns in interviews.

## Cost, Carbon and the FinOps Angle

As organisations run more models, the cost of training and inference becomes visible on the balance sheet. MLOps engineers are increasingly asked to measure and reduce it: right-sizing instances, using spot capacity, caching, batching requests, quantising models, choosing smaller models where quality allows and switching off idle GPU resources. Energy consumption has become part of the same conversation, both for sustainability reporting and because energy is a large share of compute cost.

This work is unglamorous and highly valued. If you can show that you reduced inference cost substantially without hurting quality, you have a story that almost any employer understands.

## What a Good MLOps Interview Looks Like

Strong employers run MLOps interviews that resemble the job. Expect some combination of:

- **A systems design discussion**: design a serving and monitoring setup for a specific use case, with trade-offs between batch and online inference, latency budgets, failure modes and rollback.
- **A debugging scenario**: a model's predictions have degraded; how do you find out why?
- **Code review or a short practical task**: refactor a notebook into a reproducible pipeline, or write a small service with tests.
- **A conversation about a past incident**: what broke, how you diagnosed it, what you changed afterwards.

Weak processes ask only about tool names or set puzzle-style algorithm questions unrelated to the work. That is a signal about the team as much as about the process.

## Questions Worth Asking in the Interview

- Who owns the model once it is deployed: the data scientist, the platform team or both?
- How are on-call responsibilities distributed, and how often does someone get paged?
- What proportion of your time goes to new capabilities versus maintenance?
- Is there budget and mandate for platform work, or is it done between projects?
- How do you decide to build something internally rather than buy it?

The answers tell you whether the role is engineering or firefighting, and whether the organisation will give you the space to do the work properly.

## Titles and Pay

Job titles in this area are inconsistent across Europe. The same work appears as MLOps engineer, ML platform engineer, machine learning infrastructure engineer, production ML engineer, AI engineer or simply senior software engineer in a data team. Search on responsibilities rather than titles, or you will miss roles that fit you well.

Pay generally tracks senior software engineering in the same market and sector rather than data science. Engineers who combine production experience with domain knowledge in a regulated sector — finance, healthcare, energy — tend to sit at the higher end, because the supply of people who can do both is small. Ranges vary far more by country and sector than by title, so benchmark locally rather than against international averages.

## Career Paths From MLOps

MLOps is not a dead end. Common next steps include platform engineering leadership, where you own the internal developer experience for data teams; staff or principal engineer roles spanning several teams; specialising in inference performance and hardware; moving into AI governance and assurance, where production knowledge is rare and valuable; or moving into product roles for data platforms. Some engineers return to modelling with a much stronger sense of what actually ships.

The common thread is that people who understand how systems behave in production accumulate leverage. That is why the role keeps appearing in vacancy lists even when hiring in other areas slows down.

## Building Evidence If You Do Not Have the Title Yet

If your current job does not include MLOps work, you can still build credible evidence:

- Take an existing internal model and give it a reproducible training pipeline, tests and a deployment you can describe.
- Add monitoring to something already in production and document what you learned when it alerted.
- Write an internal runbook for a model your team owns.
- Contribute to an open-source project in the ecosystem, even in small ways such as documentation or bug fixes.
- Build one personal project that is deployed and monitored rather than five that stop at a notebook.

Depth beats breadth here. One well-documented system you operated for months is worth more in an interview than a list of tools you tried once.

## A Final Word on Expectations

The best MLOps engineers are rarely the ones with the longest tool lists. They are the ones who reduce uncertainty: they make it obvious what is running, where it came from, whether it is healthy and what to do when it is not. If that kind of work appeals to you, the role offers durable demand and a clear path to seniority. If you want to spend most of your time on modelling, be honest with yourself before accepting an MLOps title — the work is engineering first, and the vacancies that pretend otherwise are the ones people leave.

## Real example

### An engineer who chose the less glamorous offer

A backend engineer with three years of experience wanted to move into machine learning. She received two offers: an "MLOps Engineer" role at a well-known scale-up, and a quieter role at a mid-sized logistics company.

At the scale-up, she asked how models reached production. The answer described a mature internal platform — but her team's job was mostly maintaining legacy training jobs. At the logistics company, the answer was blunt: two models in production, deployed manually, no monitoring, and a clear mandate to fix that within a year, with budget and a supportive manager.

She took the logistics role. In eighteen months she built a deployment pipeline, added monitoring that caught a data quality problem before it reached customers, and trained two data scientists to use it. When she next changed jobs, she could describe systems she had designed end to end — a stronger story than maintaining someone else's platform.

## Key Takeaways

- MLOps engineer jobs cover at least three different roles: platform builder, embedded productioniser and one-person generalist.
- Software engineering skill is usually the deciding factor in hiring, even for candidates with strong ML backgrounds.
- The fastest route in is to take full lifecycle responsibility for one existing model.
- Ask specific questions about deployment time, incidents and monitoring to judge a team's maturity.
- Regulation is pushing documentation, lineage and monitoring from optional to expected.
