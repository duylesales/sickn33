Most European companies hiring AI talent are not famous. They are manufacturers, insurers, logistics operators, software firms, hospitals and municipalities, competing for the same engineers as organisations with recognisable names and larger budgets. Many conclude they cannot win and pay an agency instead. In practice, employer branding for AI hiring is less about reputation than about legibility: candidates cannot evaluate what they cannot see, and most companies are invisible rather than unattractive.

## What Candidates Actually Evaluate

Experienced AI and data candidates assess four things, roughly in this order.

**Is the work real?** Is there data, a problem worth solving and something already in production, or is this an exploratory idea that will be cancelled in a year? This is the single biggest question, and most vacancies fail to answer it.

**Who will I work with?** Is there a colleague who knows more than me about something? Being the only data person in an organisation that has never shipped a model is a known career risk.

**Will I be supported?** Budget, access to data, decision-making authority, a manager who understands the work.

**Is my life going to be better?** Pay, commute, hours, flexibility, security.

Brand recognition sits far below these. A candidate who understands the problem you are solving and believes you are serious will choose you over a famous company they cannot picture themselves in.

## Employer Branding for AI Hiring Starts With the Vacancy

Your job advertisement is your brand for most candidates, because it is the only thing they will read. Three changes improve results immediately.

**Describe one real problem.** Not "leverage AI to drive business value" — the actual thing. "We forecast demand for eleven thousand products across sixty branches, and our current system was built in 2017."

**State what exists.** Data volumes, systems in production, team size and composition, tooling. Candidates infer seriousness from specifics.

**Publish the salary range.** Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970, member states are introducing requirements to provide pay information to applicants. Companies that publish ranges now gain an advantage while it is still unusual, and lose nothing but wasted processes.

Then remove the boilerplate: the list of twenty technologies, the personal qualities nobody screens for, and the claim to be innovative.

## The Honest Value Proposition Exercise

Before writing anything public, spend an hour answering five questions truthfully with the team that will host the new hire.

**What will this person actually do in year one?** Not the aspiration. The first three projects, in order.

**What do we have that a bigger company does not?** Usually one of: ownership of a whole system rather than a slice; proximity to the people affected by the work; a data asset nobody else has; a short path from idea to decision; or a location that lets someone live where they want.

**What is genuinely difficult here?** Legacy systems, limited tooling, a sceptical organisation, no existing data team. Naming this attracts the people who find it interesting and repels those who would leave in six months, which is the point.

**What would make a strong candidate refuse?** Pay band, on-site requirement, commute, technology constraints. Decide which you can change and accept the rest.

**Who is our realistic candidate?** A senior engineer from a technology company is probably not it. A strong analyst in a nearby company who wants more scope, an experienced engineer who has moved to the region for family reasons, or a good graduate from a local university might be.

Recruiting improves fastest when a company stops competing for candidates it cannot win and starts speaking clearly to the ones it can.

## What to Publish, and What Not To Bother With

Companies overestimate the effort required and then produce nothing. A minimal, maintainable set works better than an ambitious programme abandoned after two posts.

**Worth doing:** a careers page that describes the data team's actual work in five paragraphs; two or three short posts written by engineers about a real problem and how it was solved, including what failed; a clear description of the interview process; and profiles or names of the people a candidate will meet.

**Worth doing if you can sustain it:** an occasional talk at a local meetup, participation in a university project or thesis programme, and an open source contribution from work you already do.

**Rarely worth it:** glossy employer brand videos, generic culture pages, awards nobody has heard of, stock photography of people at whiteboards, and a blog nobody writes.

The test for any piece of content is whether a candidate could learn something specific from it that they could not guess. "We value innovation" fails. "Our forecasting runs nightly across eleven thousand series and we replaced the statistical models last year because of promotional volatility" passes.

Written by an engineer in forty minutes, such a post outperforms anything the marketing budget produces.

## Where Candidates Actually Look

Distribution matters as much as content, and it differs by seniority.

**Junior and graduate candidates** use general job boards, university career services, LinkedIn and specialised boards. They apply in volume and respond well to clear, encouraging postings that state what is genuinely required rather than listing five years of experience for a junior role.

**Mid-level and senior candidates** are mostly passive. They look when something prompts them: a colleague's recommendation, a company they noticed at a meetup, a specific problem that interests them. Reaching them depends on being visible in the places they already are — specialised boards, professional communities, local technical events — and on your existing employees' networks.

**Specialists** — a person who has built retrieval systems in regulated environments, or deployed vision on embedded hardware — are found through targeted search and referral, and are usually persuaded by the specific problem rather than by the company.

Practical advice for a smaller employer: use a specialised AI and data board so your vacancy sits among relevant roles rather than competing with hundreds of unrelated postings, ask your team to share it personally, and make sure the vacancy is good enough to be worth sharing.

## Referrals and Your Existing Team

Your current employees are the most credible channel you have, and most companies use them badly.

The precondition is that people are willing to recommend their workplace. If they are not, no referral bonus fixes it, and the money spent is better invested in the reasons they are unwilling.

Where the team is content, make referring easy. Tell them when a vacancy opens, in plain language, with one sentence they can forward. Let them talk to candidates informally before any process. Acknowledge referrals promptly, even when the answer is no, because nothing kills a referral programme faster than a colleague's friend being ignored for three weeks.

A modest bonus paid after a few months is fine; enormous bonuses tend to produce volume rather than quality.

Be aware of the fairness trade-off. Referral hiring reproduces existing networks, which can narrow the range of people you hire. Balance it with open channels and structured assessment so that the referral gets a conversation, not a shortcut through the process.

Former employees matter too. People who left on good terms recommend their old employer surprisingly often, and some return. Exit conversations handled decently are a recruitment investment.

## The Process Is the Brand

Everything a candidate experiences between applying and starting tells them how the company works, and they read it accurately.

A process that takes six weeks with two unexplained silences tells them decisions here are slow and communication is poor. A take-home assignment of fifteen hours tells them the company does not value people's time. An interviewer who has not read the CV tells them preparation is optional. A recruiter who cannot describe the work tells them the business does not understand its own technical function.

Conversely, a process where every stage is explained in advance, replies come within days, the technical interviewers are engaged and the decision arrives when promised is itself persuasive. Candidates routinely cite it as a reason for choosing one offer over another.

This costs nothing but attention. Set internal service levels — reply within three working days, decision within a week of the final interview — and hold to them. Tell candidates the timeline and update them if it slips.

Include a chance to ask the team real questions without the hiring manager present. Confident organisations offer it; candidates notice which ones do.

## Regional Strategy: Competing Where You Are

Many European companies outside the main technology hubs assume they cannot hire and therefore do not try properly. The evidence suggests otherwise, provided they recruit regionally rather than pretending to compete nationally.

People move for family, cost of housing, partners' jobs and quality of life, and they then look for work near where they now live. A company thirty minutes from a university city with a clear, well-written vacancy is competing against a much smaller field than it imagines.

Practical regional tactics: state the location and commute honestly, including parking and public transport; support hybrid working so the office is a place people choose rather than endure; build relationships with nearby universities and universities of applied sciences for theses and internships; appear at regional meetups; and target people who already live in the area rather than trying to attract relocation.

Being findable in your own region is often the entire problem.

## Measuring Whether Any of This Works

Keep the measurement light, or it becomes a project of its own.

Four numbers are enough: applications per vacancy, the proportion that are plausibly qualified, time from posting to signed offer, and offer acceptance rate. Track them per vacancy and compare after you change something.

The most informative source is candidates themselves. Ask every person who reaches an interview where they found the role and what made them apply. Ask everyone who declines an offer why, and record the answer honestly rather than the comfortable version. Over five or six hires, patterns are unmistakable: a commute problem, a pay band problem, a description problem or a process problem.

Also review the vacancies that failed. A role open for six months is telling you something — usually that the requirements are unrealistic, the range is below market, or the description does not convey that the work is real.

## Real example

### The manufacturer nobody had heard of

A family-owned manufacturer in the south of the Netherlands tried for eight months to hire a machine learning engineer. The vacancy described a "challenging role in a dynamic environment" and listed fourteen technologies. It produced three applications, none suitable.

The hiring manager rewrote it with an engineer. The new version explained that the company runs eleven production lines generating sensor data continuously, that they already had a vision inspection system in production built with a supplier, that they wanted to bring capability in-house, that the person would work with two process engineers and report to the operations director, and that the budget for tooling was already approved. It named the salary range and said the site was thirty minutes from Eindhoven with parking.

Eleven applications arrived in three weeks and they hired within two months. The candidate who accepted said the vacancy was the first she had read that made it clear there was actual work waiting.

## Key Takeaways

- Candidates evaluate whether the work is real, who they will work with, whether they will be supported, and whether life improves.
- The vacancy is your employer brand for most applicants.
- Specifics signal seriousness; adjectives signal nothing.
- Publishing a salary range is an advantage today and will become an obligation.
- Smaller companies win on clarity, scope and proximity rather than on prestige.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: company with no technical reputation) Do we need an engineering blog?
No. One honest, specific vacancy outperforms a neglected blog. Publish only what you can maintain.

### (Scenario: employer competing on pay) What if we cannot match technology salaries?
Compete on what you have: scope, proximity, stability, hours, the chance to own a system end to end. State it plainly rather than hoping candidates infer it.

### (Scenario: small team hiring its first data person) How do we reassure candidates they will not be alone?
Name who they will work with, what support exists, and what budget is approved. If there is genuinely no technical colleague, say so and explain what you will provide instead.

### (Scenario: employer using agencies) Are recruiters a substitute for this?
No. A recruiter still needs something true and specific to sell, and the strongest candidates often apply directly.

### (Scenario: employer starting out) What is the cheapest improvement?
Answering applications within a week. Candidate experience is brand, and most companies fail at it.
