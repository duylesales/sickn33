Moving from academia to industry in AI is easier than it was a decade ago and harder than the headlines suggest. Employers genuinely value what researchers bring — depth, independence, the ability to read and implement new methods — but they hire for different outputs. A paper is not a product, and a group of five people who each own their own project is not a team shipping one system together. Researchers who understand the difference make the transition quickly; those who assume their credentials speak for themselves often spend a frustrating year. This guide covers which industry roles suit which academic backgrounds, what to emphasise and what to unlearn, how to translate your CV and how to handle the first months.

## Which Roles Suit Researchers

**Applied scientist or research scientist.** The closest analogue: working on hard modelling problems, often with publication opportunities. Competitive, concentrated in larger companies and research-oriented labs, and usually requiring both depth and engineering competence.

**Machine learning engineer.** Building and deploying models in production. Requires more software engineering than most PhDs have, but the modelling judgement transfers directly, and the roles are far more numerous.

**Data scientist.** Analysis, experimentation and modelling to support decisions. Statistically trained researchers often excel here, particularly in domains with causal or experimental questions.

**Research engineer.** Building tooling, infrastructure and experiments for research teams. An excellent entry point for people who enjoy making other people's research work.

**Domain specialist roles.** Physics, chemistry, biology, geoscience, linguistics and medicine PhDs are valuable where the domain matters: materials, drug discovery, remote sensing, energy, health. The domain is often harder to hire for than the ML.

**Governance, evaluation and assurance.** A newer path where analytical rigour and the ability to critique evidence are the core skills.

## What Employers Actually Value From a PhD

Not the title. What they value is evidence of:

- **Independence**: you can take an ill-defined problem and make progress without being told how.
- **Depth**: you can go further than a quick literature scan when the problem requires it.
- **Rigour**: you understand experimental design, statistical claims and what constitutes evidence.
- **Learning speed**: you can absorb a new field quickly, which is exactly what applied work requires.
- **Communication**: you have explained complex work to audiences before — although often to specialists rather than stakeholders.

Employers discount: publication counts beyond a few relevant examples, the prestige of your supervisor, and technique-first thinking. Nobody in a product team cares that a method is novel if a simpler one performs adequately.

## What to Unlearn

Three academic habits cause most friction:

**Optimising for novelty.** In industry, the best solution is usually the simplest one that meets the requirement. Reaching for a sophisticated method when a baseline would do is read as poor judgement, not as ability.

**Working alone until it is finished.** Industry teams share work early, in small increments, and expect code review. A three-month silent effort followed by a large reveal is a cultural mismatch.

**Perfection before delivery.** Papers are finished once; products are shipped and improved. Learning to release something imperfect but useful, with monitoring, is the core adjustment.

Two other habits transfer badly: writing code that only you can run, and treating documentation as an afterthought.

## Translating Your CV

Rewrite your academic CV completely. A European industry CV is two pages, reverse chronological, evidence-first.

- Move publications to a short "selected publications" line unless applying to research roles.
- Convert research projects into problem–action–outcome bullets: what question, what you built, what you found, what changed.
- Surface engineering: languages, frameworks, cluster or cloud experience, code you released, pipelines you maintained.
- Surface collaboration and mentoring: supervising students, leading a work package, coordinating a consortium.
- Include teaching only briefly, unless the role involves training others.
- Put a two-line summary at the top saying what kind of role you want and what you bring.

If you completed an industrial PhD or a collaboration with a company, lead with it: it answers the question employers are really asking, which is whether you can work in their environment.

## Timing the Move From Academia to Industry in AI

There is no single right moment, but some are easier than others.

**During a PhD.** Internships and industrial collaborations are the lowest-risk way to test industry work, and they often lead to offers. Where available, industrial PhD schemes — where a company employs the doctoral researcher while a university supervises — produce candidates who need almost no transition at all.

**Immediately after the PhD.** A common and well-trodden path. Employers expect limited production experience and are usually willing to invest, especially for applied scientist and domain roles.

**After one postdoc.** Still straightforward; you bring more depth and independence.

**After several postdocs.** Harder, not because of your abilities but because employers wonder about the decision and because expectations about seniority diverge. The remedy is the same: concrete engineering evidence and a clear narrative about why you are moving now.

Whatever the timing, avoid presenting the move as a retreat from academia. Frame it as a positive choice about the kind of problems you want to work on and the pace at which you want to see results.

## Building Industry Evidence While Still in Research

You do not need to leave to start building the profile that gets you hired:

- **Release a package or tool** your group uses, with tests, documentation and a licence.
- **Take ownership of infrastructure**: the pipeline that runs experiments, the data management, the cluster workflows.
- **Collaborate with a company** through a research project, and take on the part that faces their systems.
- **Supervise students** and describe it as mentoring and project management.
- **Present to non-specialists** — industry days, outreach, consortium meetings — and get comfortable with the register.
- **Contribute to open source** in your field, even modestly.

Each of these translates directly into an interview answer. The transition is rarely about acquiring a new skill set; it is about accumulating evidence that you can deliver things other people depend on.

## Interviewing After Academia

Expect interviews to focus on delivery rather than depth. Prepare to answer: how did you decide when the work was good enough; how did others use what you built; what would you do if a simple baseline matched your method; how do you handle a deadline that forces a compromise. Practise describing your research in three minutes to someone outside your field, then in one minute. Many otherwise strong academic candidates lose interviews by explaining methods at length while the interviewer waits to hear what changed as a result.

## The First Year in Industry

The first months feel disorienting for most researchers, in predictable ways.

**Pace.** Work is delivered in weeks, not years, and priorities shift. This is not superficiality; it is a different optimisation problem, where the cost of delay is real.

**Ownership.** You will own less of the intellectual agenda and more of a system. For some this is a relief; for others it takes adjustment.

**Collaboration.** Code review, shared repositories, standups and pair work are normal. Sharing unfinished work is a professional norm, not an admission of weakness.

**Evidence standards.** In some ways lower — nobody demands a theoretical guarantee — and in others higher: your model will be measured by whether it helps in production, on data that keeps changing.

**Politics.** Priorities are negotiated between teams. Learning who decides what is a real skill, and it is one academia teaches badly.

Advice that helps: spend the first weeks asking how things are done rather than proposing changes; find the person who knows where the data really comes from; deliver one small useful thing quickly; and keep a private list of things you would improve, to revisit at month six when you have credibility.

## Staying Connected to Research

Leaving academia does not mean leaving research. Many industry roles allow publication, and applied conferences welcome industrial work. You can supervise students through university collaborations, review papers, or take part in consortium projects. Some researchers keep an affiliation or teach part-time. If continuing to publish matters to you, ask about it explicitly in interviews — policies vary enormously between companies, and it is far easier to agree before joining than afterwards.

## Which Sectors Value Which Backgrounds

- **Physics, mathematics, astronomy**: forecasting, simulation, finance, energy, remote sensing, semiconductor and industrial analytics.
- **Chemistry and materials**: pharmaceuticals, chemicals, batteries, materials discovery.
- **Biology and bioinformatics**: pharma, biotech, agri-food, medical research.
- **Linguistics and computational linguistics**: language technology, search, conversational AI, localisation.
- **Engineering disciplines**: robotics, autonomous systems, manufacturing, aerospace, energy systems.
- **Economics and econometrics**: pricing, causal inference, policy analysis, risk.
- **Geoscience**: earth observation, climate analytics, agriculture, insurance.

Your domain is usually your strongest differentiator. Generic "AI researcher" positioning puts you in competition with everyone; "computational chemist who can build production pipelines" puts you in competition with almost nobody.

## Salary and Level Expectations

In most European markets, a PhD entering industry is placed at mid-level rather than junior, often with credit for research years in domain-relevant roles. Research-specific positions in large labs pay well but are scarce; applied roles in industry pay solidly and are plentiful. Compare total packages, including pension, holiday and stability, rather than headline salaries — and be wary of accepting a junior title "to get in", which can take two years to correct.

## A Final Word

The researchers who transition best are not the ones who abandon their academic identity but the ones who bring its best parts: curiosity, rigour and the willingness to keep asking whether a result is real. Add the industrial habits — shipping small, sharing early, valuing simplicity — and you become the colleague every applied team wants: someone who can tell the difference between a model that performs and a model that merely appears to.

## Explaining Why You Are Leaving

Interviewers will ask, and the answer matters more than candidates expect. Avoid framing the move as an escape from a broken system, however justified that feeling may be: it reads as unresolved rather than considered. Equally, avoid pretending you always planned an industrial career if you did not.

What works is specific and forward-looking: you want your work to be used rather than cited; you enjoyed the applied part of your project most; you want to build systems that run rather than experiments that end; you want a team working on one problem together; you want a different pace, or more stability, or a location you can settle in. Any of these is a perfectly good reason, and stating it plainly signals self-knowledge — which is what the question is really testing.

## Real example

### The postdoc who built one thing that ran

A computational physics postdoc decided to leave academia after six years and three publications in simulation methods. Her first twenty applications to data science roles produced two interviews and no offers. Feedback mentioned "limited production experience".

She spent three months doing one thing: taking the simulation analysis code her group used, turning it into a documented package with tests and a small command-line tool, and deploying a version that ran nightly on a cloud instance, with monitoring and alerts when a run failed. She wrote a short article explaining the design decisions.

Her next applications went differently. In interviews she could describe software other people depended on, an operational failure she had diagnosed, and the trade-offs she had made. She was hired as a data scientist at an energy company, where her simulation background turned out to be directly relevant to forecasting problems — but it was the operational story that opened the door.

## Key Takeaways

- Employers value independence, depth, rigour and learning speed — not titles or publication counts.
- The habits to unlearn are novelty-seeking, working alone until finished, and perfecting before shipping.
- Rewrite your CV around problems, actions and outcomes, and surface engineering evidence.
- One piece of software that other people run is worth more than three more papers in an application.
- Domain PhDs are often more valuable in industry than generic ML credentials.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: PhD candidate) Is a PhD necessary for AI jobs in industry?
No. It is an advantage for research-oriented and domain-heavy roles, and largely neutral for engineering roles, where production experience matters more.

### (Scenario: postdoc considering leaving) Will I be overqualified for applied roles?
Rarely. The risk is being seen as impractical, which is addressed by showing engineering and delivery evidence, not by hiding your background.

### (Scenario: researcher worried about pay) Will I take a pay cut?
Usually the opposite: industry salaries for experienced researchers are typically above postdoctoral pay in most European countries.

### (Scenario: academic applying widely) Should I apply to research scientist roles only?
No. Applied scientist, ML engineer, data scientist and domain specialist roles are far more numerous and often more satisfying than expected.
