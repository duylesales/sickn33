Few documents generate as much wasted effort as the cover letter. Candidates agonise over them, many hiring managers skim them, and half of European job postings now mark them optional. Yet applications that include a good one are noticeably more likely to progress, especially for people without an obvious match on paper. This guide explains when cover letters for AI jobs actually change outcomes, what the reader is looking for, and how to write one in forty minutes that does not sound like everyone else's.

## When Cover Letters for AI Jobs Matter Most

They matter most when your CV raises a question the reader cannot answer alone:

- You are changing field, from physics, biology, economics or software engineering.
- You are a graduate whose experience is coursework and projects.
- You are applying from another country and the employer must consider relocation or permits.
- You have a gap, a short tenure, or a non-linear path.
- You meet part of the requirements and want to argue that the rest is learnable.
- The role is at a small organisation where a human reads every application.

They matter least when you are an exact match applying to a large employer with a structured process, where a screener checks boxes. Even then a short, specific letter costs little.

The practical rule: write one when you have something to say that the CV cannot say. Do not write one to repeat the CV in prose.

## What the Reader Is Actually Looking For

Hiring managers reading applications for AI roles are trying to answer three questions quickly:

1. **Does this person understand what we do?** Not the marketing description — the actual problem.
2. **Can they do the work, or learn it fast?** Evidence, not adjectives.
3. **Why us, and why now?** A reason that is true, specific and not flattery.

Everything in your letter should serve one of those questions. Anything that does not is filler, and filler is what makes letters interchangeable.

## A Structure That Works

**Opening (two or three sentences).** Say which role you are applying for and one concrete, true reason this specific role interests you. Reference something real: the product, the domain, a technical challenge implied by the posting.

**Evidence (one or two paragraphs).** Pick the two requirements that matter most and show you have met something similar. Name the problem, what you did and the outcome. One detailed example beats five claims.

**The gap (one short paragraph, if relevant).** Address the obvious question directly: the missing technology, the career change, the language. Say what you have that is close, and how you have closed similar gaps before. Do not apologise.

**Practicalities (one or two sentences, if relevant).** Availability, location, work authorisation, willingness to travel. Removing uncertainty helps the reader.

**Close (one sentence).** Say you would welcome a conversation. Nothing more.

Total: under 400 words, on one page, in the language of the vacancy.

## The Research That Makes a Letter Specific

Twenty minutes of preparation separates a letter that works from one that does not. A workable routine:

**Read the vacancy twice.** The first read tells you what the role is. The second tells you what the team is worried about. Requirements repeated in different words, or described with unusual detail, indicate real pain. A posting that mentions "working with domain experts" three times is telling you that previous hires did not.

**Look at what the organisation actually makes.** Their product pages, documentation, engineering blog, open source repositories or published papers. Five minutes here produces the one concrete sentence that makes you sound like someone who wants this job rather than a job.

**Check who you would work with.** Public profiles of the team tell you whether it is a new function or an established one, whether backgrounds are academic or industrial, and what vocabulary they use.

**Find the constraint.** Regulated sector? Safety-critical? Small data? Legacy systems? Multilingual users? Naming the constraint correctly signals that you have thought about the work, and it is the single highest-value sentence in most letters.

## Phrases to Avoid, and What to Write Instead

Certain sentences appear in most letters and therefore carry no information.

- *"I am passionate about artificial intelligence."* Everyone applying is. Replace with what you actually built and why it interested you.
- *"I am a fast learner."* Unprovable. Replace with an instance: a technology you picked up under deadline and what you produced with it.
- *"I would be a great fit for your dynamic team."* Says nothing about you or them. Replace with the specific problem you want to work on.
- *"As you can see from my CV…"* Wasted words; the reader has the CV.
- *"I am excited about the opportunity to leverage cutting-edge technologies."* Buzzword density without content.
- *"Your company is a leader in innovation."* Flattery that applies to any employer.

The replacement principle is simple: if a sentence could appear in a letter to any other company, delete it or make it specific. A letter of 250 specific words beats one of 500 general ones, and it takes less of the reader's time — which is itself a small courtesy that registers.

## Adapting the Letter to European Conventions

Expectations differ across Europe, and matching local convention costs nothing.

In the **Netherlands** and **Scandinavia**, letters are short, direct and factual. Understatement is normal; strong self-promotion can read as inflated. In **Germany** and **Austria**, applications are traditionally more formal, with structured layout and attention to certificates and qualifications, though technology employers have relaxed this considerably. In **France**, the *lettre de motivation* remains a genuine expectation for many roles and is read carefully. In **Southern Europe**, warmer and slightly more personal phrasing is common. In **Ireland** and international teams anywhere, English-language convention close to the UK or US style applies.

Two rules travel everywhere: do not include a photo, date of birth, marital status or other personal details unless local practice clearly expects it, and do write in the language of the posting when you can. Employers subject to equal treatment obligations increasingly prefer applications without personal characteristics, and several organisations have moved to structured forms for this reason.

If the employer uses an application form with free-text fields rather than a letter upload, treat the fields with the same discipline: specific, short, evidenced.

## Explaining a Career Change or a Gap

Two situations cause the most anxiety, and both are handled the same way: state the fact plainly, then move to evidence.

**Career change.** Say what you did, why you are moving and what transfers. A biologist moving into machine learning has experimental design, statistics and comfort with messy measurement; a software engineer has systems thinking and delivery discipline; a teacher has explanation skills that matter more in AI work than most job posts admit. Name the transfer explicitly rather than hoping the reader infers it, then point to something you built that proves you have begun.

**Gaps.** Illness, caring responsibilities, redundancy, relocation, study or a period of searching are all ordinary. One sentence is enough: what happened, that it is resolved, and that you are available now. Long explanations invite the scrutiny you are trying to avoid, and no reasonable employer in Europe treats a year out as disqualifying.

What does damage an application is an unexplained absence combined with vagueness, because the reader fills the silence with a worse story than the truth.

## After You Send It

A letter is part of a process, not the end of one. A few habits improve outcomes:

**Keep a record.** Role, company, date, which version you sent, contact name. After twenty applications this is the difference between a search you control and one that controls you.

**Follow up once.** If the posting gives no timeline and two weeks pass, a short polite message to the recruiter or hiring manager is reasonable. Once, not repeatedly.

**Expect silence sometimes.** Many European employers are improving at responding, but volume, hiring freezes and internal candidates all produce silence that has nothing to do with your letter.

**Reuse what worked.** When a letter leads to an interview, note which paragraph the interviewer mentioned. Over a search you will learn which of your examples land.

**Ask for feedback after rejection.** Some employers give it, most do not, and the ones that do are worth remembering as places to apply again.

## Using the Vacancy Text as Your Outline

The fastest way to write a specific letter is to let the posting supply the structure. Copy the vacancy into a document, highlight the three requirements that appear most important, and write one sentence of evidence under each. That raw material becomes the middle of your letter, and it guarantees you are answering the questions the employer asked rather than the ones you wish they had.

This also exposes mismatches early. If you cannot write an honest sentence under two of the three requirements, you have learned something useful before spending an hour: either apply anyway with a clear statement of what you would need to learn, or spend the hour on a better-matched role.

Do not, however, mirror the posting's exact phrasing throughout. Letters that echo the vacancy word for word read as mechanical, and experienced readers notice immediately. Use the posting to decide what to write about, then write in your own vocabulary.

## How Long Should It Take?

If a letter takes two hours you will stop writing them, and consistency matters more than polish. A realistic budget is forty minutes: twenty reading the company and the vacancy, fifteen writing, five cutting.

The cutting pass is the one people skip and the one that does the most work. Read the draft and delete every sentence that could appear in a letter to a different employer. Delete adjectives that describe you rather than what you did. Delete the paragraph explaining how excited you are. What remains is usually shorter, sharper and more convincing than what you started with.

If you are applying to many roles, batch the process: research five companies in one sitting, write five openings, then write the evidence paragraphs. The evidence paragraphs draw on the same two or three projects each time, so the marginal cost per application drops quickly once you have written the first few well.

One last calibration. A cover letter rarely wins a job on its own; what it does is get a human to read your CV with attention rather than scanning it. That is a modest effect, and across twenty applications it is the difference between a search that stalls and one that moves.

## Real example

### The paragraph that got the interview

A master's graduate in computational physics applied for a machine learning engineer role at a European manufacturing company. Her CV showed simulations, no industry experience and no listed exposure to the company's stack.

Her first letter said she was "passionate about applying AI to real-world problems" and "a fast learner". She sent thirty of them and got two responses.

She rewrote the approach. For each application she spent twenty minutes reading what the company actually built, then wrote one paragraph connecting her real work to their real problem. For the manufacturing company she wrote that her thesis involved detecting anomalies in sensor data from an experimental rig where labels were scarce and false alarms were expensive, that she had built the data pipeline herself because nobody else would, and that she assumed their predictive maintenance problem had the same shape — few labelled failures and a high cost of crying wolf.

The hiring manager told her later that the phrase "few labelled failures and a high cost of crying wolf" was why she was invited. It showed she understood the problem before she arrived. She sent eleven applications in the new style and received five interviews.

## Key Takeaways

- Write a cover letter when your CV leaves a question unanswered; skip it when you are an obvious match and nobody will read it.
- Answer three questions: do you understand the work, can you do it, why here.
- One specific example outperforms a list of claims.
- Address gaps directly and briefly, without apology.
- Keep it under 400 words, in the language of the posting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: graduate with no industry experience) What do I write about if I have only student projects?
Write about a project as if it were work: the problem, your decisions, what failed and what you learned. Specificity matters more than the setting.

### (Scenario: applicant unsure about language) Should I write in English or the local language?
Write in the language of the vacancy. If the posting is in the local language and you are not fluent, English is acceptable but say how you are addressing the language.

### (Scenario: candidate using AI tools) Can I use a language model to write it?
Use it to edit and tighten. Letters generated wholesale read as generic, and the specificity that persuades has to come from you.

### (Scenario: applicant applying at volume) Should I reuse one letter?
Reuse the structure, never the specifics. The opening paragraph and the evidence must be written for the role.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we ask for cover letters?
Ask only if you will read them, and say what you want to know. Unread requirements deter good candidates for nothing.
