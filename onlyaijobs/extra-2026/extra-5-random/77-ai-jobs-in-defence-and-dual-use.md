European defence spending has risen substantially, and with it demand for software and data specialists. For candidates, this raises questions that do not arise in other sectors: what the work actually involves, whether you are eligible, what restrictions apply to your career afterwards, and whether you are comfortable with the purpose. AI jobs in defence and dual-use technology deserve a clear-eyed description rather than either enthusiasm or avoidance.

## AI Jobs in Defence and Dual-Use Technology: What the Work Involves

Most data roles in this sector are further from the sharp end than the phrase suggests.

**Logistics and sustainment.** Predicting maintenance needs for vehicles, aircraft and vessels, spare parts forecasting, and supply chain management across long and specialised chains. A large share of defence data work.

**Simulation and training.** Modelling scenarios for training and evaluation, a substantial industry in its own right.

**Signal and sensor processing.** Analysis of radar, sonar, radio frequency and imagery data, often mathematically demanding and closely related to civil applications.

**Earth observation and geospatial intelligence.** Satellite and aerial imagery analysis, heavily overlapping with civil environmental and mapping work.

**Cybersecurity.** Network defence, threat detection and secure systems engineering.

**Engineering and manufacturing.** The same industrial analytics found in aerospace and automotive, applied to defence production.

**Command and decision support.** Systems presenting information to human decision-makers, where European policy discussion has emphasised meaningful human control.

**Research programmes.** Funded through national programmes and European instruments, often conducted with universities and research institutes.

## Eligibility, Clearance and What It Involves

Many roles require national security clearance, and this determines who can apply before any assessment of skill.

Clearance typically requires citizenship of the country concerned, or in some cases of an allied country with residency, and processes differ substantially between member states. Vetting examines personal history, financial position, foreign connections and associations, usually with interviews and sometimes with references from people who know you well. It takes months.

Dual nationality and family abroad are not disqualifying in most European systems, but they are examined, and the process is more involved.

Some employers sponsor clearance for a new hire; others require it already held, which makes existing clearance a valuable asset in this market.

Beyond formal clearance, facilities are controlled: restrictions on devices, no working from home for classified work, and limits on what you may discuss or publish. Candidates who expect to write about their work publicly should note that this will largely not be possible.

## Dual-Use and the Blurred Boundary

A great deal of technology in this area is dual-use: developed for civil purposes and applicable to defence, or the reverse. Satellite imagery, navigation, secure communications, drones, simulation, materials and cybersecurity all sit in this category.

For candidates this has two implications.

First, you may encounter defence applications without working for a defence company. Earth observation firms sell to agriculture and to defence ministries. Drone manufacturers serve inspection and security markets. Cybersecurity vendors protect hospitals and military networks. If the distinction matters to you, ask about the customer base rather than assuming from the sector label.

Second, dual-use technology is subject to export controls. The EU maintains a regime governing the export of dual-use items, including certain software and technology, and national authorities implement it. Transferring controlled technology to a person in a third country — including by giving them access to technical data — can constitute an export requiring authorisation.

The practical consequence for engineers is real: your employer may restrict who can access certain repositories, where you may travel with a laptop, and which colleagues in which countries may be involved in a project.

Compliance teams exist to manage this, and the advice is to follow their guidance precisely rather than making judgement calls.

## The Ethical Questions, Stated Plainly

People hold a range of defensible positions here, and this section does not argue for one. It sets out the questions worth answering for yourself before you accept a role.

**What does your work touch?** There is a considerable difference between predicting when a transport vehicle needs maintenance and developing systems involved in the application of force. Ask specifically, and ask about indirect contribution.

**Where do you draw the line?** Some engineers work on anything defensive but not offensive. Some exclude autonomous weapons entirely. Some exclude surveillance applications. Some see national defence as legitimate work and draw no internal lines. Deciding in advance is easier than deciding under pressure on a project.

**What is the organisation's position?** European policy discussion has emphasised meaningful human control over the use of force, and responsible organisations have internal review processes. Ask whether one exists and what it has declined.

**What about export customers?** Technology developed for a European customer may be sold elsewhere. Ask about the export policy.

**Can you raise a concern?** Ask what happens if an engineer objects to an application. The answer tells you whether the review process is real.

An organisation that engages seriously with these questions is a different proposition from one that treats them as naive, and the difference is visible in an interview.

## Regulation and the European Policy Context

The regulatory picture here differs from civil AI in an important respect.

The EU AI Act does not apply to AI systems placed on the market or used exclusively for military, defence or national security purposes. This is a significant carve-out, and it means the framework candidates may be familiar with from civil work does not govern defence applications in the same way.

That does not mean an absence of constraint. International humanitarian law applies to the conduct of armed conflict regardless of the technology used. National law and ministerial policy govern procurement and use. Several European states have articulated positions on human control over weapons systems, and the topic is the subject of ongoing international discussion.

Organisations in the sector also operate under quality and safety regimes comparable to aerospace, with the assurance culture that implies: defined scope, documented evidence, and human oversight designed into systems rather than added afterwards.

European funding instruments for defence research carry their own ethical review requirements for funded projects.

For an engineer, the practical implication is that the governance you encounter will be organisational and sectoral rather than the AI Act framework, and understanding which rules actually apply to your work is part of professional competence here.

## How the Hiring Process Differs

Candidates should expect a process unlike civil technology hiring.

**Timelines are long.** Between application and start date, six to twelve months is not unusual where clearance is required. Employers know this and usually maintain contact, but it affects planning and makes this a poor choice if you need to move quickly.

**Eligibility is checked first.** Nationality, residency history and sometimes existing clearance are screened before technical assessment, which can feel impersonal and is a practical necessity.

**Interviews may be constrained.** Interviewers cannot always describe the work in detail until you are cleared, which makes it harder to assess the role. Ask what they can tell you and what will become clear later.

**References and background checks are thorough**, as described in the guidance on that subject, and go further than in most sectors.

**Portfolios matter less.** Public code and writing carry less weight here because much of the workforce cannot produce them. Expect assessment through conversation and structured technical exercises instead.

**Sites are physical.** Classified work happens on site, so remote arrangements are limited and location matters.

For candidates from civil technology, the formality can be off-putting. It reflects genuine constraints rather than bureaucratic preference, and organisations that explain the reasons are generally the better employers.

## Who Hires Across Europe

**Prime contractors.** Large European defence and aerospace groups with substantial software and data organisations, present in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden, the UK and the Netherlands.

**Specialist suppliers.** Companies focused on sensors, communications, simulation, electronic systems and secure computing, distributed across the continent.

**Cybersecurity firms** serving government and critical infrastructure customers.

**Space and earth observation companies**, many dual-use by nature.

**Defence ministries and armed forces**, employing civilian analysts, data scientists and engineers directly.

**National research institutes** with defence programmes, which conduct applied research in partnership with industry and universities.

**European agencies and joint programmes**, including bodies coordinating research and procurement.

**Startups and scale-ups.** A growing category in European defence technology, with more informal cultures and funding risk attached.

Conditions at established employers are generally stable, with structured engineering cultures, collective agreements in several countries, and good training provision. Sites are frequently outside city centres, near existing industrial or military facilities, which affects commuting.

Demand has risen with European defence budgets, and software and data skills are among the areas employers most frequently report difficulty filling.

## What the Work Does to Your Career

Several practical effects are worth weighing before committing.

**Your portfolio stops growing publicly.** You will not be able to describe projects in detail, publish code, write about your work or speak at conferences about what you build. Over several years this makes an external job search harder, because your evidence is a description rather than an artefact.

**Your network narrows.** Cleared environments are smaller worlds, though the community within them is well connected.

**Clearance is an asset.** Holding it makes you considerably more employable within the sector, and it is one of the few credentials in technology with genuine scarcity value.

**Obligations may continue.** Confidentiality persists after employment ends, and in some countries cleared individuals carry reporting obligations regarding foreign contact.

**Skills transfer.** The underlying work — time series, sensing, optimisation, simulation, cybersecurity, reliability — maps directly onto civil sectors, and people move between defence and aerospace, energy or industrial analytics without difficulty.

The net effect is that a period in this sector is neither a trap nor a detour. It is a choice that trades public visibility for interesting technical work in a stable environment, and candidates who understand that trade in advance rarely regret it.

## Interviewing Well in This Sector

Employers here value rigour, discretion and the ability to work within constraint.

Expect technical questions with the flavour of aerospace: how would you validate a system when the operating conditions are hard to reproduce; how would you build a prediction with very few failure examples; how would you define the boundary of where your system is valid; how would you present uncertainty to someone making a consequential decision.

Expect behavioural questions about handling confidential information, working under documented processes, and responding when you disagree with a technical direction.

Preparation that helps: familiarity with reliability engineering, simulation, signal processing or geospatial analysis depending on the role; comfort with assurance vocabulary; and the ability to discuss your previous work at a level of abstraction that respects any confidentiality you already carry, which is itself a demonstrated skill.

Be honest about your position on the ethical questions if asked. Interviewers in this sector generally prefer a candidate with a clear view to one who has not considered it.

## Real example

### The engineer who asked the right question first

A machine learning engineer in Europe was approached about a role at a defence contractor working on maintenance prediction for military vehicles. The technical work was appealing and closely resembled industrial reliability engineering.

Rather than deciding in the abstract, he asked three specific questions during the process. What systems would his work touch, directly and indirectly? Did the company have an internal ethics review, and could they describe something it had stopped? What would he be permitted to say publicly about his employment and his work?

The answers were concrete: his work would sit within logistics and sustainment, with no involvement in targeting or weapons systems; there was a review process and the interviewer described a case where a proposed application had been declined; and he could state his employer and describe his field in general terms but not his projects.

He accepted. Two years later he said the questions had mattered less for the answers than for what asking them revealed about how seriously the organisation took the subject.

## Key Takeaways

- Most data roles in defence are in logistics, simulation, sensing, cybersecurity and engineering rather than weapons systems.
- Clearance requirements determine eligibility and add months to hiring timelines.
- Export controls and confidentiality restrict what you may publish and where knowledge may travel.
- Ethical questions are legitimate to raise in interviews, and the answers are informative.
- The work is technically substantial and the sector is currently expanding across Europe.
