Every European team hiring AI talent has had the same conversation: the senior engineers we want are expensive, scarce and already employed, and our open vacancy is six months old. Hiring junior AI talent is the obvious alternative and is often dismissed too quickly, usually because a previous attempt failed. It failed for reasons that are predictable and avoidable. This playbook covers when junior hiring works, how to assess people without a track record, and what has to exist for the investment to pay back.

## When Hiring Junior AI Talent Makes Sense

It works when three conditions hold.

**Someone can supervise.** At least one experienced person with capacity to review work, answer questions and set direction. Not necessarily a machine learning specialist — a strong software engineer or analyst with judgement is often enough.

**There is suitable work.** Defined, valuable tasks that do not require years of context: data pipelines, evaluation, monitoring, analysis, tooling, documentation. Every team has a backlog of these.

**You intend to keep them.** A junior hire is roughly break-even at a year and valuable from then on. Organisations with high turnover never reach the payoff.

It does not work when the team is a single overloaded person, when the only work available is ambiguous and strategic, or when the vacancy is a disguised attempt to get senior output at a junior salary.

## Assessing Candidates Without a Track Record

You cannot evaluate a junior on past delivery, so evaluate on trajectory, reasoning and evidence of building things.

**Depth on one thing.** Ask about the most substantial project they have done and go three questions deep. Candidates who built something real keep producing reasons; candidates who followed a tutorial do not.

**Reasoning over recall.** Give a small, realistic problem and discuss the approach. You are looking for someone who asks about the data, states assumptions and notices what could go wrong.

**Evidence of self-direction.** Something built outside coursework, a contribution, a thesis taken further than required, a competition entry. It matters less what it is than that they finished it.

**Fundamentals.** SQL, basic statistics, code that runs. Test these directly and briefly.

**Coachability.** Give a piece of feedback during the interview and see what happens. This predicts the first year better than almost anything else.

Do not test for the technologies you use. A capable junior learns your stack in weeks; judgement takes years.

## Designing the First Year

The difference between a junior hire who becomes valuable and one who drifts is almost entirely in how the first year is structured.

**Months one to three: a bounded, real project.** Something with a clear definition of done, a genuine user and no dependency on years of context. Monitoring, evaluation, a data quality tool, an internal dashboard, a pipeline improvement. The goal is a completed piece of work they can point to, and a tour of your systems as a side effect.

**Months four to six: ownership of something small.** A component, a recurring report, a scheduled job — something that breaks occasionally and teaches them what operating software means.

**Months seven to twelve: a project with ambiguity.** A problem where the approach is not obvious and they must propose one. Review the proposal rather than the code.

Throughout: pair them on other people's work regularly, include them in design discussions from day one even when they say nothing, and give them one presentation to a non-technical audience.

Two things to avoid: a first project that depends on a colleague who is unavailable, and a first project so open-ended that nobody can say when it is finished.

## Supervision That Actually Happens

Almost every failed junior hire traces back to supervision that was promised and then absorbed by other priorities.

Make it explicit and defensible. A named supervisor, a fixed weekly slot in the calendar, and an understanding with the supervisor's manager that this time is part of their objectives rather than an addition to them. Four hours a week for the first three months, tapering after that, is a realistic figure.

Structure the time. A weekly review of work in progress, a code review habit with actual comments rather than approvals, and an open channel for short questions. Juniors waste enormous amounts of time being stuck on things a colleague would resolve in two minutes, and the main job of early supervision is to make asking cheap.

Set the norm explicitly: "If you are stuck for more than thirty minutes, ask." Without that instruction, conscientious juniors will spend a day rather than appear incapable.

Rotate the reviewer occasionally so the junior sees more than one way of working and does not become dependent on a single person.

And review the arrangement at three months. If the supervision is not happening, that is a management problem to fix, not evidence that the hire was a mistake.

## Where to Find Junior Candidates in Europe

The channels differ from senior hiring, and the most effective ones are local.

**Universities and universities of applied sciences.** Thesis projects and graduation internships are the strongest pipeline available to most employers. A student who spends five or six months on a real problem in your organisation arrives fully informed, and you have seen them work. Several European countries have well-established structures for this, and departments are usually keen to place students with genuine industrial problems.

**Internships and traineeships.** Paid, structured, and an effective low-risk trial for both sides. Treat them as recruitment rather than as cheap labour, and give interns real work.

**Applied science and vocational programmes.** Often overlooked in favour of research universities, and frequently a better fit for engineering-oriented roles, because these students have more practical project experience.

**Career changers and bootcamp graduates.** Variable, but the strong ones bring prior professional experience from other fields and are often more mature about stakeholders and deadlines than fresh graduates.

**Local meetups and student associations.** Cheap, effective and largely uncontested by larger employers.

Regional presence matters here more than national advertising. Students apply close to where they study and where they have connections.

## Writing a Vacancy Juniors Will Apply To

Junior vacancies are frequently written as senior vacancies with the salary reduced, which produces either no applications or the wrong ones.

**Ask for what is genuinely required.** A junior role demanding three years of experience with five specific technologies excludes exactly the people you intend to hire. List two or three things that actually matter and say the rest can be learned.

**Say what they will learn and from whom.** Naming the supervisor's background and the first project converts a generic posting into a concrete opportunity.

**Describe the work honestly, including the unglamorous parts.** Candidates who accept an inflated description leave when they discover the reality; candidates who accept an honest one stay.

**Publish the salary range.** Junior candidates in particular have little information about market rates, and transparency attracts applications. Member states are implementing pay transparency requirements under Directive (EU) 2023/970 in any case.

**Encourage applications from people who do not meet everything.** Evidence across many organisations suggests that candidates from under-represented groups self-select out of vacancies where they do not meet every criterion, and a sentence addressing this measurably widens the pool.

**Keep the process short.** Juniors apply widely and accept early. A six-week process loses good people to faster employers.

## Assessing Fairly, and Defensibly

Junior hiring is where unstructured assessment does the most damage, because there is no track record to anchor on and impressions fill the gap.

Use the same structure for every candidate: the same exercise, the same questions, the same criteria written down before you start. Beyond fairness, it is what makes comparison possible when candidates have wildly different backgrounds.

Be careful what you are actually measuring. A take-home assignment of eight hours tests available free time, which correlates with caring responsibilities, part-time work and financial circumstances rather than with ability. Keep practical exercises short and offer a live alternative.

Watch for proxies you did not intend. Enthusiasm for side projects correlates with having evenings free. Confidence in a second language is not competence. A prestigious university is a weak predictor compared with what someone actually built.

Equal treatment obligations across the EU apply to recruitment, and structured, criteria-based assessment is both fairer and easier to evidence if questioned.

Finally, involve more than one assessor and have them write independently before discussing. For candidates with no track record, a single person's impression is a thin basis for a decision that will shape someone's career.

## Retention: Keeping the Investment

A junior hire becomes genuinely valuable in year two, which is exactly when the market starts contacting them. Three things keep people.

**Visible growth.** Increasing scope, harder problems, and an explicit conversation about what the next level requires. People leave when progression is invisible, more often than when it is slow.

**Pay that tracks capability.** A junior salary that remains a junior salary at twenty months is an invitation to leave, and it is usually cheaper to adjust than to rehire. Review after the first year deliberately rather than waiting for a resignation.

**Interesting work and credit for it.** Let them present their own work. Put their name on it. Junior people who are visible to the wider organisation stay longer, and it costs nothing.

Also worth attention: a colleague at a similar stage makes an enormous difference. Hiring two juniors rather than one is disproportionately more effective, because they support each other, compare notes and normalise asking questions.

Accept that some will leave regardless, and that this is not a failure. A company known for developing good junior engineers attracts more of them, and people who leave well often return later or recommend others.

## Mistakes That Waste the Investment

A short list of what goes wrong, drawn from patterns that recur across European teams.

**Hiring a junior to do senior work.** The vacancy says junior, the expectations are of an experienced engineer, and the hire is judged against a standard nobody stated. This is the most common failure and it is entirely self-inflicted.

**No defined first project.** The new person spends two months asking what to do and concludes they are not needed.

**Access delays.** Starting without accounts, data access or a working environment wastes the fortnight when motivation is highest. Arrange it before the start date.

**Isolation.** A junior placed in a business unit far from any technical colleague, with a manager who cannot review their work. This is the second most common failure.

**Only leftover tasks.** Work nobody else wants, with no path to anything better, produces a resignation at twelve months.

**No feedback until the review.** Small corrections given weekly are easy; six months of accumulated concerns delivered at once is demoralising and usually unfair.

**Treating the internship as free labour.** Interns talk to each other and to their departments. A reputation for wasting students' time closes a channel that takes years to reopen.

## Real example

### The graduate hire that paid for itself in nine months

A logistics company in the Netherlands had been trying to hire a senior machine learning engineer for seven months. The operations director eventually asked the data team lead what would happen if they hired two graduates instead.

The lead resisted, on the grounds that he had no time to supervise. They compromised: one graduate, a defined first project, and four hours a week of his time protected in the calendar.

The graduate spent three months on monitoring and evaluation for the existing forecasting system — work the team had postponed for a year. She found two data quality problems that had been silently degrading forecasts, and built the dashboard that let the team detect the next one within a day rather than a quarter.

By month nine she was running her own small project. The team lead's assessment was that the four hours a week had been the cheapest engineering capacity he had ever bought, and that the senior vacancy would probably have stayed open anyway.

## Key Takeaways

- Junior hiring works when there is a supervisor, suitable work and an intention to retain.
- Assess trajectory, reasoning, self-direction and coachability, not technology familiarity.
- Protect the supervisor's time explicitly; goodwill is not a plan.
- Give a defined, valuable first project rather than leftovers.
- The payback period is around a year, so treat it as an investment, not a discount.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: team with no senior ML engineer) Can we hire a junior anyway?
Only with a credible alternative source of supervision — an experienced software engineer, an external mentor or a consultancy arrangement. A junior alone will struggle and leave.

### (Scenario: employer worried about retention) Will they leave after two years?
Some will. Many stay longer when growth is visible. Budget for the possibility rather than avoiding the hire because of it.

### (Scenario: employer comparing sources) Internships or direct hires?
Internships and thesis projects are excellent, low-risk pipelines, particularly near universities. Both work; run them together if you can.

### (Scenario: employer with a tight budget) Are juniors cheap?
Cheaper in salary, not free overall, because supervision has a cost. The saving is real but smaller than the salary difference suggests.

### (Scenario: employer with one opening) One senior or two juniors?
If you have supervision capacity, two juniors often build more durable capability. If you do not, one senior is the safer choice.
