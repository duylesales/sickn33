Every organisation that grows a data function eventually faces the same argument. The business units want their own analysts because the central team is slow; the central team wants everyone together because the embedded people are duplicating work and doing it inconsistently. Both are right. Choosing between centralised or embedded data teams is less about finding the correct model than about knowing which problems you are choosing to have.

## Centralised or Embedded Data Teams: The Three Models

**Centralised.** All data and AI people report into one function, which takes requests from the business. Common as a first structure and in smaller organisations.

**Embedded.** Data people sit inside business units, reporting to those units, working on their problems full time.

**Hybrid, often called hub and spoke.** A central team owns platform, standards, tooling and scarce specialisms, while practitioners sit in business units. The central function provides the technical home; the units provide the problems.

Most European organisations of any size end up at some version of the third, having tried one of the first two and encountered its limits.

## What Each Model Does Well and Badly

**Centralised works well for** consistency, technical standards, career development, sharing knowledge, avoiding duplication, and managing scarce specialists. It also makes governance straightforward, which matters as obligations under the EU AI Act grow.

**Centralised fails at** domain understanding and responsiveness. The team becomes a queue, business units resent the wait, and the analysts never develop deep knowledge of any one area because they rotate across requests.

**Embedded works well for** domain depth, speed, relevance and relationships. Analysts understand the business, and the work is used because it answers real questions.

**Embedded fails at** technical quality, consistency and career development. Isolated practitioners have nobody to review their work, definitions diverge between units, and the same problem is solved three times differently. People leave because they cannot grow.

**Hybrid attempts both** and succeeds when the central function is genuinely useful rather than a control layer. It fails when the hub becomes a bureaucracy the spokes route around.

## How Size Changes the Answer

The right structure depends heavily on how many people you have, and most disagreements about operating models are really disagreements about scale.

**One to three people.** Centralised by necessity. The priority is finding work worth doing and getting something into production. Formal structure is irrelevant; what matters is a sponsor and a defined problem.

**Four to eight.** Still centralised, with individuals beginning to specialise by domain. Introduce shared standards now, while it is easy, rather than later when there are conflicting habits to reconcile.

**Nine to twenty.** The pressure to embed becomes real, because the central queue is visible and business units are frustrated. This is where a hybrid usually begins: a platform and standards core, with practitioners aligned to domains.

**Twenty to sixty.** Hybrid with formal structure. A defined platform team, domain-aligned groups, specialists in scarce disciplines, and a community that holds standards across all of them.

**Beyond sixty.** Multiple layers, with platform, governance and domain groups, and a real risk of bureaucracy. The question shifts from where people sit to whether the internal interfaces work.

The other variable is maturity. An organisation with no data platform should not distribute its engineers, because building the foundation requires concentration. One with a functioning platform can afford to spread the analytical work across the business.

## What the Hub Must Own

A hybrid model only works when the central function provides things practitioners want, rather than approvals they must obtain.

The hub should own: the data platform and the tooling everyone uses; the core data models and the definitions of the metrics the organisation runs on; technical standards, code review practice and the deployment path; scarce specialisms too rare to distribute, such as optimisation, causal inference or machine learning engineering; the governance framework, including documentation, model inventory and the obligations attached to regulated systems; and the technical career framework, levelling and development.

The hub should not own the business problems. Deciding what to build belongs with the people accountable for the outcome.

The test of whether a hub is working is simple: do embedded practitioners use it voluntarily? If they build their own pipelines because the platform is painful, or maintain their own definitions because the central ones do not fit, the hub is failing and adding process will not fix it.

One useful discipline is for the hub to treat practitioners as customers, with the responsiveness that implies. Central teams that measure how long a colleague waits for access, or how long a deployment takes, tend to be the ones people use.

## What Embedded People Need

Distributing practitioners into business units solves relevance and creates predictable problems that must be addressed deliberately.

**A technical home.** A weekly technical review, a shared channel, code review by a peer who understands the work, and attendance at the data function's planning. Without this, embedded analysts become isolated and their work quality drifts.

**A dotted reporting line.** Someone technical involved in their objectives, their development and their performance assessment. A business manager who cannot evaluate the work cannot advocate for it or develop the person.

**Shared standards and tooling.** So that moving between units is possible and work is transferable.

**Career progression owned centrally.** This is the most common cause of departures among embedded practitioners: their manager cannot promote them into a technical level because the unit has no such ladder.

**Protection from becoming a reporting service.** Business units under pressure will use a data person to produce spreadsheets. Someone must defend the boundary.

**Rotation, occasionally.** Moving between units every few years prevents stagnation and spreads knowledge.

Organisations that embed without providing these lose their embedded people within two years, usually to employers with a proper technical function, and then conclude that embedding does not work.

## Governance Under the New Obligations

Regulatory developments have made the operating model question more consequential than it used to be.

Under the EU AI Act, organisations deploying or providing AI systems carry obligations that depend on risk classification: documentation, data governance, human oversight, logging, monitoring and, for providers of high-risk systems, conformity assessment. Sectoral frameworks in finance, health and critical infrastructure add their own.

These obligations need an owner. In a fully embedded model, where each business unit builds its own systems, it is entirely possible for an organisation not to know how many AI systems it operates, which is an uncomfortable position when a regulator asks.

The practical responses used by European organisations: a central inventory of AI systems that every unit must register in; a classification process determining which obligations apply; templates and tooling that make documentation the default rather than an extra task; a review step before deployment for anything in a regulated category; and a named accountable owner per system.

This does not require centralising the people. It requires centralising the register, the standards and the review, while the work happens where the domain knowledge is.

Organisations that establish this early find it inexpensive. Those that wait discover twenty undocumented systems and a deadline.

## Common Failure Patterns

Several recurring structures produce predictable outcomes.

**The service desk.** A central team receiving tickets from the business, with no involvement in defining problems. Produces high output, low impact and rapid turnover, because nobody joins a data team to fill requests.

**The innovation lab.** A central team separated from operations, building prototypes with no route to production. Impressive demonstrations, nothing deployed, and eventual disbandment.

**The lonely analyst.** One data person per business unit, no technical community, no review. Work quality varies unpredictably and people leave within two years.

**The shadow function.** Business units hire their own analysts because the central team is slow, without telling anyone. The organisation ends up with three incompatible definitions of revenue.

**The platform with no users.** A central team building infrastructure disconnected from what practitioners need, which they then route around.

**The bureaucratic hub.** A central function whose main activity is approving other people's work, adding delay without adding capability.

The common thread is a mismatch between where the problems are and where the capability sits, or between what a central function provides and what practitioners need. Diagnosing which pattern you have is more useful than adopting a model from a conference talk.

## Making a Transition Without Losing People

Reorganisations unsettle technical teams, and data people have options, so handling the change badly is expensive.

**Explain the problem, not just the structure.** People accept change that addresses something they recognise. Announcing a new model without naming the problem it solves produces cynicism.

**Involve the team in the design.** They know where the friction is, and the resulting structure is better for it.

**Be explicit about reporting lines and career paths.** The first questions everyone has are who is my manager and how do I get promoted. Ambiguity here is the main cause of departures during reorganisations.

**Protect the technical community.** Whatever the structure, keep the review, the shared standards and the regular technical meeting. This is what makes distribution survivable.

**Move gradually.** Embedding two or three people and learning from it is better than restructuring everyone at once.

**Set a review date.** Say that the model will be assessed in a year, which makes it a decision rather than a decree.

**Keep the definitions stable during the change.** A reorganisation that also fragments metric definitions creates a mess that takes years to undo.

## What This Means for Candidates

Candidates read operating models in interviews, and employers should expect the question.

Experienced data professionals ask where the team sits, who they would report to, and whether there is a technical community, because they have learned that these determine whether a role develops them. An organisation that can describe its model clearly is signalling that it has thought about how the work gets done.

The answers that attract good candidates are specific: a named technical manager, a described review practice, a platform that exists, and a clear statement of who decides what gets built.

The answers that repel them are vague: a business manager with no technical background, no peers, no defined progression and no route to production.

This is worth knowing because operating model decisions are usually made for delivery reasons and have significant recruitment consequences. An isolated embedded role is materially harder to fill than the same role inside a functioning technical community, and the vacancy will stay open longer.

## Real example

### The company that solved it by moving three people

A European manufacturer with about four thousand employees had a central data team of nine. Business units complained constantly about delays; the team complained about unclear, conflicting requests.

The proposed solution was to disband the central team into the units. The head of data proposed something narrower instead.

Three analysts moved permanently into the three units with the most demand: operations, commercial and supply chain. They kept a dotted reporting line to the data function, attended its weekly technical review, and used its platform and standards.

The remaining six focused on the data platform, shared models, the tooling everyone used and the two specialisms too scarce to distribute.

Within a year, request queues had largely disappeared because the embedded analysts handled most of what the units needed and knew which requests were worth escalating. Technical standards held because the analysts remained part of a technical community.

The head of data's assessment was that the argument had never been about structure. It had been about whether the people doing the work understood the business, and moving three desks solved it.

## Key Takeaways

- Centralised buys consistency and governance at the cost of domain depth and speed.
- Embedded buys relevance and responsiveness at the cost of quality and development.
- Most organisations converge on a hybrid where a central hub owns platform and standards.
- The hub must be useful to practitioners, not a control layer they route around.
- The right model changes as the organisation grows; expect to revisit it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: company with three data people) Which model for a small team?
Centralised. Below roughly six people, embedding creates isolation without meaningful gains in domain depth.

### (Scenario: organisation with embedded analysts already) How do we fix inconsistency?
Create a technical community: shared standards, a regular review, common tooling and a single owner for core definitions, without necessarily changing reporting lines.

### (Scenario: manager worried about career paths) How do embedded people progress?
Through a dotted line to a technical function that owns levelling, review and development, or they leave.

### (Scenario: employer facing new obligations) Does regulation favour one model?
Governance is easier to run centrally. Under the EU AI Act, obligations around documentation, oversight and monitoring need a clear owner, which distributed models must define explicitly.

### (Scenario: organisation considering change) How often should we revisit this?
Every couple of years, or whenever the team doubles. The structure that suited eight people rarely suits twenty-five.
