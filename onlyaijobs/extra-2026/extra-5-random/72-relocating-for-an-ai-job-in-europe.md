Salary comparisons between European countries are almost meaningless without the rest of the picture: what tax takes, what housing costs, what healthcare and childcare are provided, whether your partner can work, and how long a permit takes. Relocating for an AI job in Europe can be an excellent decision and an expensive mistake, and the difference usually lies in questions candidates do not think to ask until after they have accepted.

## The Legal Starting Point

**EU and EEA citizens** may live and work in any member state, with registration requirements that vary but are generally straightforward. Qualifications are broadly recognised, social security is coordinated across the Union, and moving is an administrative exercise rather than an immigration process.

**Non-EU citizens** need a permit. The routes include national skilled worker permits, which differ by country, and the EU Blue Card under Directive (EU) 2021/1883, intended for highly qualified employment with a salary threshold set relative to national averages. The revised directive improved mobility between member states and shortened some qualifying periods, though implementation varies.

Several countries operate additional schemes for highly skilled migrants with employers registered as sponsors, which are often faster than the general routes.

Practical realities: processing times vary from weeks to several months; employers must usually be involved as sponsor; family members' rights to join you and to work differ between countries and permit types; and time spent under one permit does not always count toward permanent residence in another country.

Verify current rules with the national authority rather than relying on general summaries, including this one.

## What to Ask Before Accepting

**Relocation support.** Does the employer pay for the move, temporary accommodation, and assistance with registration, tax and housing? Practical help matters more than money in the first month.

**Permit handling.** Who applies, who pays, what happens if it is refused, and what the timeline is.

**Partner employment.** Whether your partner has the right to work, and whether the employer helps them find something. This is the most common reason relocations fail.

**Tax treatment.** Several European countries offer favourable tax arrangements for incoming skilled workers, with conditions and time limits. Ask specifically whether you qualify and for how long.

**Housing.** Availability and cost in the actual area, not the national average. In several European cities, finding housing is harder than finding the job.

**Notice and return.** What happens to your permit if the employment ends, and whether relocation support is repayable if you leave early.

## Building an Honest Comparison

The calculation that matters takes an evening and prevents most disappointment.

Start with gross salary in both places, then apply income tax and social contributions using the official calculators most European tax authorities publish. The differences are large: effective rates on a comparable salary vary by tens of percentage points across the continent, and countries with higher rates typically return the difference in services you would otherwise buy.

Subtract housing for the actual neighbourhood you could live in, at current market rents rather than published averages. In several European cities the constraint is availability rather than price, and a deposit plus agency fee can be a substantial upfront cost.

Subtract childcare, which ranges from heavily subsidised to a major monthly expense depending on the country and the child's age, and check what allowances exist and whether you qualify.

Then add what is provided rather than purchased: healthcare, pension contributions, public transport, holiday entitlement and parental leave. These are not incidental. A country with high taxes and thirty days of leave, subsidised childcare and strong health coverage may leave you better off in both money and life than one with low taxes and none of it.

Finally note what you lose: proximity to family, an existing network, and a language you speak effortlessly.

## Permits in Practice

For non-EU citizens, the permit determines the timeline and constrains later choices, so the details matter.

**Who applies.** Usually the employer, often with a relocation agency. Ask who handles it, whether they have done it before, and how long their last one took.

**Timelines.** From a few weeks under fast-track schemes for registered sponsor employers to several months through general routes. Plan your resignation around the permit, not the reverse, and never resign before the permit is granted.

**Salary thresholds.** Blue Card and several national schemes require a salary above a defined level, which varies by country and is updated periodically. This can affect what an employer is able to offer.

**Family.** Rights for partners and children to join you, and for partners to work, differ by country and permit type. Unmarried partners are recognised in some countries and not others.

**Job changes.** Some permits tie you to an employer for an initial period, and changing jobs may require a new application. Understand this before you accept, because it affects your options if the role disappoints.

**Permanent residence and citizenship.** Qualifying periods differ, and time under some permits counts differently. If long-term settlement matters to you, check the path before choosing a country.

**Recognition of qualifications.** Rarely an issue for AI roles, which are unregulated professions, but relevant if your partner works in a regulated field such as healthcare or teaching.

## The Partner Question

More relocations fail because of the accompanying partner than for any other reason, and it is consistently under-discussed during hiring.

The practical issues are concrete. Does the partner have the legal right to work? In what language does their profession operate? Are their qualifications recognised, which matters greatly for teachers, healthcare professionals, lawyers and accountants? Is there a labour market for what they do in that specific city?

Then the less concrete ones. The person who moves for a job arrives with colleagues, structure and purpose. The partner arrives with an empty calendar in a country where they cannot easily work, cannot yet speak the language and know nobody. Six months of that is corrosive, and it is the point at which many families return.

What helps: choosing a location where the partner has a realistic path to employment or study; arranging language courses from the start, ideally funded by the employer; arriving with a plan for the partner rather than an intention to sort it out later; and treating the first year's social effort as a shared project rather than an individual one.

Employers who support the partner actively — introductions, job search help, language classes, a network of other international families — retain international hires far better, and it is entirely reasonable to ask what they offer.

## Language, Honestly

Most AI and data teams across Europe work in English, and you can be technically effective without the local language. Living well is a different matter.

Where English is sufficient at work, the local language still determines how much of your life is accessible: municipal administration, healthcare appointments, school communication, tenancy agreements, neighbours, and the informal conversations in which colleagues actually bond. People who never learn it often report feeling permanently adjacent to the place they live.

Career effects appear later. Roles closer to the business, management positions, public sector work and customer-facing functions frequently require the local language, so a purely English-speaking career can plateau.

The Netherlands, the Nordics and Ireland are the easiest places to function in English; German-speaking countries, France, Spain and Italy require more effort outside international companies.

Practical advice: start before you arrive, even at a basic level; ask whether the employer funds courses, as many do; and accept that conversational competence takes a year or two of consistent effort rather than a few months.

For a first move, choosing a country where you can function in English while learning the local language is a reasonable strategy, provided you actually do the learning.

## Practicalities of the First Three Months

Administrative sequencing catches people out, because several steps depend on each other.

In most countries you will need to register your address with the municipality, obtain a national identification or tax number, open a bank account, register with the health system or insurer, and arrange a tenancy. Each frequently requires one of the others, and the order matters. Employers with experience of international hires know the sequence; ask them for it rather than discovering it.

Housing is the hardest step in several European cities. Landlords may require proof of income, a local bank account and sometimes an employment contract you cannot yet produce, which is why temporary accommodation for the first month or two is worth negotiating.

Other practical matters: whether your driving licence is recognised or must be exchanged; how to transfer or preserve pension entitlements accrued elsewhere, which EU coordination rules address for state pensions but not always for occupational schemes; and whether you retain tax obligations in your previous country in the transition year.

Budget for the upfront cost, which is usually larger than expected: deposit, agency fees, furniture, and a period of double expenses.

None of this is difficult. It is simply volume, arriving at the same time as a new job, which is why employer support in the first month has disproportionate value.

## Relocating for an AI Job in Europe: Judging Whether the Move Is Worth It

After the arithmetic, the decision rests on less quantifiable things, and it is worth naming them explicitly.

**Career.** Does the role offer something you cannot get at home — scale, a specialism, a sector, a team with more experienced people? This is the reason that ages best, because it compounds and remains valid even if you return.

**Time horizon.** A two-year move to build experience is a different decision from an indefinite one. Being honest about which you intend changes what matters: for a short move, career and savings dominate; for a long one, language, family and belonging dominate.

**Reversibility.** How easy is it to return? For EU citizens, straightforward. For others, leaving may forfeit progress toward permanent residence.

**What you are leaving.** Ageing parents, a network, a partner's career, a place you are embedded in. These are real costs that do not appear in a spreadsheet, and people who dismiss them often underestimate them.

**Whether you like the place.** Visit before deciding if you possibly can, outside of an interview trip, including in the worst month of the year. Climate, daylight and the texture of daily life affect people more than they expect.

A move made for a clear reason, with the costs understood, generally goes well even when it is difficult. A move made to escape something rarely does.

## If It Does Not Work Out

A meaningful proportion of international moves end earlier than planned, and treating that as a possibility rather than a failure makes the decision easier.

Common reasons: the job differed from what was described, the partner could not build a life, the winter was harder than expected, family circumstances changed at home, or the company restructured.

Protective steps taken in advance cost little. Keep savings sufficient to return. Understand what your permit requires if employment ends — several countries allow a period to find new work, but the duration varies. Know whether relocation support is repayable if you leave within a defined period, since clawback clauses are common. Maintain your professional network at home. And avoid making irreversible decisions — selling a home, ending a partner's career permanently — in the first year.

Returning after eighteen months abroad is not a setback on a CV. International experience is read positively across European hiring, and the ability to work in an unfamiliar environment is exactly what employers say they value.

## Real example

### The offer that looked better than it was

A machine learning engineer in southern Europe received an offer from a company in a northern European city at roughly double her current salary. It seemed decisive.

Before accepting she spent an evening building a comparison. Income tax and social contributions in the new country took a considerably larger share. Rent for a modest two-bedroom apartment near the office was more than triple what she paid. Childcare for one child was a significant monthly cost, partially offset by allowances she had to apply for. Her partner, a teacher, would need language proficiency he did not have.

The disposable income difference, after housing and childcare, was modest. The career difference was not: the role offered production systems at a scale she had no access to at home, in a team of fifteen rather than two.

She accepted, for the reason that survived the analysis rather than the one that prompted it. Two years later she described the move as the right decision made for the right reason, and noted that if she had decided on the salary figure alone she would have arrived expecting something the move could not deliver.

## Key Takeaways

- EU citizens move freely; non-EU citizens need a permit, with the Blue Card and national schemes as the main routes.
- Compare disposable income after tax, housing and childcare, not gross salary.
- Partner employment is the most common cause of failed relocations.
- Ask what happens to your permit and relocation support if the job ends.
- Career reasons survive scrutiny better than salary reasons.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: non-EU candidate) Will employers sponsor a permit?
Larger employers and those registered as sponsors usually will; smaller companies often cannot. Ask at the first conversation rather than the last.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about language) Can I work in English?
In most technology and AI teams across Europe, yes. Daily life, administration and your partner's job prospects are a different question.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing offers) How do I compare salaries between countries?
Net of tax and social contributions, then subtract housing and childcare. Public salary calculators exist for most countries.

### (Scenario: candidate with a family) What about schools and childcare?
Availability and cost vary enormously, including within a country. Research the specific municipality, not the national picture, and register early where waiting lists exist.

### (Scenario: employer) How do we support relocating hires?
Handle the permit, pay for the move, provide temporary accommodation, help with registration and banking, and give practical support to the partner.
