Your professional profile does two jobs: it makes you findable by people searching for someone like you, and it answers the question a hiring manager has after reading your application. Most profiles do neither, because they were written once as a CV transcript and never revisited. Building a LinkedIn profile for AI jobs that works takes about ninety minutes, and for passive candidates it is often the highest-return ninety minutes in a job search.

## How Recruiters Actually Search

Recruiters searching for AI and data candidates in Europe typically filter by a combination of: job title keywords, skills, location or region, seniority, industry and sometimes language. They then scan results at speed, reading the headline, current role and the first lines of the summary.

Three consequences follow.

**Keywords determine whether you appear at all.** If your title is "Analytics Specialist" and recruiters search for "data scientist" and "machine learning engineer", you are invisible regardless of how good you are. Include the standard terms for the work you do somewhere in your headline, summary or experience.

**Location must be set correctly.** Many searches are geographic. An unset or outdated location removes you from results in the region where you actually want to work.

**The headline carries disproportionate weight.** It is visible in every search result, message and comment.

## What to Write in Each Section

**Headline.** Not your job title alone. State what you do and the domain: "Machine Learning Engineer — forecasting and time series in logistics" tells a recruiter more in eight words than most summaries do in a paragraph.

**Summary.** Four to six sentences. What you work on, the kinds of problems you have solved, the technologies you actually use, and what you are open to. Write in the first person and in plain language.

**Experience.** For each role, two or three bullets describing outcomes rather than duties. Name systems, scale and results where you are permitted to.

**Skills.** A focused list of things you would defend in an interview. Thirty listed skills dilute the signal from the five that matter.

**Education and projects.** For students and career changers, the projects section carries real weight. Describe each as a small case study.

## A LinkedIn Profile for AI Jobs Should Match Your CV

Discrepancies between a profile and a CV are noticed and are read as carelessness at best. Dates, titles and employers should agree. If your CV emphasises forecasting and your profile emphasises computer vision, a hiring manager will not know what you are.

Pick one direction, present it consistently, and change both documents together when your focus changes.

## Writing the Summary Without Sounding Like Everyone Else

The summary is where most profiles collapse into interchangeable phrases. A structure that avoids it:

**Sentence one: what you do, concretely.** "I build and operate forecasting systems for a European retailer, covering about eleven thousand product-store combinations."

**Sentences two and three: the kinds of problems you have solved.** Name two or three, with enough specificity that a reader can picture them. Mention the awkward parts — messy data, long feedback loops, regulated environments — because those signal real experience.

**Sentence four: how you work.** Whether you own systems in production, who you work with, what you care about technically. Keep it factual.

**Sentence five: what you are open to.** Explicit and current. "Open to machine learning engineering roles in the Netherlands or remote within CET" saves everyone time.

Avoid: "passionate about leveraging data to drive business value", "results-driven professional", lists of buzzwords, and third-person narration about yourself. None of these distinguish you, and the reader has seen forty of them today.

One useful test: could this summary belong to two hundred other people? If yes, add specifics until it could not.

## Describing Work You Cannot Describe

Many people in AI work under confidentiality constraints, in defence, healthcare, finance or on unreleased products. This is a solvable problem.

Describe the shape of the work without the specifics. "Built a classification system processing several hundred thousand documents monthly in a regulated environment, with human review for uncertain cases" reveals nothing confidential and tells a reader a great deal.

Use magnitudes rather than exact figures where exact figures are sensitive, and say when something is approximate.

Focus on your contribution and the engineering rather than on the client or the commercial outcome. Naming a client without permission is a genuine problem; describing that you built streaming pipelines for a telecommunications operator usually is not — though check your contract, because some explicitly prohibit even that.

If in doubt, ask your employer what you may say publicly. Most have a clear answer, and having asked is itself a good sign to a future employer.

What you must not do is describe work you did not do, inflate your role in a team effort, or imply ownership of results that were collective. Interviewers check, colleagues talk, and in a professional community as small as European AI the reputational cost far exceeds any benefit.

## Handling Recruiter Messages

If your profile works, messages follow, and most of them will not interest you. Managing them well costs little and occasionally pays off years later.

Reply briefly to messages that describe an actual role, even to decline. A two-line reply saying you are not looking but would be interested in that kind of work in future keeps a useful contact and takes twenty seconds.

Ignore mass messages with no company name, no salary indication and no description of the work. You are not obliged to respond to a template.

When a message does interest you, ask three questions before engaging: which company, what the salary range is, and why the role is open. Recruiters who cannot answer are usually working without a real brief.

Be explicit about confidentiality if you are employed: say your search is private and that your current employer must not be contacted.

And keep a note of who you spoke to and about what. Two years later, when you are actually looking, a recruiter you replied to politely is a warm contact rather than a cold approach, and that is worth more than most job applications.

## Your Wider Online Presence

A profile is one part of what an employer sees. For technical roles, three others matter.

**Code repositories.** Quality over quantity. Two or three repositories with a clear README, a description of the problem, instructions to run it and honest notes on limitations are worth more than twenty forked tutorials. Pin the good ones. Delete or archive the abandoned ones, or at least mark them as exercises.

**Writing.** A handful of posts explaining something you learned, or a project write-up on a personal site, gives a hiring manager evidence of how you think and communicate. This matters more than most people expect, because communication is the skill teams most often find missing.

**Public contributions.** Answers to questions, contributions to open source tools, a talk at a local meetup, a dataset or a small library. Each is a signal that you engage with a community.

**What is already visible.** Search your own name occasionally. Old accounts, forum posts and photographs are part of what an employer may see, and you are entitled to request removal of what should not be there.

None of this is required. But for candidates without an impressive employer on their CV, it is the most reliable way to demonstrate competence before anyone speaks to you.

## Privacy, Data and Your Right to Control It

Your profile is personal data on a commercial platform, and it is worth being deliberate about what is public.

Decide what a stranger should be able to see. Full employment history, education dates and location are usually fine; your personal phone number, home address, date of birth and photographs of your family are not, and none of them help you get hired. In most of Europe, employers should not be considering age, marital status or family situation at all, so giving them the information serves no purpose.

Under GDPR you have rights over the data platforms and employers hold about you, including access and, in many circumstances, erasure. Recruitment agencies hold candidate records; you can ask what they have and ask them to delete it.

Be aware that profile visibility settings affect both privacy and findability, and that the two trade off. Complete privacy means recruiters cannot find you either.

For employed candidates, one specific setting matters: disable notifications to your network before making substantial edits, since a burst of profile changes is a widely recognised signal that someone is looking.

## Keeping It Current Without Effort

Profiles decay. The most common failure is a profile that was accurate three years and one job ago.

A sustainable habit is to update it twice a year, in a fifteen-minute pass: adjust the headline if your focus has shifted, add one or two bullets for anything significant you shipped, refresh the skills list by deleting what you no longer use, and check that your location and openness statement are correct.

The better habit is to keep a private running note of what you have delivered — a line every month with the project, your contribution and the outcome. This takes two minutes a month and solves the hardest part of updating any professional document, which is remembering what you actually did. It also makes performance reviews and CV updates trivial.

One more point about timing: update your profile before you need it. A profile rewritten the week you start looking, after a redundancy announcement, is both less considered and more conspicuous than one maintained steadily. The people who benefit most from a good profile are those who were not looking when the right message arrived.

## Networking Without Networking

Many technical people dislike the idea of networking, and the good news is that the version that works for AI careers is mostly not what they are imagining.

It is not approaching strangers at events with a pitch. It is staying loosely in contact with people you have actually worked with, being visible in a few communities where your kind of work is discussed, and occasionally being useful to someone without an agenda.

Practically: keep in touch with two or three former colleagues per job; attend a local meetup a few times a year in your domain; answer a question when you know the answer; and reply to people who contact you with real questions.

The reason this matters is straightforward. A significant share of AI and data roles in Europe are filled through referrals and direct approaches rather than through open applications, and those channels run on weak ties — people who know roughly what you do and remember you exist.

## Real example

### The profile that was found by accident

A data engineer in Portugal had a profile listing his title, his employer and nothing else. He was not looking for work and received no approaches.

Out of curiosity he spent an evening rewriting it: a headline naming streaming data and the energy sector, a summary describing the pipelines he had built and their scale, and three bullets per role focused on what the systems did rather than which tools he used.

Within two months he received four approaches, two of them from companies in the energy sector specifically looking for streaming experience. He took none of them at the time. Eighteen months later, when his employer restructured, he contacted one of those recruiters directly and was in a process within a week.

His conclusion was that the profile had not created opportunities so much as made him locatable, and that being locatable is what matters when circumstances change unexpectedly.

## Key Takeaways

- Recruiters find you by keywords, location and seniority, then read the headline and summary.
- Use the standard terms for your work, even if your internal title is unusual.
- Write outcomes, not duties, and keep the profile consistent with your CV.
- For students and career changers, projects are the section that does the work.
- Being findable matters most when your circumstances change without warning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employed candidate) Will my employer notice if I update my profile?
Possibly, if you make many changes at once and have activity broadcasts enabled. Turn off notifications before editing and make changes gradually.

### (Scenario: candidate who dislikes posting) Do I need to post content?
No. Most people who are approached never post. A clear profile is enough; posting is optional and only worth it if you enjoy it.

### (Scenario: student with no experience) What do I put in experience?
Internships, student jobs, thesis work, competition entries and substantial course projects, each described as a problem you solved.

### (Scenario: candidate with a GitHub account) Do employers look at my code?
Sometimes, especially for junior roles. A small number of clean, documented repositories with readable READMEs serves you better than many abandoned ones.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we source candidates from profiles?
It works, but write to people about a specific problem rather than sending a generic message; response rates differ dramatically.
