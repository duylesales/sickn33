Most people change jobs from inside another job, and it is harder than it sounds. You have limited hours, limited privacy, an employer who deserves your attention while you are still paid for it, and processes that can run for two months. Job searching while employed in AI and data roles adds a specific complication: technical interviews demand preparation time that your evenings may not have. This guide covers how to run such a search efficiently, discreetly and without damaging your current position or your health.

## Deciding First Whether You Are Actually Leaving

Before spending a single evening on applications, be honest about what you want. Searches driven by a vague sense of dissatisfaction tend to produce offers that do not solve the problem, and people accept them because the process created momentum.

Write down what would have to change for you to stay: scope, pay, manager, technology, commute, flexibility, direction. Then ask whether any of it is obtainable where you are. Sometimes a conversation with your manager achieves more than three months of interviews. Sometimes writing the list makes it obvious that nothing will change, which is itself a clear answer.

If you do leave, that list becomes your evaluation criteria for offers, which is far better than judging by salary alone at the point when you are tired and flattered.

## Job Searching While Employed: The Discretion Rules

Assume anything on a work device or work network is visible to your employer. In practice:

- Use a personal email address and personal devices for everything related to the search.
- Do not store your CV or application notes on company systems.
- Take interview calls outside the office, not in a meeting room.
- Be careful with professional network settings; sudden profile activity is noticed.
- Do not tell colleagues, even trusted ones, until you have signed.
- Never use company time in a way you would be uncomfortable explaining.

Discretion is not deception. You are entitled to look for other work; your employer is entitled to your attention during working hours. Keeping those separate protects both sides.

## Building a Schedule That Survives Contact With Reality

The failure mode of employed job searches is not lack of opportunity; it is inconsistency. Two intense weekends followed by six quiet weeks produces nothing. A modest weekly rhythm produces offers.

A workable allocation for most people is three to five hours a week, divided as:

- **Ninety minutes of searching and applying.** Read new vacancies, shortlist, write one or two tailored applications.
- **Sixty to ninety minutes of preparation.** Rotate through fundamentals, system design, project narratives and the specific technologies that keep appearing in the roles you want.
- **Thirty minutes of admin.** Update your tracker, follow up where needed, reply to recruiters.

Put it in your calendar as a fixed appointment rather than intending to fit it in. Searching that depends on spare energy at the end of a working day does not happen, because there is rarely spare energy at the end of a working day.

Expect the whole process to take three to six months in European AI hiring, longer for senior or specialised roles. Planning for that horizon prevents the despair that arrives in week five when nothing has happened yet.

## Handling Interviews Around a Full-Time Job

Interview logistics cause more stress than the interviews themselves. A few tactics reduce it substantially.

**Ask for early, late or lunchtime slots.** Most European employers accommodate this routinely for employed candidates, and a recruiter who refuses is telling you something about the organisation.

**Use holiday for the demanding stages.** Technical rounds and on-site days deserve a full day, not a gap between meetings. Take the day, sleep properly and travel without pressure.

**Batch where you can.** If two companies are at final stage, try to schedule them in the same week so the disruption is contained and you can compare offers.

**Prepare once, reuse everywhere.** Three well-rehearsed project stories, a refreshed grasp of fundamentals and a standard set of questions for interviewers will carry you through most processes. Company-specific preparation should take thirty minutes, not three hours.

**Protect your delivery at work.** Nothing is worse than a search that damages your reputation before you leave, particularly in the small professional communities that European AI fields tend to be.

**Decline take-homes that are unreasonable.** A twenty-hour assignment is not compatible with employment; ask for a live session instead. Many employers agree.

## What Your Contract Actually Says

Before you get far, read your employment contract properly. Several clauses matter in European AI roles.

**Notice period.** One to three months is typical, and senior contracts can be longer. Notice usually starts from the end of a calendar month in several countries, which can add weeks to your actual leaving date.

**Non-competition and non-solicitation.** Enforceability varies significantly between countries, and courts in several member states limit or require compensation for restrictions. Do not assume a clause is unenforceable, and do not assume it is airtight either; if the clause is broad and your next employer is a competitor, take proper advice.

**Intellectual property.** Work produced in employment generally belongs to the employer. If your portfolio contains anything built at work, check before publishing it, and be careful in interviews not to disclose confidential details.

**Training repayment.** Some employers require repayment of course or certification costs if you leave within a defined period. Rules differ by country and some restrict this, but check the figure before you resign.

**Bonus and equity timing.** Vesting dates and bonus eligibility can be worth a great deal; a start date shifted by a few weeks sometimes preserves both.

## Keeping Your Integrity Intact

The ethical questions in an employed search are simple once stated, and worth stating.

Do the job you are paid for. Your team should not be able to tell from your output that you are looking. This matters practically as well as morally: references, rehiring and reputation all depend on how you behaved in the final months.

Do not take anything with you. Code, data, documents, customer lists and model artefacts belong to your employer, and copying them is both a contract breach and, where personal data is involved, a data protection incident.

Do not recruit colleagues on your way out. Beyond any contractual restriction, it damages relationships you may need later.

Be honest with prospective employers about your availability and notice period. Promising a start date you cannot meet creates a bad first impression before you arrive.

Do not use interviews you have no interest in as practice, beyond perhaps one. It wastes other people's time, and in small professional communities people remember.

None of this requires martyrdom. It simply means leaving in a way you would be comfortable describing later, which in a field as interconnected as European AI you may well have to.

## Counteroffers and the Conversation With Your Manager

When you resign, there is a reasonable chance of a counteroffer, particularly if you are hard to replace. Think about it in advance, because the moment is emotionally loaded.

Ask what the counteroffer actually changes. If you are leaving because the work is not interesting, the manager is difficult or the direction is wrong, more money does not address it. If you are leaving primarily because you are underpaid and everything else is good, a counteroffer can be legitimate — though it is worth asking why the correction required a resignation.

Consider the aftermath. In many organisations you are now known as someone who looked, which can affect how you are considered for long-term projects. In others it is treated as normal. You know which kind of place you work in.

Whatever you decide, do not use an offer you will not accept as leverage. It works occasionally and damages trust permanently when it does not.

If you accept the counteroffer, tell the other employer promptly and honestly. If you decline it, thank your manager genuinely — resignation conversations handled with grace are remembered as long as the ungraceful ones.

## Resigning and Leaving Well

Give notice in writing, to your manager first, in person or by call where possible. Keep the message short and neutral: you are leaving, your last day is X under your notice period, and you intend to hand over properly.

Then do the handover seriously. In AI and data teams this is more important than in many fields, because so much knowledge is undocumented: why a feature is excluded, which data source is unreliable, what the model actually does at the boundary. Write it down. A good handover document takes two days and is the last thing colleagues will remember about working with you.

Practical items worth completing before you go: transfer ownership of repositories, pipelines and dashboards; document credentials and access properly through the right channels; close out or reassign open tickets; and record where the evaluation sets and training data live.

Keep relationships open. European AI communities are small, colleagues move, and the person you hand over to may interview you in five years. Send a short message to people you valued working with, and leave the door genuinely open rather than performatively.

Finally, take a break between jobs if you possibly can, even a week. Starting a new role rested is worth more than the salary of the days you gave up.

## Protecting Your Energy

Searching while working is tiring in a specific way: it adds a second set of obligations without removing the first, and rejection arrives while you are already depleted.

A few things genuinely help. Keep a written record of what you have done rather than what you have achieved, because outcomes are slow and effort is not. Limit applications so that each one is worth the energy; ten considered applications will outperform fifty rushed ones and cost less. Expect a hit rate that feels low — a handful of interviews from twenty applications is normal in European AI hiring, even for strong candidates — so that it does not read as a verdict on you.

Tell one person outside work. A partner or friend who knows what you are doing makes the process less isolating and provides a reality check when a single rejection starts to feel like a pattern.

Stop when you need to. Pausing a search for three weeks costs very little; grinding through it exhausted and interviewing badly costs opportunities.

## Working With Recruiters Without Losing Control

Employed candidates in AI and data fields are contacted by recruiters regularly, and that channel can be useful or a drain depending on how you manage it.

Useful behaviour looks like this: reply to the ones who describe an actual role rather than a generic opportunity, state plainly what you are looking for and what you are not, and insist that your CV is never sent anywhere without your explicit agreement for each named company. That last rule prevents the most common problem, where the same profile is submitted to an employer twice through different routes and both applications are disqualified.

Ask a few questions before engaging: who the client is, why the role is open, what the process involves and what the salary range is. A recruiter who cannot answer these is unlikely to help you.

Be equally clear about discretion. Say that you are employed, that your search is confidential and that your current employer must not be approached. Reputable agencies handle this every day.

Finally, remember that recruiters are paid by employers, not by you. Good ones are genuinely helpful, but the advice you receive about whether to accept an offer comes from an interested party, and should be weighed accordingly.

## Real example

### The search that took four months and one calendar

A machine learning engineer at a European software company decided to move after his team's direction changed. He had two young children, no spare evenings and a demanding delivery schedule.

His first attempt failed. He applied to fifteen roles in one week, then could not prepare for the interviews that followed, performed poorly in two and withdrew from three. The experience left him convinced the market was bad.

Three months later he restarted differently. He blocked two hours on Sunday evening for the search and nothing else. He applied to at most two roles a week, chosen carefully. He used his statutory holiday entitlement for interview days, booking a full day for each so he was not rushing between a technical interview and a stand-up.

He also prepared once rather than repeatedly, writing out three project narratives and refreshing the fundamentals over several weeks.

It took four months to sign. He described the difference as scheduling rather than skill: the same person, the same experience, but a process that fitted inside a real life.

## Key Takeaways

- Decide what you actually want before applying; it becomes your criteria for judging offers.
- Keep the search entirely off work devices and work time.
- Apply narrowly and prepare properly rather than applying widely and arriving unprepared.
- Use holiday for interviews and batch them where possible.
- Tell your employer only when you have a signed contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate worried about references) Will employers contact my current manager?
Reputable employers ask permission first and usually only at offer stage. Say clearly that your current employer must not be contacted before you resign.

### (Scenario: candidate with a long notice period) Does a three-month notice period hurt my chances?
Rarely for senior roles, where employers expect it. Mention it early so nobody is surprised at offer stage.

### (Scenario: candidate asked why they are leaving) How honest should I be about my reasons?
Be factual and forward-looking. Criticising your current employer at length rarely helps, even when justified.

### (Scenario: candidate receiving a counteroffer) Should I accept a counteroffer?
Only if it fixes the actual reason you looked. Money alone rarely does, and the conversation has already changed how you are seen.

### (Scenario: employer) How should we treat candidates who are currently employed?
Offer interview slots early or late, keep the process short, and never contact their employer without written permission.
