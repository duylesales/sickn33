Candidates prepare for hours to answer questions and think about theirs in the lift. This is the wrong allocation, because the interview is the only opportunity you will have to find out what you are agreeing to, and the vacancy text tells you almost nothing. Good questions to ask an employer in an AI interview do two things at once: they get you information you cannot obtain elsewhere, and they demonstrate that you understand what makes these jobs succeed or fail.

## What You Are Actually Trying to Establish

Five things determine whether a data or AI role is good, and none of them appear in a job description.

**Does the work reach anyone?** Teams that build prototypes nobody deploys are demoralising regardless of the technology involved.

**Is the data usable?** Whether you spend your first year modelling or plumbing.

**Who will you learn from?** Whether there is someone whose judgement you respect.

**Does the organisation understand what you do?** Whether you will spend your time managing unrealistic expectations.

**Will your life be better?** Hours, commute, flexibility, pay, security.

Every question below serves one of these. Ask six or seven across the process rather than all of them at once.

## Questions to Ask an Employer in an AI Interview: The Core Set

**"What has the team put into production in the last year, and who maintains it?"** The single most informative question available. A specific answer means a route to production exists. Vagueness or a list of pilots means it may not.

**"What would I be working on in my first three months?"** If nobody can answer, the role is not defined and you will spend months finding work.

**"Where does the data come from, and what state is it in?"** Honest answers describe problems. An answer claiming everything is clean means they have not looked.

**"Who would review my work?"** Establishes whether you have technical company or are alone.

**"What happens when the team says something is not feasible?"** Reveals whether technical judgement carries weight.

**"What has the team stopped or cancelled, and why?"** Organisations that can answer are organisations that make decisions.

**"How does the team know its systems are still working?"** Monitoring practice reveals operational maturity better than any tooling question.

## Questions About the Data

Since most of your time will be spent here, these deserve more attention than they usually receive.

**"Walk me through where the data for this work comes from."** Listen for the number of systems, whether anyone owns them, and whether the answer is confident or improvised.

**"Is there a data platform, and who maintains it?"** A dedicated platform team changes your job substantially. Its absence means you are the platform team.

**"What is the data quality actually like?"** The answer you want describes specific problems. An answer of "it's pretty good" from someone who does not work with it daily means nobody has checked.

**"How long does it take to get access to a new data source?"** Days, weeks or months tells you the friction you will live with.

**"Has anyone built a data dictionary?"** Its existence indicates a team that documents; its absence is normal but tells you what your first months will involve.

**"What historical data exists, and how far back is it reliable?"** Particularly important for anything involving forecasting or trends.

**"Are there personal data constraints on what I can use?"** Relevant everywhere in Europe and decisive in some sectors, and the answer indicates whether governance is handled or improvised.

## Questions About the Team and How Work Happens

**"How is the team structured, and where does it sit in the organisation?"** Central function, embedded in a business unit, or part of engineering — each produces a different job.

**"Who decides what the team works on?"** If priorities arrive fully formed from elsewhere, you are executing requests rather than solving problems. Some people prefer that; know which you are getting.

**"How many projects is the team running at once?"** More than one per person usually means nothing finishes.

**"What does a typical week look like?"** Meetings, focus time, interruptions.

**"How long has the team existed, and how many people have left in the past two years?"** Turnover is the most honest indicator available.

**"How long has my prospective manager been here, and what is their background?"** A manager who cannot evaluate technical work cannot defend it.

**"What happened to the last person in this role?"** Promoted, left, or a new position — each is informative, and hesitation is informative too.

**"How are decisions recorded?"** Teams that write things down are teams where knowledge survives.

Ask at least one of these of someone other than the hiring manager. The comparison between answers is frequently the most useful information of the whole process.

## Questions About Production and Operations

These distinguish teams that ship from teams that present.

**"How does a model get from a notebook to something a user touches?"** Listen for whether a path exists or is invented each time.

**"Who is responsible for a system after it launches?"** If the answer is nobody, that becomes you by default without the authority to do anything about it.

**"Is there an on-call rotation, and how often was the last person paged?"** Covered in its own guide, and worth asking early because it materially affects your life.

**"What monitoring exists on deployed systems?"** Infrastructure only, or inputs and outcomes too.

**"Tell me about the last incident and what changed afterwards."** Teams that can describe one have operational maturity; teams that cannot either have nothing running or do not review.

**"How often do you deploy changes to production models?"** Weekly and annually are different jobs with different constraints.

**"How much of the team's capacity goes to maintaining what exists?"** Anything below twenty per cent usually means maintenance is not happening and debt is accumulating toward you.

Candidates who ask these are immediately identifiable as people who have operated systems, which is the scarcest profile in most European hiring processes.

## Questions About Conditions

These are legitimate and should be asked explicitly rather than inferred.

**"What is the salary range for this role?"** Under Directive (EU) 2023/970, member states are introducing requirements to provide pay information to applicants, so asking is increasingly expected. Ask early rather than at offer stage.

**"What is the expectation on office presence, and is it contractual or discretionary?"** A policy can change; a contract is harder to change.

**"What are the actual working hours in this team?"** Ask for the last busy period rather than the policy.

**"What happens at month-end, year-end or peak season?"** Every organisation has one, and it shapes several weeks a year.

**"What is the training budget, and did people use it last year?"** The second half of the question is the informative one.

**"How does progression work, and what would the next level require?"** Vague answers indicate progression by negotiation rather than by criteria.

**"What is the notice period, and is there a probationary arrangement?"** Standard in Europe and worth confirming.

**"Is part-time or flexible working possible?"** Even if you do not need it now, the answer indicates the organisation's general posture.

Ask these of the recruiter or hiring manager rather than of engineers, who often do not know.

## Questions About Governance and Ethics

Increasingly relevant in Europe, and revealing about how an organisation operates.

**"Are any of your systems classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act, and how are you handling that?"** A confident answer indicates the organisation has engaged with it. Confusion indicates work ahead, which may be an opportunity or a mess.

**"Who is involved when a project raises data protection questions, and at what stage?"** Early involvement means governance is a design input; late involvement means projects get stopped after they are built.

**"Has the team declined to build something, and what was it?"** The best single question about an organisation's ethics, because it requires a specific example rather than a value statement.

**"What happens if I raise a concern about a project?"** Listen for a process and for evidence it has been used.

**"How do you handle human oversight in deployed systems?"** Reveals whether oversight is designed or nominal.

**"What is your position on where data is processed?"** Relevant where confidentiality or data residency matters, and indicative of how carefully these decisions are made.

None of these are confrontational. They are the questions any serious practitioner in a European organisation encounters regularly, and asking them signals that you have.

## Reading the Answers

The content matters; the manner matters as much.

**Specific beats general.** "We deployed a demand forecasting service in March and Marta maintains it" is worth more than "we have several models in production".

**Honest problems beat clean claims.** A manager who describes what is difficult is describing reality. One who says everything works well either does not know or is selling.

**Hesitation is data.** A long pause before answering what happened to the last person in the role usually precedes something worth knowing.

**Consistency across people matters.** Ask a version of the same question to two people. Where the manager and the engineer describe different realities, believe the engineer.

**Watch how they treat you.** Lateness, poor preparation, interviewers who have not read your CV, silence between stages. This is the organisation's best behaviour.

**Notice what they ask you.** A process that never asks about production, evaluation or stakeholders is a process from a team that does not do those things.

And if an interviewer responds badly to reasonable questions — defensively, dismissively, or with irritation — you have learned how disagreement is handled there, which is worth the awkwardness of having asked.

## Before You Accept

A few final checks once an offer arrives, which candidates often skip in relief.

**Get the important things in writing.** Title, level, salary, hours, location expectation, notice period, and any specific agreement about flexibility or scope. A verbal arrangement with a manager who leaves is worth little.

**Read the contract properly.** Notice period, non-competition and non-solicitation clauses, intellectual property, training repayment obligations and any clawback on relocation or bonuses. These vary considerably across Europe and are difficult to renegotiate later.

**Ask to speak to someone who joined in the past year.** They remember the onboarding and the gap between what was described and what they found.

**Ask what would make the first six months a success**, and whether that is written down.

**Check the practical facts.** The actual commute at the actual time of day, and what the office is like on a normal Tuesday.

Take a day before responding. Enthusiasm at offer stage is real and temporary, and the questions you forgot to ask will occur to you overnight.

## Real example

### The question that changed a decision

An experienced machine learning engineer reached a final interview with a company whose product and salary both appealed. The process had been good and she expected to accept.

In the last conversation she asked what the team had deployed in the previous twelve months, and who maintained it.

The answer took some time. Two projects had been completed and handed to another department, which had not integrated them. One was running but was maintained by a contractor who had left. A fourth had been cancelled when the sponsor changed role.

She then asked what would be different for her work. The hiring manager was honest: they were trying to fix exactly this, and she would be part of that attempt, but nothing had changed yet.

She declined, and took a less exciting role at a company where three systems were running and the engineers who built them still owned them.

Two years later she said it had been the best professional decision she had made, and that a single question had produced the information that made it.

## Key Takeaways

- Ask what has reached production and who maintains it; it predicts satisfaction better than anything else.
- Vagueness about your first three months means the role is not defined.
- Honest answers about data quality are a good sign, not a warning.
- Ask what the team has cancelled; decisive organisations can answer.
- Ask about conditions explicitly rather than assuming they match the vacancy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate worried about appearing difficult) Will these questions damage my chances?
No. Experienced hiring managers read them as evidence of production experience, and a manager who reacts badly to them has told you something useful.

### (Scenario: candidate in an early screening call) When should I ask what?
Practicalities and salary early, work and team in the technical rounds, conditions and governance at final stage.

### (Scenario: candidate receiving vague answers) What if they cannot answer?
That is an answer. Ask once more, more specifically, and weigh the response.

### (Scenario: candidate who wants to speak to the team) Can I ask to talk to an engineer?
Yes, and confident employers arrange it readily. Ask for a conversation without the hiring manager present.

### (Scenario: employer) Should we welcome these questions?
Yes. Candidates who ask them are the ones who will notice problems early and raise them, which is what you want.
