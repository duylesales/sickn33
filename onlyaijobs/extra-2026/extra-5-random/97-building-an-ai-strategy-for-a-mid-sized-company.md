Mid-sized European companies are under pressure to have an AI strategy, and most of the advice available is written for organisations with a hundred times their resources. Building an AI strategy for a mid-sized company is a narrower exercise than the literature suggests: choose two or three problems where the value is real, establish whether the data supports them, decide honestly what to build and what to buy, and resource it properly. Everything else is elaboration.

## Start With Problems, Not Capability

The most common failure is starting from the technology. A company decides it should use AI, commissions a workshop, produces a list of twenty ideas, pilots three and deploys none.

The alternative is to start from the operational decisions that are currently made badly or slowly, and ask which of them data could improve.

Practical questions to ask each part of the business: what decision do you make repeatedly that you are unsure about? What takes people a long time and feels mechanical? Where do errors cost us most? What do you wish you knew a week earlier?

The answers produce a short list of candidate problems grounded in the business rather than in the technology. Most will not need machine learning; several will be solved by better reporting, a rule, or fixing a process. That is a good outcome rather than a disappointing one, because those solutions are cheaper and more reliable.

Then pick two. Not eight.

## Building an AI Strategy for a Mid-Sized Company: The Readiness Test

Before committing to a candidate problem, test it against five questions.

**Does the data exist?** Not in principle — in a system, with history, retrievable. Check by extracting it rather than by asking.

**Is there enough history?** Seasonal problems need several years; rare events need many occurrences.

**Will the outcome be observable?** If you can never tell whether a prediction was right, you can never improve or justify the system.

**Is there a decision attached?** Someone must be able to act on the output, with the authority and the time to do so.

**Can we quantify the value?** Hours, euros, errors avoided. If nobody can estimate it, the project will be cancelled the first time budgets tighten.

A problem that fails on data or on decision should be set aside, not forced. A problem that passes all five is worth starting.

## Assessing What You Actually Have

An honest inventory takes a few days and prevents most disappointment.

**Systems.** List the systems that hold operational data: the enterprise resource planning system, the manufacturing or service platform, the customer relationship system, the finance ledger, spreadsheets that people rely on. Note who administers each and whether data can be extracted without disrupting operations.

**History.** For each, how far back does usable data go, and what happened in between — migrations, reorganisations, definition changes.

**Quality.** Rather than asking, test. Pull a sample, count the rows, check whether identifiers are unique, look at null rates, and try to reconcile one figure against something the business publishes. Failing to reconcile is informative.

**Access.** How long does it take to obtain data, and who decides.

**People.** Who understands each system. In many mid-sized companies this is one person per system, and several of them are close to retirement.

**Tooling.** What already exists for reporting, scheduling and storage. Using it is almost always better than introducing something new.

This inventory is itself valuable beyond any AI project, and the act of producing it usually reveals two or three improvements worth making immediately.

## Build, Buy or Partner

For a mid-sized company the honest answer is usually a mixture, and the distinction that matters is whether a capability differentiates you.

**Buy** where the problem is common and mature products exist: document processing, transcription, standard reporting, common security tooling, customer service automation. You are not going to build these better than a specialist vendor, and the guidance on evaluating vendors applies.

**Build** where the problem depends on your specific data, process or domain, and where the improvement matters commercially. Demand forecasting for your particular product mix, quality prediction for your process, or a model of your own customer behaviour are rarely available off the shelf.

**Partner** for the first build if you have no internal capability: a bounded engagement with a contractor or consultancy, with knowledge transfer written into the contract and an internal person involved throughout.

**Use a student project** to test feasibility cheaply, as described in the guide on university collaboration. For a question you are unsure about, five months of a graduation student costs a fraction of any alternative and produces an evidence-based answer.

The sequence that works for most mid-sized companies is to partner or use a student for the first project, hire once you know the capability is worth having permanently, and buy everything that is not specific to you.

## Resourcing It Properly

Projects fail on resourcing more often than on technology, and the gaps are predictable.

**Someone must own the outcome.** Not the project — the outcome. A named person in the business who wants the result, will act on it, and will defend the work when priorities shift. Projects without this die quietly.

**Someone must own the system afterwards.** Whoever builds it, somebody internally must be able to operate it, check it and fix it. If nobody can, you have bought a dependency rather than a capability.

**Budget for maintenance.** A deployed model requires monitoring, occasional retraining and adaptation when source systems change. A reasonable planning assumption is that ongoing cost is a meaningful fraction of the build cost each year.

**Allocate time from the business.** The domain experts who explain what the data means and judge whether output is sensible need hours, and those hours must come from somewhere.

**Expect the data work to dominate.** Most of the effort is obtaining, cleaning and understanding data rather than modelling. Plans that assume otherwise slip.

**Do not make it someone's side project.** The most common resourcing failure in mid-sized companies is asking a capable person to do this alongside a full-time role, which produces neither.

## Governance Without Bureaucracy

Mid-sized companies need governance proportionate to their size, and a small amount established early prevents expensive problems.

**Keep a register.** A simple list of every AI or automated decision system in use, with its purpose, its owner, what data it uses and who it affects. This takes an hour to start and answers the first question any regulator, auditor or customer will ask.

**Classify before you build.** Determine whether a proposed use falls into a category with obligations. Under the EU AI Act, uses in recruitment and worker management, access to essential services, creditworthiness assessment and several other areas are treated as high-risk, with requirements including documentation, human oversight, logging and monitoring. Knowing this at design stage is cheap; discovering it after deployment is not.

**Involve data protection early.** Where personal data is involved, a short conversation at the start avoids a project being stopped at the end.

**Define the human role.** For every system, state what it decides and what a person decides, and ensure the person has the time and information to exercise judgement.

**Document as you go.** Purpose, data, evaluation, limitations, owner. Two pages per system.

None of this requires a committee. It requires one person with the responsibility and a template.

## Generative AI Specifically

Most mid-sized companies are being asked about language models rather than about forecasting, and the strategy question differs slightly.

The applications that consistently deliver value are unglamorous: searching and answering questions over internal documents; drafting routine correspondence with human review; extracting structured information from invoices, orders and forms; summarising long documents for people who must read them; and supporting customer service agents rather than replacing them.

The applications that consistently disappoint are those presented as autonomous: systems answering customers without review, generating content that is published unchecked, or making decisions without a person in the loop.

Three practical decisions shape everything. Where processing happens, which matters for confidential data and may require EU-hosted or self-hosted options. What the acceptable error rate is and what happens when the system is wrong, which is a business decision rather than a technical one. And who checks the output, which requires capacity that must be planned.

Start with an internal use case rather than a customer-facing one. The risk is lower, the users can be trained, and you will learn what the technology does and does not do before anything reaches a customer.

And set a policy on staff use of external tools, because in the absence of one people will use them with company data.

## Measuring Whether It Worked

Projects that cannot demonstrate value are cancelled during the next budget review, regardless of their technical quality.

Agree the measure before starting, in the organisation's own terms: stockouts avoided, hours saved, scrap reduced, invoices processed without intervention, customers retained. A percentage improvement in a statistical metric is not a business measure.

Establish the baseline before the change. This is the step most often skipped, and without it any claim afterwards is contested.

Measure honestly, including when the result is disappointing. An organisation that only ever reports successes will stop believing its own reporting.

Report at a defined interval to the person who sponsored the work, and keep it short.

Watch for the second-order effects. A forecasting improvement that planners do not trust produces no benefit; a document system that staff bypass produces none either. Adoption is part of the measurement.

And review the whole programme annually against the two or three problems you chose. If one is delivering and one is not, stop the one that is not rather than continuing because it was in the plan. The ability to stop things is what distinguishes companies that build capability from those that accumulate abandoned pilots.

## When to Hire, and Who

The decision to hire internally should follow evidence rather than ambition.

The signal to hire is that you have a problem worth solving continuously, a partner or student project has shown it is tractable, and there is work for a person for the foreseeable future. Hiring before you know what the person will do produces the outcome described in the guide on working as the only data person.

For a first hire, prefer breadth over specialism. Someone who can obtain data, build something simple, deploy it and explain it to the business is worth more than a specialist in any one technique. Look for evidence of delivery in a similar-sized organisation rather than credentials.

Set them up properly: a sponsor, a defined first project, access arranged before the start date, a budget for tooling and development, and a plan to hire a second person within two years so the capability does not rest on one individual.

Be realistic about competition. You are unlikely to outbid technology companies on salary, and you do not need to. Regional employers hire successfully by offering ownership, proximity, stability and genuine flexibility — and by being findable at all.

## Real example

### The company that spent six months on the right question

A European packaging manufacturer with around six hundred employees was asked by its board for an AI strategy. The managing director resisted commissioning a consultancy study and instead asked three managers a single question: what decision do you make every week that you would like to make better?

The answers were specific. Production planning was uncertain about demand for two product families. Quality was unsure which line settings drove a recurring defect. Sales could not tell which customers were drifting away until they had gone.

They tested each against data. Demand history existed and was good. Quality data existed but the defect was recorded inconsistently by shift. Customer order history existed and was clean.

They chose demand forecasting and customer drift, and deferred quality until the recording was fixed — which a supervisor did in three weeks once someone explained why it mattered.

Both projects delivered within a year. The managing director's reflection was that the strategy document ran to two pages and that the six months of asking questions had been the strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- Start from decisions made badly, not from technology.
- Test candidate problems against data, observability, decision and value before committing.
- Choose two problems, not eight, and finish them.
- Most good answers are not machine learning, which is a saving rather than a failure.
- Resource ownership and maintenance, not just the build.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: company with no technical staff) Where do we begin?
With the problem selection above, which requires no technical expertise. Bring in help once you know what you are trying to do.

### (Scenario: board asking for a strategy document) How long should it be?
Two or three pages: the problems chosen, why, what is required, who owns it, and how success will be measured.

### (Scenario: company weighing cost) What does a first project cost?
Far less than most expect if scoped narrowly. A bounded contract engagement or a student project can establish feasibility before larger commitment.

### (Scenario: employer asking about regulation) What are our obligations?
It depends on the use. Under the EU AI Act some uses are high-risk with substantial duties, and GDPR applies to any personal data. Take advice at design stage.

### (Scenario: company that tried before) Why did our last attempt fail?
Usually no data, no owner for the result, or a pilot with no route to production. All three are preventable.
