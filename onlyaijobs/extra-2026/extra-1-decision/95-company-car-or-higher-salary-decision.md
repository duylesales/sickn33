The offer includes a car. For roles with client visits, site work or a long drive to the office, this is common in the Netherlands, and it is one of the few benefits that candidates consistently misjudge in both directions. Some treat it as free transport worth a large amount and accept a lower salary for it. Others dismiss it because they cycle to work, without noticing that the alternative on offer might be worth more to them than the car ever would. The arrangement is specific and the arithmetic is individual, and it is worth doing before you decide.

This is general information, not tax advice. Rates, categories and rules in this area change regularly, so verify the current position with the Belastingdienst or an adviser for your own situation.

## What a Lease Car Actually Is

The employer leases a vehicle and makes it available to you. You use it for work and, in most arrangements, privately as well.

Private use has a tax consequence: an amount is added to your taxable income, calculated as a percentage of the vehicle's list value, which increases the tax you pay. The percentages have differed for electric and conventional vehicles and have been adjusted in recent years, and the rules on how long a rate applies to a given car are specific — which is exactly why you should check the current position rather than relying on what a colleague experienced two years ago.

You can avoid the addition only by genuinely not using the car privately beyond a small threshold, which requires a formal declaration to the tax authority and a full journey administration. Most people do not choose this route, and those who do take the record-keeping seriously.

Fuel or charging is often covered, sometimes with limits. Insurance and maintenance sit with the lease. There may be a personal contribution if you choose a vehicle above the allowed category, and there are usually rules about mileage limits, damage and who else may drive.

## Why It Is Offered

**The role requires travel.** Site visits, client locations, multiple offices.

**Sector norm.** In parts of industry, logistics, construction, medical devices and commercial functions, a car is standard and its absence would be noticed.

**Recruitment.** For candidates who drive, it is an attractive and visible part of a package.

**Practicality.** Some locations are genuinely difficult to reach by public transport, particularly outside the Randstad and at industrial sites.

## The Comparison Nobody Does Properly

To compare a car against salary, work out the following.

**What the car costs you in tax.** The addition increases your taxable income, so your net pay falls by a meaningful amount every month. That is the price of the benefit.

**What you would otherwise spend.** If you currently own a car, include depreciation, insurance, road tax, maintenance, tyres and fuel. If you do not own one and would not buy one, this line is zero and the car is worth much less to you than to a colleague who drives anyway.

**What the salary alternative would be.** Ask explicitly what the package looks like without the car. Employers usually have a figure.

**The knock-on effects.** Salary drives pension accrual, holiday allowance and borrowing capacity; a car generally does not. If a mortgage application is coming, this difference can matter more than the monthly comparison.

For someone who drives a lot privately and would otherwise run a car, the arrangement is frequently good value. For someone who cycles to the station and rents a car twice a year, it is usually a poor trade against salary.

## The Alternatives Worth Asking About

**A mobility budget.** Increasingly common at Dutch employers: an amount you can spend across transport options — public transport, a bicycle, occasional car use, sometimes paid out in part. For people who commute by train or bike, this is normally the better arrangement.

**A public transport arrangement,** including business travel cards for employees who commute by rail.

**Travel allowance** for commuting by your own means, where the tax-free level is set annually.

**A bicycle scheme,** which many employers offer and which suits a large share of Dutch commutes.

**Simply more salary,** which is the cleanest option and the one candidates forget to ask for.

An employer who offers only a car may still have these alternatives; ask what exists rather than assuming the car is the whole menu.

## The Lock-In

Lease contracts typically run for several years, and that has consequences.

**Your choice is fixed for the term,** including if your circumstances change — a move, a new baby, a switch to mostly remote work.

**Leaving the employer ends the arrangement.** Usually the employer carries the consequences, and some contracts include provisions about early termination costs, particularly if you chose an expensive vehicle. Read that clause.

**Changing to a lower-value car mid-term is rarely possible.**

**If your role becomes remote,** you may find yourself paying tax on a benefit you barely use. This is worth thinking about in a field where working patterns change.

## Questions to Ask

- **What would the package be without the car?**
- **Is there a mobility budget or an alternative arrangement?**
- **What category of vehicle applies to this role, and what would a personal contribution be for a different choice?**
- **Is private use permitted, and is fuel or charging covered?**
- **What happens if I leave during the lease term?**
- **Are there mileage limits or restrictions on who may drive?**
- **Does the arrangement follow a collective agreement?** If so, the terms may be fixed.

## When a Car Is Worth Taking

- Your role genuinely involves regular travel to sites or clients.
- You would own a car anyway, and the total cost you currently carry is substantial.
- Your commute is by car and public transport is not realistic.
- Private use is permitted and you would use it.
- The salary alternative offered is noticeably lower than the value of running your own vehicle.
- You are settled enough that a multi-year arrangement is not a constraint.

## When to Take the Salary

- You commute by bicycle or train, which is a very large share of Dutch data professionals.
- You do not own a car and would not buy one.
- You are planning a mortgage application, where salary counts and a car does not.
- Your working pattern is likely to become more remote.
- You prefer flexibility to a multi-year commitment.
- The employer's alternative is a mobility budget that fits how you actually travel.

## How to Negotiate

Ask for the comparison in writing: the package with the car, and the package without it. Employers with a car policy usually have both figures, and seeing them side by side settles most of these decisions in a minute.

If you do not want the car, ask specifically whether the value can go to salary or to a mobility budget. Some employers can do this and some cannot, because the car comes from a different budget or a policy applies to the role — but the answer is often yes, and it is rarely offered unprompted.

And if you do want the car, check the personal contribution rules before choosing a vehicle, since the difference between categories can be larger than people expect over a four-year term.

## What Happens If Your Working Pattern Changes

A lease runs for years and working patterns in data roles do not. This is the mismatch that turns a good decision into an irritating one.

Consider the realistic scenarios before committing. If the team moves to two days on site, a car chosen for a daily commute becomes an expensive convenience you still pay tax on every month. If you move house closer to the office, the same applies. If your role shifts towards a site you can reach by train, the calculation inverts entirely.

Ask three questions before choosing.

**Can the arrangement be ended or changed mid-term, and at whose cost?** Some employers allow a return of the vehicle under defined conditions; others do not.

**Does the employer offer a switch to a mobility budget at renewal?** A growing number do, and knowing the option exists makes a four-year commitment feel shorter.

**What is the expected office pattern for this role in two years?** Nobody can promise it, and the honest answer tells you how stable the assumption is.

If the answers are uncertain and your commute is marginal, the safer choice is salary or a flexible budget: those adapt to whatever your week becomes, and a lease does not.

## Real example

### An engineer in Apeldoorn who asked for the alternative

A data engineer is offered a role at a manufacturer with sites in three provinces. The package includes a lease car, and the salary is slightly below what she had hoped.

She cycles to her current job and does not own a car. She asks what the package looks like without it.

The answer is a higher salary plus a mobility budget, because the company introduced one two years ago and rarely mentions it to candidates who are offered a car.

She works out both options. With the car, the taxable addition would reduce her net pay every month for a benefit she would use mainly for the occasional site visit. Without it, she gets a higher base — which counts for her pension, her holiday allowance and the mortgage application she is planning — plus a budget that covers her train travel to sites.

She takes the salary and the budget, and hires a car on the handful of days when a site visit makes driving simpler.

Two years later, when she buys a house, the higher base is what determines her borrowing capacity — which, she notes, no one mentions when they hand you a set of car keys.

## Key Takeaways

- A lease car with private use adds an amount to your taxable income based on a percentage of the vehicle's list value, so it reduces your net pay every month; rates and rules change, so verify the current position.
- Compare properly: the tax cost, what you would otherwise spend on a car, the salary alternative, and the effect on pension, holiday allowance and borrowing capacity.
- It is usually good value if you would run a car anyway and travel for work; usually poor value if you commute by bicycle or train.
- Ask about mobility budgets, public transport arrangements, bicycle schemes and travel allowances — many employers have them and mention them only on request.
- Check the lease term, the personal contribution rules and what happens if you leave during the contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a car) Does a lease car cost me anything?
Yes, if you use it privately. An amount based on a percentage of the vehicle's list value is added to your taxable income, reducing your net pay each month. Current rates and categories should be verified.

### (Scenario: candidate who cycles to work) Should I take the car if I do not drive much?
Usually not. Ask what the package is without it — a higher salary or a mobility budget generally suits people who commute by bicycle or train much better.

### (Scenario: candidate planning a mortgage) Does a car affect borrowing capacity?
Salary drives borrowing capacity, pension accrual and holiday allowance; a car generally does not. If a mortgage is coming, that difference can outweigh the monthly comparison.

### (Scenario: candidate considering alternatives) What else can I ask for?
A mobility budget, a public transport arrangement, a bicycle scheme, a travel allowance, or simply a higher salary. Many Dutch employers have several of these and mention them only if asked.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about commitment) What happens if I leave during the lease?
The employer usually carries the consequences, though some contracts include provisions about early termination costs, particularly for higher-value vehicles. Read that clause before choosing a car.
