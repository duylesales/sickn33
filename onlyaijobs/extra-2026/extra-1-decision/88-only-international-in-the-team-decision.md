The interviews were in English, everyone spoke it well, and nobody seemed to find the question of language interesting. That is the normal Dutch experience: proficiency is high, switching is effortless, and colleagues are generally happy to accommodate. What candidates discover later is that a team can be genuinely welcoming in English and run most of its actual life in Dutch — the quick exchange over a desk, the decision made walking back from the coffee machine, the message in a group channel, the ten years of documentation nobody is going to translate. Being the only international in a team is rarely a barrier to doing the work. It is frequently a barrier to everything that surrounds the work, and that is what determines whether you stay.

## Where the Gap Actually Appears

**Meetings that begin in English.** They hold for a while. Then two people get into the detail, switch, and resolve the thing you came for. Nobody is excluding you deliberately; they are thinking faster in their own language.

**The informal channel.** Lunch, the walk to the station, the corridor. This is where problems are flagged before they become tickets and where opinions are formed about who is good.

**Written history.** Tickets, decision records, procedures and email threads from before the team switched to English.

**Administration and policy.** HR documents, collective agreement provisions, pension information, internal forms. Often available only in Dutch, and these are the documents that concern your own position.

**Humour and idiom,** which is trivial until it is the reason you feel like a guest after a year.

## What Employers Say, and What Happens

Most employers describe themselves as English-speaking sincerely. Three things go unsaid, usually because nobody has noticed them.

**Effort decays.** A team switches to English for a new colleague and holds it for a month or two. Under pressure — an incident, a deadline, a difficult stakeholder — it reverts, because that is where the fluency is.

**Documentation lags years behind.** A team that writes in English today has a decade of Dutch behind it.

**Stakeholders are not in the team.** Your colleagues may speak excellent English while the planners, adjusters, technicians or clinicians you depend on do not, or do not enjoy it.

Ask the question in a form that cannot be answered with an intention: not "is the working language English" but "what happened in the last technical discussion that got difficult".

## The Career Cost Nobody Mentions

Information reaches you later and in summary form. Over a year, that means you contribute later to decisions, you hear about opportunities after others, and you are less present in the conversations where projects are allocated.

This is not discrimination and it produces a similar outcome. The remedy is structural rather than emotional: work that is visible in writing, one-to-one relationships that do not depend on group settings, and a manager who understands that summarising the Dutch part of a conversation is part of the job.

## The Retention Reality

People rarely leave these roles because of the work. They leave because after eighteen months they are professionally content and socially peripheral, and a role elsewhere with three other internationals looks like relief.

That is worth planning for rather than discovering. If the team has had international colleagues before, ask how long they stayed. If the honest answer is a year each, the pattern is about the environment rather than about the individuals.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **Have international colleagues worked in this team, and are they still here?**
- **What language do people use when they are not in a meeting?**
- **In what language are documentation, tickets and decisions written?**
- **Which stakeholders would I work with, and are they comfortable in English?**
- **Is language training funded, and is time allowed for it?** Time matters more than money.
- **What does the team do when a discussion switches?** A team that has an answer has thought about it.
- **Is there anyone whose role includes helping me navigate the organisation?**

## What Good Looks Like

- Other internationals who have stayed for years.
- Written decisions in English as a habit, not a policy.
- A manager who reflexively summarises when the room switches.
- Language training with hours attached, treated as development.
- Stakeholders in functions where English is genuinely normal.
- An explicit buddy arrangement for the first months.
- Honesty in the interview: "meetings are in English, the corridor is in Dutch, and you will want to learn some."

## What You Can Do

**Learn to read first.** It is faster than speaking and it unlocks documentation, tickets, policy and the group channel. Three to six months of consistent effort changes your position substantially.

**Ask for the switch to be flagged rather than avoided.** Most colleagues prefer "sorry, can you give me the short version in English" to the alternative, which is you missing the decision. Dutch working culture handles direct requests well.

**Invest in one-to-one relationships.** Individual conversations happen in English readily, even with people who are quiet in English in a group. This is where the informal information actually becomes available to you.

**Make your work visible in writing.** A weekly written summary compensates for the conversations you were not part of.

**Take the language course seriously enough to be seen trying.** Effort changes how colleagues treat the situation, even before fluency arrives.

**Build a professional network outside the team** — meetups, communities of internationals in Dutch tech — so that belonging does not depend entirely on one group of colleagues.

## When It Works Fine

- You intend to learn Dutch, and the employer supports it with time.
- The team writes things down in English as a matter of course.
- Your stakeholders are technical or international.
- The organisation has a track record of internationals staying.
- You have a social life outside work that does not depend on colleagues.
- The work itself is strong enough to justify the effort of the first year.

## When to Think Twice

- No international has stayed longer than a year.
- Documentation, tickets and stakeholder work are all in Dutch with no plan to change.
- The manager has no answer for what happens when the room switches.
- You would also be remote, which removes the informal contact that helps most.
- You have no intention of learning any Dutch in a role that plainly requires some.
- You are early in your career and depend heavily on informal learning.

## What to Ask the Employer For

Most of what makes this work is within a manager's gift and costs almost nothing. Asking before you start is far easier than raising it in month six, and the requests below are ordinary enough that reasonable employers simply agree.

**A summary when the room switches.** Ask the manager to make it their habit — a thirty-second recap in English after a Dutch exchange. Coming from the manager rather than from you, it sets the norm for everyone else.

**Decisions in writing, in English.** Not meeting minutes, just outcomes: what was decided, by whom, why. This is the single most effective measure, and it benefits the whole team.

**A named buddy for three months,** for the questions that are too small to bring to a manager — how expenses work, what an acronym means, who actually decides something.

**Language training with hours attached.** Money for a course is common; time during the working week is what makes people finish one. Ask for both, and ask for it in the contract rather than in a policy.

**An introduction round with stakeholders,** arranged by your manager. Individual conversations in English are easy; getting them scheduled is the barrier.

**Inclusion in the informal channel.** Ask to be added to the group chats where the day-to-day happens, even if they are in Dutch. Reading them with a translation tool is how many people bootstrap both the language and the context.

**A second international hire, eventually.** If the team plans to grow, say plainly that you would welcome not being the only one. Managers rarely think of it unprompted, and it changes the dynamic permanently.

If several of these requests meet resistance, that is useful information about how much the team has thought about working with international colleagues — which is the thing you were trying to find out.

## If You Are Also New to the Country

Being the only international in a team is one situation; arriving in the Netherlands at the same time is a heavier one, and it is worth separating the two.

In the first months your colleagues are also your main source of practical knowledge — how registration with the municipality works, what the health insurance obligation means, which bank account arrangements are normal, how the housing market behaves. In a team where several people have been through it, that knowledge is freely available. In a team where nobody has, every question requires explanation from someone who has never had to think about it.

Ask what relocation support exists and whether anyone in the organisation has handled it before. Larger employers often have a process and an adviser; smaller ones may offer help that is genuine and improvised. Either is workable if you know which it is.

And give the two adjustments separate timelines in your own mind. Professional settling takes a few months; settling in a country takes longer, and confusing the two makes people conclude the job is wrong when what is actually hard is everything around it.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Enschede who asked how long the last one stayed

A data scientist from Spain is offered a role at a manufacturer. The interviews are in English, the manager is welcoming, and the work is genuinely interesting.

She asks whether international colleagues have worked in the team. Two have: one stayed four years and was promoted, one left after eight months. She asks what was different, and the manager answers honestly — the first learned to read Dutch quickly and asked a lot; the second worked mostly remotely and became isolated.

She takes the role, with two things agreed: a language course with two hours a week of work time, and a buddy for the first three months.

The first months are tiring. Meetings are in English until they are not, and she asks for summaries constantly, which feels awkward for about a fortnight and then becomes normal.

At six months she reads Dutch well enough to follow the group channel and the tickets, which she describes as the single change that made her feel part of the team rather than a visitor in it.

Two years on, she leads a project and still speaks Dutch imperfectly. Nobody minds, she says, because she understood what was going on long before she could join in.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch teams switch to English readily and revert under pressure; the work is usually accessible while the surrounding information is not.
- The cost lands in the informal channel, written history, administration and the conversations where projects are allocated.
- Ask whether international colleagues stayed, what language is used outside meetings, and what the team does when a discussion switches.
- Learn to read Dutch first — it is faster than speaking and unlocks documentation, tickets and policy.
- Ask for summaries, invest in one-to-one relationships, make your work visible in writing and build a network beyond the team.

### (Scenario: international candidate weighing an offer) Is being the only international in a team a problem?
Rarely for the work itself, often for everything around it: informal information, written history and the conversations where opportunities are decided. It is manageable when the team is aware of it.

### (Scenario: candidate in interviews) What question reveals the reality?
Not whether the working language is English, but what happened the last time a technical discussion got difficult, and how long previous international colleagues stayed.

### (Scenario: candidate planning to adapt) Should I learn Dutch?
Learning to read is the highest-return step and is achievable in months. Speaking follows later and matters less for access to information.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about awkwardness) Is it acceptable to ask people to switch back?
Yes. Dutch working culture handles direct requests well, and colleagues generally prefer giving you a short summary to having you miss the decision.
