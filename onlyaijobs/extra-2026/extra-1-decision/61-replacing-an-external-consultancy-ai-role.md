The vacancy is unusually specific. The organisation has been buying its data work from an external party for several years, the invoices have grown uncomfortable, and management has decided to build the capability internally. You would be the first, or among the first, and the brief is to take over what the consultancy built. It sounds like ownership handed to you on day one, and sometimes it is. It is also one of the few roles where most of your first year is determined by decisions made before you arrived — in a contract you have not read, in code you have not seen, by people who have no incentive to make your arrival easy.

## Why Organisations Insource

The stated reason is almost always cost, and it is usually genuine: several years of external day rates buy a lot of salary. But the decisive reasons are typically the ones mentioned second.

**Dependency has become uncomfortable.** Nobody internally can answer questions about systems the business now relies on, and that realisation tends to arrive during an incident or an audit.

**Continuity risk.** The consultants who built it have rotated off the account, and the current team is re-learning it as slowly as an internal hire would.

**Regulatory pressure.** In regulated sectors, being able to explain and document your own systems is not optional, and outsourcing the work does not outsource the accountability.

**Speed.** Every change requires a scope discussion, a quote and a planning slot. Internal teams iterate; external contracts do not.

Ask which of these drove the decision. If the answer is only cost, the organisation may not have budgeted for how long a proper transfer takes.

## What You Are Actually Inheriting

**Code you did not write, in a style you would not choose.** Sometimes excellent, sometimes assembled under deadline pressure by three different people over four years.

**Models with undocumented assumptions.** The choices that matter — which population was excluded, why that threshold, what happens in edge cases — often live in nobody's document.

**Infrastructure that may not be yours.** Systems sometimes run in the consultancy's environment, with their tooling and their accounts.

**Relationships.** The business stakeholders trust the consultant who has sat with them for years. That trust transfers to you slowly and only through delivery.

**Expectations set by someone else.** Response times, reporting rhythms and a willingness to accommodate requests that a permanent team cannot sustain.

The last one surprises people. An external party bills for extra work and therefore rarely refuses it. When you arrive with a fixed salary and a fixed week, the same requests become a workload problem, and stakeholders experience the change as a downgrade.

## The Handover Decides Your First Year

Everything depends on how the transfer is arranged, and there are three recognisable versions.

**The clean handover.** The contract runs on in reduced form during a defined transition, the consultancy is paid for knowledge transfer, documentation is a deliverable, and named people are available for questions for a period afterwards. This works.

**The rushed handover.** The contract ends on a date, a few sessions are scheduled, and everything that is not written down leaves with them. Survivable if the systems are simple, painful if they are not.

**The hostile handover.** The consultancy is losing revenue and knows it. Cooperation is minimal and technically compliant. Documentation arrives as a slide deck. This version costs a year.

Ask directly which one is planned, whether knowledge transfer is a paid, contracted obligation, and what happens if it proves inadequate.

## The Contract Questions That Are Really Technical Questions

You are unlikely to read the supplier contract, and you can still ask what it contains.

**Who owns the code and the models?** Ownership of work produced under a services contract depends on what was agreed, and it is not automatic. If the organisation does not own it, your job changes from taking over to rebuilding.

**Where does it run, and on whose accounts?** Migrating out of a supplier's environment is a project of its own, and it is frequently underestimated.

**Which licences and third-party components are involved,** and do they transfer?

**What documentation is contractually owed,** and has anyone checked whether it exists?

**Is there a support obligation after the transition,** and for how long?

An organisation that can answer these has prepared. One that cannot has decided to insource without examining what insourcing means, and you would be the person discovering it.

## The Political Layer

Someone chose the consultancy originally, and that person may still be there. Insourcing implies that the arrangement was expensive or ineffective, which is a judgement on their decision. Occasionally your arrival is unwelcome for reasons that have nothing to do with you.

There is a second dynamic. Business stakeholders who liked their consultant may resist, quietly, by continuing to route requests through the old channel or by comparing your pace unfavourably. This fades with delivery, and it is real for the first months.

Ask who sponsored the decision to insource, how senior they are and whether the affected departments agreed. A change driven by finance alone, over the objections of the teams using the systems, is a harder position than one everyone wanted.

## A Realistic Timeline

**Months one to three.** Reading code, running things, breaking things in a test environment, mapping what exists. Almost no visible output, which is uncomfortable if expectations were set at cost savings from day one.

**Months three to six.** You can maintain what exists. Small improvements start. The first incident you handle alone is the moment stakeholders begin to trust you.

**Months six to twelve.** You start replacing the parts that cannot be maintained, usually beginning with data pipelines rather than models.

**Year two.** The systems become genuinely yours, and you start building things the consultancy would have quoted for.

If management expects the middle of that timeline in the first quarter, say so early. The gap between expectation and reality is the main reason these roles go wrong.

## Where the Career Value Is

This role builds something interviewers find convincing: you took over production systems you did not write, understood them, kept them running and improved them. That is harder than greenfield work and it demonstrates more.

You also learn a specific, portable set of skills — reading unfamiliar code fast, reverse-engineering model logic, writing documentation that did not exist, migrating infrastructure, and managing stakeholders through a change they did not ask for.

The risk is the opposite outcome: a year spent maintaining fragile systems with no budget to improve them, which teaches endurance rather than engineering.

## When It Is Worth Taking

- The handover is contracted, paid and time-boxed, with named people available afterwards.
- The organisation owns the code and the environment, or has a plan and a budget to migrate.
- There is a sponsor with authority who wanted this, not only a finance-led decision.
- You would not be entirely alone — a platform team, engineers, or a second hire planned.
- Management understands that the first quarter produces understanding rather than output.
- There is budget to rebuild what cannot be maintained.

## When to Decline

- The supplier relationship has already ended and nobody can answer questions.
- The organisation does not own what it is asking you to maintain.
- The expectation is that you replace several consultants on your own, immediately.
- Documentation does not exist and nobody is contractually obliged to produce it.
- The decision was made purely on cost, against the wishes of the teams involved.
- You are early in your career; this role rewards experience and punishes its absence.

## How to Structure the First Year

Negotiate the terms of the handover before you accept, not after. Ask for overlap with the outgoing team in writing, and for documentation as a deliverable with a deadline.

Then, in the first weeks, write your own inventory: what exists, what it does, what depends on it, what would break first and what nobody understands. This document is the single most valuable thing you produce in your first quarter, for the organisation and for you.

Stabilise before improving. Resist the temptation to rebuild in month two; you do not yet know why things are the way they are, and some of the strange decisions will turn out to be correct.

Communicate a plan with phases and dates, so that a quiet first quarter reads as progress rather than absence. And keep a record of what you found, because the gap between what was promised at handover and what was actually delivered is information your sponsor needs.

## Real example

### An engineer in Enschede who inherited four years of someone else's work

A machine learning engineer joins a manufacturer that has spent four years buying data work externally. Three models are in production: demand forecasting, quality prediction and a maintenance planner. The consultancy's contract ends two months after he starts.

He asks the handover questions during the interview. The organisation owns the code, which is in their own repository, and the models run on their own cloud environment. Knowledge transfer is contracted: eight sessions and a documentation deliverable, paid, before the contract ends.

The reality is mixed. The sessions happen and are useful. The documentation is thin, and the quality model turns out to depend on a manual file that someone at the consultancy updated monthly — a fact that emerges three weeks after they leave, when the model quietly starts using stale data.

He spends his first quarter writing an inventory and automating that file. The forecasting model he leaves untouched for six months, because it works and he does not yet understand why.

By month nine he has rebuilt the pipelines, retired one model that nobody was using, and improved the maintenance planner with data the consultancy never had access to. The manufacturer's costs are lower, and the systems are explainable for the first time.

His conclusion: the job was ninety percent archaeology and ten percent modelling in year one, and asking about the handover in the interview was what made it survivable.

## Key Takeaways

- Insourcing is usually driven by dependency, continuity risk and speed as much as by cost; ask which reason actually decided it.
- You inherit code, undocumented assumptions, possibly someone else's infrastructure, and expectations set by a supplier who billed for extra work.
- The handover determines your first year: insist it is contracted, paid, time-boxed and includes documentation as a deliverable.
- Ask who owns the code and the environment, what licences transfer and what support obligation survives the contract.
- Expect a quiet first quarter, stabilise before rebuilding, and write your own inventory of what exists and what would break first.
