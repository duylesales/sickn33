The vacancy says machine learning, and the interview reveals something different: the organisation has bought a platform. A supplier provides the models — demand forecasting, document classification, fraud scoring, scheduling — and the role is to make it work here, keep it working and answer for it when it does not. For candidates who trained to build models, this can feel like a demotion dressed up as an AI job. Sometimes it is. Increasingly often it is not, because the difficult part of applied AI in a mid-sized Dutch organisation was never the algorithm, and the people who can make a bought system genuinely work are scarcer than the people who can train one.

## Why Organisations Buy Rather Than Build

The reasoning is usually sound. Building requires a team, a platform, monitoring, retraining, documentation and continuity — a standing commitment most organisations of moderate size cannot staff. A supplier offers a working system next quarter with a support contract behind it.

Regulation has pushed in the same direction. Compliance obligations around AI and personal data are substantial, and buying from a supplier who has done the documentation work is attractive. It does not, however, remove your organisation's own responsibilities: under the AI Act, an organisation using a system has obligations of its own, including in areas such as human oversight, monitoring and using the system in accordance with the instructions provided. Someone internally has to understand and discharge those.

That someone is frequently the role being offered to you.

## The Three Versions of This Role

**The configurator.** You set parameters in an interface, run the supplier's reports and raise tickets when something looks wrong. Little technical depth, little influence, and the least valuable version for a career.

**The owner-integrator.** You build and maintain the data pipelines feeding the system, integrate outputs into the organisation's processes and systems, monitor quality, and hold the supplier to account. Substantial technical work, most of it data engineering and evaluation rather than modelling.

**The evaluator-governor.** You are responsible for whether the system performs acceptably: independent evaluation on your own data, bias and error analysis, human oversight design, documentation for regulators and auditors, and the decision about whether to keep using it. Increasingly common in regulated sectors, and genuinely senior work.

Vacancies for all three describe "working with AI". The interview questions below separate them.

## What This Work Builds That Transfers

**Independent evaluation.** Assessing a system you did not build, on your own data, against a baseline, is a distinct and portable skill. Many organisations discover they have no idea whether their purchased system outperforms the process it replaced.

**Data integration.** Getting internal data into the shape a system expects, reliably and repeatedly, is ordinary data engineering and always in demand.

**Monitoring and drift detection.** The supplier monitors their model in general; nobody but you monitors it on your population.

**Governance literacy.** Impact assessments, human oversight arrangements, logging, documentation, and the ability to explain a system to an auditor, a works council or a regulator. This is a growth area in the Dutch market.

**Supplier management.** Contracts, service levels, escalation, and the ability to tell the difference between a supplier explanation and an answer. Undervalued and useful everywhere.

## What It Does Not Build

Modelling depth. Experimentation. The judgement that comes from having a model fail in a way you are responsible for and having to fix the method rather than the configuration. If you want to be a machine learning engineer or research-oriented scientist, a few years of platform operation will not get you there, and interviewers will notice the gap.

There is also a lock-in risk. Deep expertise in one supplier's product is valuable to organisations using that product and close to worthless elsewhere. Vendor certifications are not a substitute for demonstrable general skill.

## Questions That Reveal Which Version You Are Being Offered

- **Who evaluates whether the system works on our data?** If the answer is "the supplier", the role is configuration.
- **What would I build myself?** Pipelines, integrations, monitoring, reporting — or nothing.
- **Can we see the model's behaviour, or only its outputs?** Determines whether meaningful analysis is possible.
- **Who is accountable if the system produces a wrong or unfair outcome?** Under the AI Act and privacy rules there must be an answer; if nobody knows, that is a finding.
- **What happens at contract renewal, and who advises on it?**
- **Is there any internally built system, or a plan for one?**
- **What does the human oversight arrangement look like in practice?** A serious answer describes who reviews what, and when they override.

## When the Role Is Worth Taking

- You would own evaluation, monitoring and integration rather than a settings screen.
- The organisation operates in a regulated sector, where oversight and documentation work is substantive and transferable.
- The data engineering around the system is real and yours.
- The role includes advising on whether to continue, extend or replace the system, which is genuine influence.
- You want to move towards governance, ML oversight or a lead role where judgement matters more than implementation.
- The alternative is a role with no production systems at all — a running bought system beats a prototype nobody uses.

## When to Decline

- The work is configuration and ticket-raising, with the supplier holding everything meaningful.
- You are early in your career and would learn no engineering practice.
- Nobody can explain what the system is evaluated against.
- The title says machine learning engineer and the work contains none.
- The organisation's plan for the next three years is to buy more of the same, and you want to build.

## How to Keep Your Options Open If You Accept

Build your own baseline. A simple internal model to compare against the purchased system is defensible work, valuable to the organisation and the single best way to keep your modelling skills alive. Frame it as due diligence, because that is what it is.

Own the data layer. Whatever the supplier does, the pipelines, quality checks and integrations can be yours, and they are the most portable part of the role.

Write the evaluation framework. Test sets, metrics, monitoring, an annual review. This becomes both the organisation's memory and your interview material.

Learn the regulatory side properly. Deployer obligations, impact assessments, oversight design and documentation are becoming a specialism in their own right in the Netherlands.

Avoid becoming the product expert only. Certifications in one platform age quickly; general skills do not.

## The Renewal Conversation Is Where the Role Becomes Senior

Every purchased system reaches a contract renewal, and that moment is the most influential one available to the person who runs it. Organisations frequently arrive at it with no evidence, renew out of inertia and repeat the cycle. Bringing evidence changes both the outcome and your standing.

What the conversation needs is not an opinion but a comparison: what the system produces, what the previous process produced, what a simple internal alternative would produce, what the total cost is including your own time and the integration work, and what switching would cost.

Preparing that requires exactly the work worth doing anyway — a retained test period, a baseline, monitoring history and a record of incidents and supplier responses. Start collecting it in your first months, because the year before renewal is too late to reconstruct it.

Three outcomes are common. The system is clearly better and is renewed on better terms, because you now know its weaknesses. The system is adequate for some cases and not others, producing a hybrid where internal work supplements it — often the best result technically and the most interesting for you. Or the system is not justifying itself, and the organisation builds, replaces or stops.

Whichever it is, the person who produced the evidence becomes the person consulted about what happens next. That is a materially different position from the one described in the original vacancy, and it is reachable from inside almost any platform role.

## If You Want to Move Back to Building

Suppose you take the role and, two years later, want to return to building models rather than operating someone else's. That move is entirely possible, provided you have kept three things alive.

**A baseline you built yourself,** with a defensible validation design. One is enough. It answers the question interviewers actually ask.

**The data layer.** Pipelines, quality checks, feature preparation and monitoring are engineering work regardless of who trained the model, and they read as engineering work on a CV.

**A written record of your evaluation practice.** Test set design, metrics, how you detected degradation and what you did about it. This is the part employers find hardest to hire for.

What does not help is a list of platform features you can configure. When you describe the role later, lead with what you built and measured, and treat the supplier's product as the context rather than the subject.

## Real example

### An engineer in Leeuwarden who built the baseline nobody asked for

An engineer joins a housing organisation that has purchased a maintenance prediction system. The vacancy promised machine learning; the reality is a supplier platform with a configuration interface and monthly reports.

Rather than accept the role as defined, he asks a question nobody had asked: how do we know it is better than what we did before? There is no answer, because the supplier's accuracy figures are based on their general model, not on this housing stock.

Over two months he builds a comparison — a simple internal model on historical maintenance data, a shared test period and a clear metric. The purchased system wins on some property types and loses on others, particularly older stock, which turns out to be most of the portfolio.

The finding changes the relationship with the supplier and produces a hybrid arrangement, with internal adjustments layered on the purchased predictions. His role changes with it: evaluation, monitoring and the data pipelines are now his, and he advises on renewal.

Two years later he applies elsewhere. The interview spends almost no time on the platform and a great deal on how he designed the comparison, which is exactly the skill the new employer lacked.

## Key Takeaways

- Buying rather than building is a rational choice for many Dutch organisations, and it creates real internal work rather than removing it.
- The role has three versions — configurator, owner-integrator and evaluator-governor — and only the first is a poor career move.
- Transferable value lies in independent evaluation, data integration, monitoring, governance literacy and supplier management.
- The gaps are modelling depth and experimentation, plus the lock-in risk of becoming an expert in one supplier's product.
- If you accept, build your own baseline, own the data layer, write the evaluation framework and learn the deployer obligations properly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a platform role) Is working with a bought AI system bad for my career?
Not if you own evaluation, integration and monitoring. It is a poor move only when the work is configuration and the supplier holds everything meaningful.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the role) How do I tell which version of the role this is?
Ask who evaluates the system on your data, what you would build yourself, whether model behaviour is visible and who is accountable for wrong outcomes.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about skills) What transfers from this work?
Independent evaluation, data engineering, monitoring and drift detection, governance and documentation, and supplier management.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking about regulation) Does buying a system remove the organisation's responsibility?
No. Organisations using AI systems carry their own obligations, including around human oversight, monitoring and correct use, and someone internally must discharge them.

Build an internal baseline for comparison, own the data layer, keep general skills current and treat vendor certifications as an addition rather than a foundation.
