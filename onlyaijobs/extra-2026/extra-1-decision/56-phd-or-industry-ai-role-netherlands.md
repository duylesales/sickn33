The Dutch version of this decision differs from the one described in most international advice, and the difference matters. Here, a PhD position at a university is normally an employment contract: you are staff, paid under the universities' collective agreement, accruing pension and holiday, with the employment protections that come with a job. It is not an extension of studenthood. That reframes the question. You are not choosing between studying and working — you are choosing between two jobs, one of which pays less now, lasts four years and produces a specific kind of credential, and one of which pays more now and produces production experience.

## What a PhD Position Actually Is Here

A fixed-term employment contract, typically four years, at a university or institute. A salary that increases each year under the collective agreement, holiday allowance, an end-of-year payment and pension accrual. Teaching duties in many positions. A supervisor, a research topic that is sometimes yours and sometimes inherited from a funded project, and a thesis at the end.

Some positions are funded by industry partners, and some are formally shared with a company. These industrial arrangements sit between the two paths described here and are worth asking about specifically, because they often combine research depth with production exposure.

## What Each Path Builds

**A PhD builds depth and independence.** Formulating a problem nobody has solved, designing experiments that could disprove your own hypothesis, reading critically, writing precisely, presenting to people who will argue with you. These are real professional skills, and they transfer further than PhD sceptics claim.

**Industry builds delivery.** Working systems, other people's constraints, engineering practice, stakeholders, maintenance, the difference between a result and a product. These are what most employers assess for, and they are hard to acquire in a university setting.

**A PhD gives you a credential with specific doors attached.** Research roles in industrial labs, applied research institutes, senior positions in regulated and scientific domains, and academia. In parts of the Dutch market — pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, energy research, healthcare, climate, defence-adjacent research — a doctorate is close to a requirement for the most interesting work.

**Industry gives you compounding market position.** Four years of production experience, a network of colleagues who move to other employers, and a salary that has been rising from a higher base throughout.

## The Money, Honestly

A PhD position pays less than an industry role for the same person, and the gap compounds over four years through both salary and pension accrual. Afterwards, doctorate holders often enter at a higher level, but rarely high enough to recover the difference immediately.

So the financial case for a PhD is weak in isolation. It becomes reasonable when the doctorate opens work you could not otherwise do, and it becomes poor when it is undertaken because the industry search felt daunting. That second reason is more common than people admit, and it is the one that produces unhappy third years.

## The Supervision Question Decides More Than the Topic

Ask anyone who has done a PhD what determined their experience, and almost all will say the supervisor rather than the subject.

Before accepting a position, speak to current and former candidates of that supervisor — not with the supervisor present. Ask how often they meet, how quickly drafts are returned, how many candidates finished and in what time, what happened to those who struggled, and whether people leave for industry mid-way.

Ask the supervisor directly how they see the position: an apprenticeship in research, or a pair of hands on a funded project. Both exist. Only one develops you.

Also ask what happens if the research direction fails, because in machine learning it frequently does. A supervisor with a plan for that is a good sign.

## When a PhD Is the Right Choice

- You want research roles, in academia or in an industrial or applied research setting, and have checked that those roles exist near where you want to live.
- The specific topic genuinely interests you enough to survive year three, which is where most people struggle.
- The supervision is demonstrably good.
- You enjoy open problems, ambiguity and long horizons more than shipping.
- Your field effectively requires it for the work you want.
- You can accept four years at a lower salary without strain.

## When Industry Is the Better Choice

- You want to build things people use, and the appeal of research is mainly the technical work rather than the inquiry.
- You are unsure what you want. Industry keeps more doors open, and a PhD can still be started later, sometimes funded by an employer.
- The available positions are on topics you find lukewarm.
- Your financial or family situation makes four years at a reduced salary genuinely difficult.
- You are drawn to the status of the title rather than the work. This is worth being honest with yourself about.

## What Employers Actually Think of a PhD

It varies more than candidates expect, and the variation is predictable.

**Research-heavy employers** treat it as necessary or strongly preferred, and assess your publications and depth.

**Regulated and scientific sectors** treat it as a strong signal of rigour, particularly where methods must withstand external scrutiny.

**Product and platform teams** are generally neutral to mildly positive. They will assess your engineering, and a candidate with four years of production experience often interviews better for those roles.

**Some employers are mildly wary,** from experience with doctorate holders who found delivery work unsatisfying. The answer is easy if you have it: a project you took to production, even during the PhD.

The single best way to protect your options is to do something applied during the doctorate — an internship, a collaboration with a company, a tool other researchers actually use, code that someone else runs.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting a PhD Position

- **Is the topic defined, or would I shape it?**
- **How much teaching is expected?**
- **Who else is in the group, and at what stage?** A group with senior candidates and postdocs is a much better learning environment than an isolated position.
- **What is the completion rate and the average duration?**
- **Is there funding for conferences, compute and collaboration?**
- **Are industry collaborations possible?**
- **What have recent graduates from this group gone on to do?**

## What the Four Years Actually Look Like

Candidates imagine the first year and the title. The shape of the middle is what determines whether people finish well.

**Year one.** Reading, orientation, a great deal of uncertainty about what your contribution will be. Often teaching duties alongside. Productive-feeling work is rare, which is disorienting for anyone arriving from a structured environment.

**Year two.** The first real results, the first submission, often the first rejection. This is where most candidates learn that the work is not mainly technical: it is framing a question so that an answer means something, and defending it against people who will look for holes.

**Year three.** The hardest year, consistently. The novelty is gone, peers from your degree are earning considerably more and buying houses, and the thesis is still abstract. People who have a collaboration, an internship, a teaching role they enjoy or a good group around them get through it more easily than those working alone on a difficult topic.

**Year four.** Writing, pressure, and frequently a funding cliff, since contracts and completion rarely align neatly. Ask in advance what happens if you need longer, because the answer varies by institution and by supervisor.

Two structural realities are worth knowing. Completion often takes longer than the contract, and machine learning moves fast enough that a topic can be overtaken by external work mid-way. Neither is a reason to avoid a doctorate. Both are reasons to choose a group with a plan, a supervisor who responds quickly and a topic with more than one viable direction.

## Keeping the Industry Door Open While You Do It

If you take the PhD, a few habits cost little and preserve everything.

**Ship something.** Code others run, a tool used beyond your group, a model deployed with a partner. One such item answers the delivery question that follows doctorate holders into interviews.

**Do an industry collaboration or internship,** ideally in year two or three. It gives you a network, a reality check and often the role you take afterwards.

**Keep engineering standards.** Version control, tests, reproducibility. Research code has a reputation, and being the exception is noticed.

**Talk to people outside academia regularly.** Meetups, conferences with industrial tracks, former colleagues. Networks decay quietly over four years.

**Write for people who are not specialists.** The ability to explain your work to a business audience is what converts a doctorate from a credential into an advantage.

## Real example

### A graduate in Delft who asked the previous candidates first

A master's graduate has two offers: a PhD position on efficient models for edge devices, and a machine learning engineer role at a Dutch technology company paying considerably more.

He almost accepts the industry role for the salary. Instead he contacts three former candidates of the supervisor. All three finished, two within four and a half years, and all three describe weekly meetings and drafts returned in days. One now works in an industrial research lab; the others are in industry, both saying the doctorate opened their roles.

He takes the PhD, with two conditions he negotiates himself: a six-month industry collaboration in year three, and an agreement that his code will be released openly.

Year three is as hard as everyone warned. The collaboration is what carries him through it, and it also produces the deployment experience the doctorate would not have.

He now works in an applied research group, on problems he could not have reached without the title. His view is that the decision was never PhD versus industry, but this supervisor versus that manager — and that he only found out which was which by asking the people who had already been there.

## Key Takeaways

- In the Netherlands a PhD position is a paid job with a contract, pension and collective agreement terms, so the choice is between two jobs rather than between study and work.
- A PhD builds depth, independence and a credential with specific doors; industry builds delivery, engineering practice and a compounding market position.
- The financial case for a doctorate is weak in isolation and reasonable when it opens work you could not otherwise do.
- Supervision determines the experience more than the topic — speak to current and former candidates before accepting.
- Do something applied during the doctorate to keep industry options fully open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: graduate weighing offers) Is a PhD worth it for an AI career in the Netherlands?
It is worth it when the roles you want require or strongly prefer one, and when the supervision is good. As a general career accelerator it is weaker than four years of production experience.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing pay) How does the money compare?
A PhD position pays less than an equivalent industry role, and the gap compounds through salary and pension over four years. Later entry at a higher level rarely recovers it immediately.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing a position) What matters most in choosing a PhD position?
Supervision. Speak to current and former candidates without the supervisor present, and ask about meeting frequency, feedback speed, completion rates and what happens when research fails.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about industry options) Does a PhD hurt my chances in industry?
Generally not, though product teams assess engineering rather than publications. Doing something applied during the doctorate removes most of the doubt.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure what they want) What if I cannot decide?
Industry keeps more options open, and a doctorate can be started later, sometimes with employer funding or as an industrial arrangement.
