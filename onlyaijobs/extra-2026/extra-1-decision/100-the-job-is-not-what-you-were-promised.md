You accepted a role to build models and you are writing reports. You were told the data existed and it does not. The project you were hired for was cancelled two weeks before you started and nobody mentioned it. The manager who convinced you has moved on. Sometimes the mismatch is obvious in week one; more often it emerges slowly enough that you spend two months wondering whether you are being unreasonable. This is common, it is rarely anyone's deliberate deception, and it is one of the few situations where acting early is both easier and more effective than waiting — which is precisely the opposite of what most people do.

## First, Establish Which Kind of Mismatch This Is

The right response depends entirely on the cause, and the causes look similar from the inside.

**Organisational change.** A reorganisation, a cancelled project, a departed sponsor, a budget that moved. Nobody misled you; the ground shifted. Frequently recoverable.

**Optimism at hiring.** The role was described as the employer hoped it would be rather than as it was. The data was going to be ready, the platform was going to be built, the team was going to grow. Recoverable only if the intention was genuine and is still alive.

**A gap nobody noticed.** The manager and the recruiter described different jobs, and you heard the one you wanted. Uncomfortable and common, and usually resolvable by a direct conversation.

**Deliberate misrepresentation.** Rare, and it happens: a role sold as strategic that was always operational support, or promises that were never plausible. Not recoverable, and the right response is to leave.

**Your own adjustment.** Sometimes the job is what was described and the first months of any job feel wrong. Worth ruling out honestly before concluding anything.

## What You Actually Agreed

Go back to the written offer and the contract. This is the moment when everything you had put in writing at the negotiation earns its value — scope, first project, training budget, working arrangement, reporting line.

Where a commitment is written, the conversation is simple: this was agreed, it is not happening, how do we fix it. Where it was only spoken, you are relying on memory and goodwill, which is a weaker position and not a hopeless one.

Note the distinction between a term of employment and an expectation. Your contract may specify salary, hours and function without describing the work in detail, and an employer generally has latitude to direct the work within the agreed function. A role that has drifted from modelling into reporting may not breach anything formally, and it can still be a legitimate reason to leave.

## Have the Conversation Early

The instinct is to wait — to be sure, to avoid seeming difficult, to give it a chance. Waiting makes it harder, because expectations settle and your position becomes the norm.

Have the conversation at around eight to twelve weeks. By then you know enough to be specific and it is early enough that adjustment is still easy.

Make it concrete rather than emotional. Name what was agreed, describe what is happening, and ask what can be done. "We discussed that I would own the forecasting work and lead a second hire. I am spending most of my week on reporting requests. Can we look at how to get to the original scope, and by when?"

Ask for a timeline, not a reassurance. "It will settle down" is not a plan; "the reporting moves to the analytics team in April" is.

And write a short summary afterwards, sent to your manager. Not as a threat — as a record both of you can refer to, which is exactly what was missing the first time.

## What Reasonable Employers Do

Most respond well, because a new hire who is visibly mismatched is expensive and embarrassing. Expect one of three outcomes.

**Correction.** Work is reassigned, the original scope is restored, a plan is agreed. Common when the cause was organisational change.

**A dated commitment.** The situation cannot change now, and here is when it will. Acceptable if the date is specific and the person making it will still be there.

**Honesty that the role is what it is.** Disappointing and useful. It converts an open question into a decision you can make.

An employer who becomes defensive, or treats the question as a lack of commitment, has given you a fourth answer.

## If Nothing Changes: Your Options

**Adapt deliberately.** Decide that the job as it exists is worth doing, and stop measuring it against the one you were promised. This is a legitimate choice when the work is decent and the alternative is disruption.

**Stay with a horizon.** Give it six or nine months, extract what is valuable — a production system, a domain, a qualification — and plan to move on your own terms. Most people who do this leave well.

**Leave during probation.** Where a probation period is still running, either side can end the contract immediately. It is the cleanest exit available and the least costly to explain.

**Resign with notice.** Ordinary, professional and slower. Check your notice period and leave with a proper handover.

Whichever you choose, choose it. The outcome to avoid is drifting for two years in a role you never wanted while telling yourself it might improve.

## The Dutch Practicalities

**Probation.** If a valid probation period applies, it is a short window and it is genuinely mutual. Mismatch is exactly what it exists for, and using it is not a failure.

**Notice.** After probation, your notice period applies — often one month, sometimes longer if agreed in writing. Check the contract.

**References.** Dutch employers rarely give detailed references, and the community is small enough that impressions travel informally. Leaving properly matters more than the length of time you stayed.

**Advice.** If you believe terms are being breached, take advice before acting. Your union, a legal expenses insurer or the free **Juridisch Loket** are the usual routes.

## How to Explain It Later

This worries people more than it should. A short tenure explained plainly is a minor item; a short tenure explained defensively becomes the subject of the interview.

Say what happened in one or two sentences, without criticism of the employer: the project I was hired for was cancelled before I started, the role became reporting support, I raised it, we agreed it could not change, and I decided to move rather than spend two years away from the work I do.

Interviewers hear this regularly, and what they are actually assessing is whether you raised it professionally and left properly. Both of which are within your control, starting now.

## When to Give It More Time

- The cause was organisational and a credible correction is under way.
- You are in the first eight weeks and still adjusting.
- The team and manager are good, and the work is temporarily wrong.
- There is a dated commitment from someone with the authority to keep it.
- You would gain something specific by staying six more months.

## When to Leave Quickly

- You were deliberately misled.
- The role is fundamentally different and the employer says it will not change.
- The organisation is in a state — reorganisation, ownership change, departures — that makes any commitment unreliable.
- Your manager reacts badly to a reasonable, specific conversation.
- Staying would mean two years away from the work you want, at a cost your career cannot absorb.

## What This Teaches You for the Next Offer

Whatever you decide, the situation is worth converting into a checklist, because almost every mismatch traces back to something that could have been established beforehand.

**Get the scope in writing.** Not the job title — the first project, the systems you would own, who you report to, what success looks like in six months. Employers write this readily when asked and almost never volunteer it.

**Ask what happened to your predecessor,** and how long they stayed. The most informative question in any process, and the one candidates skip most often.

**Ask how secure the sponsorship is.** Which director wants this role to exist, how long have they been there, and what happens to the work if they leave. Roles built on one person's enthusiasm are the ones that change first.

**Speak to a future colleague without a manager present.** Ask what they would change and what the last difficult period was like.

**Ask what is already in production,** rather than what is planned. Plans describe intentions; running systems describe capability.

**Convert every verbal commitment into a written one,** even informally, in a short email confirming what was agreed. Nobody objects, and it is what makes the conversation at ten weeks straightforward rather than a contest of memories.

None of this guarantees the role will be what it was described as — reorganisations and cancellations happen to careful people too. It does mean that when the ground shifts, you will know exactly what was agreed, which is the difference between a negotiation and a disagreement.

## Real example

### An engineer in Haarlem who raised it at ten weeks

An engineer joins a services company to build a demand forecasting capability. Three weeks in, the sponsoring director leaves and the forecasting project is paused. He is asked to help with reporting "for a while".

At ten weeks he asks for a conversation. He brings the offer letter, which specifies the forecasting scope, and describes how his week is actually spent. He asks two questions: can the forecasting work restart, and if not, when will the reporting move elsewhere.

His manager is honest. The forecasting project needs a new sponsor and there is no candidate; the reporting is now the team's main deliverable for the year.

They agree a middle path with a date: he takes one forecasting problem for the planning team, which does have a sponsor, and the reporting is shared across the team from April.

April arrives and the reporting is not shared. He gives notice in May, works the month properly, documents everything and leaves on good terms.

In interviews afterwards he explains it in two sentences. Nobody probes it. The forecasting work he insisted on taking, small as it was, is what he talks about instead.

## Key Takeaways

- Establish the cause first: organisational change, hiring optimism, a gap nobody noticed, deliberate misrepresentation, or your own adjustment — the response differs for each.
- Go back to the written offer; commitments in writing make the conversation simple, and the absence of them is the lesson for next time.
- Have the conversation at eight to twelve weeks, make it specific, ask for a dated plan rather than reassurance, and summarise it in writing afterwards.
- If nothing changes, choose deliberately between adapting, staying with a horizon, leaving during probation or resigning with notice — the outcome to avoid is drifting.
- A short tenure explained plainly is a minor item on a CV; what interviewers assess is whether you raised it professionally and left properly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: new joiner three months in) How long should I wait before raising it?
Around eight to twelve weeks. Early enough that adjustment is easy, late enough that you can be specific about what is actually happening rather than about how it feels.

### (Scenario: employee with verbal promises) What if the promises were never written down?
You are relying on goodwill, which often works. Be specific about what was discussed, ask for a dated plan, and write a short summary afterwards so a record exists from now on.

### (Scenario: employee considering leaving) Is leaving during probation a bad idea?
No. A probation period is mutual and exists for exactly this situation. It is the cleanest and least costly exit available, and easier to explain than a year spent in the wrong role.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about their CV) How will a short tenure look?
Minor, if explained plainly in one or two sentences without criticising the employer. Interviewers hear this often and are assessing how you handled it, not how long you stayed.

### (Scenario: employee unsure whether to stay) When is it worth staying?
When the cause was organisational and a credible, dated correction is under way, when the team and manager are good, or when six more months would give you something specific and portable.
