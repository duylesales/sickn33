After several years in which remote work was assumed, a growing number of Dutch vacancies specify daily presence. Sometimes the reason is unavoidable: the data is on a machine you cannot reach from home, the process you are modelling is physical, or the environment is restricted for security or clinical reasons. Sometimes the reason is preference dressed as necessity. The distinction matters, because full presence is the single largest ongoing cost an employer can attach to a job — not in money, but in hours of your life, repeated every week for years.

## Where On-Site Is Genuinely Required

**Physical processes.** Manufacturing, agriculture, logistics hubs, energy installations. If you are modelling a production line or a sorting process, watching it is not optional, and the people who understand it are there.

**Restricted environments.** Some data cannot leave the premises: clinical systems, defence-adjacent work, certain financial and public-sector environments, anything air-gapped. Where access is the constraint, no amount of policy discussion changes it.

**Instrumented laboratories.** Work tied to specific equipment, sensors or test setups.

**Sites with poor connectivity** or systems that exist only on internal networks.

**Early-stage teams with nothing written down,** where presence substitutes for documentation. This is a real reason and a temporary one; ask whether it is expected to change.

## Where It Is Preference

**"Culture" and "collaboration"** without specifics. Usually genuine belief rather than cynicism, and still not a technical requirement.

**Management discomfort with distance.** An organisation that measures presence because it cannot measure output.

**Uniformity.** Everyone else is on site, so you must be too, regardless of whether your work needs it.

**A recent policy change** made after an executive read something. Ask when the policy changed and what preceded it.

The Dutch legal position is relevant here: under the **Flexible Working Act**, employees of employers with at least ten employees can request a change of workplace, though employers retain more discretion for workplace requests than for hours. In practice, presence is a matter of what is agreed, so agree it before you start rather than assuming flexibility appears later.

## What Daily Presence Actually Gives You

It is worth being fair about this, because the advantages are real and under-argued.

**You see the thing the data describes.** Standing at the line, watching an operator override the system, noticing that the sensor is mounted badly — none of this reaches you through a dashboard. In industrial and clinical settings, the difference in model quality is substantial.

**Informal knowledge arrives continuously.** Why a shift records things differently, which supplier's deliveries are always wrong, what happened in the incident everyone still talks about.

**Trust builds faster.** Stakeholders who see you regularly bring you problems earlier, which is how data work becomes useful rather than requested.

**Onboarding is far easier,** particularly in complex domains.

**Boundaries are clearer.** Work stays at work, which some people value highly after years of the opposite.

## What It Costs

**Time, compounding.** An hour each way is roughly ten hours a week, around four hundred a year. Over three years that is the equivalent of several months of waking life.

**Flexibility that families rely on.** School runs, a sick child, a delivery, a medical appointment — all become logistics rather than adjustments.

**Weather and rail.** In a country where many people commute by train and bicycle, a difficult winter or a disrupted line is a recurring tax on your week.

**Geographic narrowing.** A fully on-site role restricts you to employers within a radius you are willing to travel daily, which is a smaller set than candidates assume.

**Energy for deep work.** Open-plan offices are not always where concentration happens, and arriving tired reduces the hours that matter most.

## Do the Commute Arithmetic Honestly

Measure the real journey at the real times: door to desk, in the morning peak, not the map estimate. Add the return. Multiply by five, then by forty-six weeks.

Then ask what compensates. A higher salary that works out at a modest hourly rate for your travel time is not compensation; a role that genuinely advances your career might be. A journey you can use — a train where you read or work — is meaningfully different from one spent driving.

Check the travel arrangements too: what is reimbursed, whether a public transport pass is provided, whether travel time counts in any way. These vary widely between Dutch employers and are negotiable more often than candidates realise.

## Questions to Ask

- **Why is presence required?** Listen for a technical reason or a cultural one.
- **Which parts of the work genuinely need the site, and which do not?**
- **Is any flexibility possible — a day a fortnight, occasional home working for focus, flexibility in exceptional weeks?**
- **What are the core hours, and how rigid are they?** Shifting your day by an hour can transform a commute.
- **What is reimbursed, and is a public transport pass provided?**
- **Do others have arrangements?** If exceptions exist, the policy is negotiable.
- **Is this expected to change?** Some on-site requirements are transitional, tied to a migration, a security review or an early-stage team.

## When It Is Worth It

- The work genuinely requires proximity, and that proximity would make you better at it.
- The commute is short, or usable.
- The domain is one you want to learn deeply, and being present is how that happens.
- You prefer the separation between work and home.
- The employer offers something concrete in exchange: flexible start times, a compressed arrangement, real travel reimbursement.
- The role would be materially worse remotely, which you can articulate yourself.

## When to Decline

- The requirement is cultural and the work is plainly location-independent.
- The commute exceeds what you would accept in year three, not just in month one.
- Core hours are rigid and the journey is at peak times.
- Your household depends on flexibility that the role removes.
- No exceptions exist for anyone, which suggests none will exist for you either.
- Comparable work exists closer to home — which is worth checking before deciding rather than after.

## How to Make It Work If You Accept

Negotiate the edges rather than the principle. Employers who cannot offer home working can often offer flexible start and finish times, which is frequently worth more, because it moves your journey out of the peak.

Ask about a compressed arrangement: four longer days rather than five, where the work allows it. This is common in the Netherlands and halves a week's commuting cost in one step.

Make the journey productive or restorative deliberately — reading, a podcast, a walk at one end. A commute you have decided how to use is a different experience from one you endure.

And revisit it. Arrangements that are impossible at hiring sometimes become possible after a year of trust, and the conversation is much easier once you have delivered something.

## The Site Itself Matters More Than the Policy

If you will be there every day, the physical reality of the place deserves the same scrutiny as the job description. Candidates inspect the team and never look at the building.

**Where would you actually sit?** A desk in an open-plan office beside a call centre is a different working life from a quiet corner near the engineers. Ask to see the desk, not the reception area.

**Can you reach the thing you are modelling?** In industrial and clinical settings, the whole argument for presence is access. Ask whether you would be allowed onto the floor, into the lab, alongside the process — some sites keep office staff firmly separated, in which case you have the commute without the benefit.

**Where are your actual colleagues?** A surprising number of on-site roles put you in a building where your team is not: the data people sit at head office, the engineers at another location, and you would be video-calling from a different site every day. That is the worst combination available — full presence, remote collaboration.

**How does the journey end?** Parking, secure bicycle storage, the distance from the station, the last kilometre in November rain. These small frictions are what make or break a five-day commute over years.

**What is the daily rhythm?** When people arrive and leave, whether lunch is communal, whether anything happens after hours. This determines how much of the informal knowledge you actually get, which was the point of being there.

Ask to visit at a normal working time rather than for a scheduled interview slot. Employers who are comfortable with that request are usually the ones with nothing to hide about the environment.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Zeeland who chose the factory over the office

A data scientist is offered two roles. One is a hybrid position at a services company, two days on site, fifty-five minutes each way. The other is fully on-site at a food producer twenty minutes from her home, working on production quality.

She expects to prefer the hybrid role and does the arithmetic anyway. The hybrid role's two days cost her nearly four hours of travel a week; the on-site role costs three and a half, for five days of presence.

She takes the on-site role. Within two months she has learned more about the process than any dashboard would have shown her: a scale that drifts after cleaning, two shifts that record rejects differently, an operator workaround that explained an anomaly nobody had been able to trace.

The quality model she builds is better because of things she saw rather than measured. She also negotiates a flexible start time, arriving at half past seven and leaving early enough to avoid the worst of the traffic.

Her view: being on site is only expensive when the site is far away, and the real decision was about distance rather than about presence.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate genuine requirements — physical processes, restricted data, instrumented labs — from cultural preference expressed as necessity.
- Daily presence genuinely improves work in industrial, clinical and early-stage settings, where informal knowledge and seeing the process matter.
- The cost is time compounding over years, lost household flexibility, weather and rail disruption, and a much smaller set of employers within reach.
- Do the arithmetic on the real journey at real times, and check what is reimbursed and whether travel time is recognised.
- Negotiate the edges — flexible start times, compressed days, occasional exceptions — which employers can often grant even when home working is impossible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered an on-site role) Is a fully on-site AI role a bad sign?
Not necessarily. In manufacturing, clinical and restricted environments, presence is a genuine requirement and often makes the work better. It is a warning when the work is plainly location-independent.

### (Scenario: candidate weighing the commute) How should I assess the travel?
Measure the real door-to-desk journey at peak times, multiply across the year, and ask whether the journey is usable. A short on-site commute can cost less than a long hybrid one.

### (Scenario: candidate hoping for flexibility) Can I ask for home working later?
You can request a change of workplace under the Flexible Working Act, though employers retain considerable discretion. Agree what you need before starting rather than assuming it will loosen.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) What can I ask for if home working is impossible?
Flexible start and finish times to avoid peak travel, a compressed four-day arrangement where the work allows, travel reimbursement or a transport pass, and occasional exceptions for focused work.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing options) What is the strongest argument for being on site?
Seeing the process the data describes. In industrial and clinical settings, informal observation regularly explains anomalies that no amount of analysis would have uncovered.
