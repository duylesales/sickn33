Some data teams work at a steady pace all year. Others live on a cycle: everything builds towards a period of weeks when the organisation makes most of its money or carries most of its risk, and the rest of the year is shaped around preparing for it and recovering from it. In the Netherlands this covers a large share of interesting applied work — retail and e-commerce in the run-up to December, horticulture and agriculture through the growing season, energy through winter, tax and financial reporting at fixed deadlines, insurance after storms, tourism and hospitality through summer. The work is often excellent. The annual rhythm is the thing to understand before you sign, because it decides when you can take leave, when you can learn and when your evenings belong to you.

## What the Peak Does to a Data Team

**Change freezes.** In most peak-driven organisations, nothing goes to production for a period before and during the peak. Freezes can last weeks, and everything you wanted to ship must be ready before it starts.

**On-call and standby.** Someone must be reachable when a pipeline fails at the worst possible moment. Ask how this is arranged and compensated.

**All hands on the operational problem.** Roadmap work stops. If forecasting is wrong, the whole team works on forecasting.

**Higher stakes on the same models.** A demand forecast that is adequate in March can be expensive in December, and the scrutiny rises accordingly.

**Leave restrictions.** The peak is usually when nobody may be away, which frequently coincides with school holidays or the end of the year.

## The Upside People Underestimate

**Clarity.** During the peak, priorities are obvious and decisions are fast. Many people find this the most satisfying period of their year.

**The quiet season is genuinely quiet.** A well-run cyclical organisation uses the trough for platform work, refactoring, experimentation and training — the things that never happen in teams with a constant drip of requests.

**Forecasting that matters.** In peak businesses, demand forecasting, capacity planning and pricing are core rather than decorative, and the feedback loop is fast and unambiguous.

**An annual rhythm to plan a life around.** If you know December is intense and February is calm, you can arrange the rest accordingly, which is not true in organisations where the pressure is random.

**Measurable impact.** Peak performance is counted in detail, so improvements are visible in a way most data work is not.

## The Downside to Take Seriously

**Leave at the wrong time of year.** If the peak covers Christmas, your ability to travel when family expects it may be limited for years.

**Recovery is real.** After an intense peak, teams are tired, and the weeks afterwards are less productive than the plan assumes.

**Freezes block learning.** A two-month freeze is two months without shipping, which is dull and slows your development.

**The cycle repeats.** The third December is the same as the first. Some people find the rhythm reassuring; others find it a treadmill.

**Peaks expose everything.** Whatever is fragile in the platform will fail under load, and it will fail when it costs most.

## The Leave Question, Concretely

This deserves a direct conversation rather than an assumption.

Dutch rules give employees considerable say over when they take holiday: as a general principle an employer sets leave in line with the employee's wishes unless there are serious business reasons to refuse, and refusals must be raised within a set period after the request. In peak-driven organisations, "serious business reasons" is exactly what a peak provides, and many employers have an explicit policy or a provision in a collective agreement.

Ask what the policy is, how it is applied, and how the team handles people with school-age children when the peak coincides with school holidays. Employers who run a blackout period usually have a fair system for it; those who improvise generate resentment every year.

## What the Quiet Period Is Really Used For

This is the question that separates good cyclical employers from exhausting ones.

In a well-run organisation, the trough is planned: a list of platform improvements, the refactoring that the peak revealed as necessary, training, documentation and the experiments nobody had time for. People come back from the peak, recover, and build.

In a badly run one, the trough is consumed by everything that was deferred during the peak plus the projects that were postponed from last year, and the quiet period never arrives.

Ask what the team did in the last off-season, specifically. The answer predicts your year more reliably than any description of culture.

## Questions to Ask

- **When exactly is the peak, and how long does it last?**
- **What is the freeze period, and what are the rules?**
- **How does standby work during the peak, and how is it compensated?**
- **What is the leave policy around the peak?**
- **What did the team build in the last off-season?**
- **What broke during the last peak, and was it fixed?**
- **How much of the year is peak-related work in total?** Including preparation and recovery, this is often more than candidates expect.
- **Does the peak vary?** Weather-driven businesses have less predictable cycles than calendar-driven ones.

## When It Suits You

- You like intensity in bursts and quiet periods for deep work.
- Your life outside work can flex around a known annual pattern.
- You want forecasting, planning or pricing work where the stakes are real.
- The organisation genuinely uses the off-season for building.
- Standby is organised and compensated properly.
- The peak does not collide with something in your life that cannot move.

## When to Decline

- The peak covers a period when you need to be away, every year.
- The off-season is consumed by deferred work, so there is no building phase.
- Standby is informal and unpaid.
- The platform is fragile and nobody is allowed time to fix it.
- Freezes last months and there is no meaningful work during them.
- You need steady, predictable hours for reasons that will not change.

## How to Work With the Cycle

Front-load everything. Anything that must ship should be done well before the freeze, because the last week before a freeze is where mistakes are made.

Use the run-up to make things observable. Monitoring added in October is what lets you sleep in December.

Plan your own development into the trough deliberately — a course, a refactor, a new area — and get it agreed before the peak, because after it everyone is tired and the calendar fills.

Write the peak retrospective while it is fresh. What broke, what was manual, what nearly failed. In cyclical organisations this document is the single most valuable artefact of the year, and almost nobody writes it.

And take your recovery seriously. The organisation will be ready to start the next cycle before you are.

## What the Peak Weeks Actually Look Like

Descriptions of "an intense period" are vague enough to hide very different realities, so ask what the days are like.

**Monitoring becomes the job.** Dashboards watched in real time, alerts that matter, and a shared channel where problems surface within minutes rather than at the next stand-up.

**Decisions compress.** Questions that normally take a week of analysis get answered in an afternoon, with the data available rather than the data you would like. This is genuinely good training in judgement and uncomfortable if you prefer thoroughness.

**Fixes are tactical by design.** During a peak you patch; you do not refactor. Writing down what you patched, as you patch it, is what makes the off-season productive rather than archaeological.

**The whole organisation is watching the same numbers.** Forecast accuracy stops being a metric in a report and becomes a subject of daily conversation with people who have strong opinions.

**Hours stretch, within limits.** Working time rules on maximum hours and rest apply during a peak as at any other time, and an organisation that plans properly staffs for it rather than relying on people absorbing it. Ask how the last peak was staffed, and whether anyone took time off in lieu afterwards.

**Then it stops.** The transition from maximum intensity to nothing is abrupt, and teams that plan a recovery week handle it far better than teams that start the next project immediately.

Ask someone who worked the last peak to describe a single day. The answer is more informative than any policy, and people generally enjoy telling that story.

## Real example

### An engineer in Zaandam who asked what happened in February

An engineer is offered a role at an e-commerce company where the last quarter of the year is most of the business. The forecasting work is interesting and the peak is openly described as intense.

She asks two questions. What did the team build between January and June last year? And what broke during the last peak?

The answers are good. The team spent the first half of the year rebuilding the demand forecasting pipeline and adding monitoring, precisely because two failures during the previous peak had been discovered by the commercial team rather than by anyone technical.

She also asks about leave. There is a blackout from mid-November to the end of December, applied to everyone, with an explicit arrangement that people who work through it take extended leave in January or February.

She accepts. The first peak is harder than expected and the January leave is genuinely honoured. The retrospective she writes in early January becomes the basis for the next off-season's work.

Three years in, she says the rhythm suits her better than the steady pressure of her previous job — and that the blackout period is the thing she would check first if she ever moved again.

## Key Takeaways

- Peak-driven organisations — retail, horticulture, energy, tax, insurance, tourism — shape the whole year around a period of weeks, including freezes, standby and leave restrictions.
- The upside is clarity, high-stakes forecasting work, measurable impact and a genuinely quiet building season, if the organisation uses it.
- The downside is leave at the wrong time of year, freezes that block shipping, fatigue after the peak and an annual repetition some people find wearing.
- Dutch rules give employees considerable say over holiday timing, and a peak is exactly the kind of serious business reason that permits restrictions — so ask about the policy explicitly.
- Ask what the team built in the last off-season and what broke in the last peak; those two answers predict your year better than any description of culture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a role in a peak business) Is a seasonal-cycle role harder than a steady one?
Not harder overall, but differently distributed: intense weeks with freezes and standby, and a quieter season that good employers use for building and training.

### (Scenario: candidate with family plans) Can my employer refuse leave during the peak?
Dutch rules generally require employers to follow employees' wishes on holiday timing unless there are serious business reasons, and a peak qualifies. Ask for the policy and how it is applied.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the team) What should I ask to judge the organisation?
What the team built in the last off-season and what broke during the last peak. Together they show whether the quiet period is used or consumed.

### (Scenario: candidate concerned about development) Do freezes hold back my learning?
They can. Ask how long the freeze lasts and what work happens during it, and plan your own development into the trough with agreement in advance.

### (Scenario: candidate weighing the work) What kind of data work dominates?
Forecasting, capacity planning, pricing and operational monitoring, with unusually fast and unambiguous feedback because peak performance is measured in detail.
