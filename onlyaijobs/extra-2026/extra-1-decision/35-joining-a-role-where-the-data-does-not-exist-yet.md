The interview goes well until you ask to see some data. There is a pause, then an explanation: the figures live in the ERP system, some of it is in spreadsheets maintained by one person in planning, the machines log to a local database nobody has exported for years, and the customer records are in a package the supplier manages. Everyone is friendly, the budget is real and the ambition is genuine. What they do not have is anything you could train a model on next month. Taking this job is either the best career decision available to you or two years you will struggle to describe in your next interview, and the difference is visible in advance if you ask the right questions.

## Why This Situation Is So Common in the Netherlands

A large share of Dutch employers with genuinely valuable data problems are small and medium-sized companies, regional manufacturers, care organisations, water boards, wholesalers and family businesses. They digitalised operations years ago, but for running the business rather than for analysis. Their data exists and is not accessible; it is accurate in the operational system and inconsistent across systems; nobody has ever had the job of making it usable.

At the same time, the AI Act's AI literacy obligation, sector pressure and visible competitors have pushed many of these organisations to hire their first data professional. The ambition arrives before the foundation, which is not a failure of leadership so much as the normal order of events.

## The Two Very Different Versions of This Role

**The foundational role.** The organisation understands that the first year is plumbing: getting access, building pipelines, defining metrics, establishing trust in numbers. They have budgeted for it, someone senior is accountable, and the first modelling project is planned for later. This is a genuinely good job, and the experience is portable everywhere.

**The disappointment.** The organisation expects AI within six months, has not resourced data engineering, cannot grant access without a third-party supplier's cooperation, and treats the foundation work as an unexpected delay rather than the plan. Here you spend a year apologising for the absence of results you were never resourced to produce.

The words used in the vacancy are identical. The difference is entirely in the answers to a handful of questions.

## The Questions That Separate Them

- **Can I see a sample of the data in the first interview round?** Willingness matters more than quality. An employer who cannot show you anything after two conversations may not have access themselves.
- **Who owns the source systems, and are they internal or supplier-managed?** An external supplier controlling your data access is the single most common blocker, and it is expensive to resolve.
- **What is the budget for data engineering and infrastructure, separate from my salary?** A data hire without tooling is a person with a laptop and good intentions.
- **What has been tried before, and what happened?** A failed proof of concept is useful history, not a warning, provided someone learned from it.
- **Who is accountable for the first year's outcome — me, or a sponsor?** If the answer is only you, the risk is entirely yours too.
- **What would you consider success after twelve months?** "A model in production" from an organisation with no data foundation is a mismatch you can hear immediately. "Reliable weekly figures everyone trusts" is a sign of realism.
- **Is anyone internally able to review my technical work?** If not, will they fund an external reviewer or mentor?

## What the Work Actually Looks Like

If you take a foundational role, expect the first year to be dominated by activities that are valuable and unglamorous: negotiating access with suppliers, mapping which system holds the authoritative version of which fact, discovering that two departments define the same term differently, building the first pipelines, writing documentation nobody has ever had, and producing a small number of reliable numbers that people slowly begin to trust.

Modelling, when it starts, is often simpler than you expected and more useful than anyone predicted, because the constraint was never algorithmic sophistication.

The organisational skill matters as much as the technical one. You will need domain experts to explain processes, IT to grant access, a supplier to deliver an export and a sponsor to protect the time. Data professionals who enjoy that kind of work thrive in these roles; those who want to spend their days on modelling find them exhausting.

## Why It Can Be an Excellent Career Move

**You build something that did not exist.** That is a stronger interview story than incremental improvements to a mature platform.

**You see the whole chain.** Problem definition, access, modelling, deployment, adoption. Most people specialise into one segment for years.

**Your influence is disproportionate.** The decisions you make about definitions, architecture and governance shape how the organisation uses data for a decade.

**Foundational skills are portable.** Every employer needs someone who can make messy data usable; far fewer need another person who can tune a model.

**These roles are frequently near where you live.** Regional employers hiring their first data professional are spread across the country rather than concentrated in a few cities.

## Why It Can Be a Trap

**Nothing to show.** If the year produces pipelines nobody uses and a report that changed no decision, the experience is hard to describe convincingly.

**Isolation.** No peer to review your work, no one to learn from, no correction when you develop bad habits.

**Expectation mismatch.** Being judged against a promise of AI you never made is the most common way these roles end badly.

**Technical stagnation.** A year of SQL and file exports without modern tooling or production deployment can leave a visible gap.

**The supplier problem.** If access depends on a third party with no incentive to cooperate, your first year can be spent waiting.

## What to Secure Before You Accept

If the answers are good and you want it, convert the good intentions into something durable.

**Scope the first six months in writing.** Foundation work, named explicitly, with the first modelling project after it. This is the single most valuable protection, because it makes expectation drift visible.

**Get the sponsor named.** A person, not a department.

**Agree a tooling budget** and, if possible, external data engineering support for the first months.

**Agree a review point** at six months, against the scoped goals rather than against "AI results".

**Arrange technical review.** An external mentor, a peer network or a consultancy on call. This is cheap insurance against a year of unreviewed decisions.

**Discuss privacy early.** You will be working with customer, patient or employee data across systems, and the GDPR obligations sit with the employer but the practical design sits with you.

## The Sequence That Usually Works

Foundational roles succeed or fail on sequencing, and the order that works is counterintuitive for people trained to start with a model.

**Start with one decision, not one dataset.** Find a recurring decision somebody makes badly today — a weekly order quantity, a maintenance interval, a staffing plan — and work backwards to the data it needs. Starting from "what data do we have?" produces inventories nobody uses.

**Deliver a trustworthy number before a clever one.** The first genuine win in most of these organisations is a figure people stop arguing about. It is unglamorous and it buys you the credibility to do everything afterwards.

**Automate the boring path early.** If a report takes you two days by hand each month, automating it frees the time you need for the harder work and demonstrates the value of engineering to people who have never seen it.

**Only then model.** With access established, definitions agreed and a sponsor who trusts the numbers, a straightforward model often delivers more value than a sophisticated one would have six months earlier.

**Document as you go.** In an organisation with no data history, your documentation becomes the institutional memory. It also protects you from becoming the single point of failure.

Teams that follow roughly this order tend to have something in production within a year. Teams that start by building a platform or by attempting the most interesting model usually do not.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Almere who scoped the first six months

A data scientist accepts a first-data-hire role at a wholesaler thirty minutes from home. In the final conversation she asks to see data and is shown an ERP export and two spreadsheets. It is worse than described: inconsistent product codes, no history beyond eighteen months, and the warehouse system managed by an external supplier.

She does not decline. She asks three things: that the first six months are described in her offer letter as building a reliable data foundation rather than delivering models, that the operations director is named as sponsor, and that a budget exists for a data engineer for three months.

The employer agrees to all three, partly because nobody had thought about it in those terms. The first six months are exactly as unglamorous as expected. At month eight, the first forecast goes live for one product group. At month fourteen it covers the whole assortment and the purchasing team uses it weekly.

When she interviews elsewhere two years later, that sequence — no data, then a foundation, then a forecast people rely on — is the story every interviewer wants to hear in detail.

## Who Else Needs to Be Hired

One question separates organisations that have thought this through from those that have not: who comes after you?

A foundational role rarely succeeds as a permanent team of one. At some point the work splits — someone maintains pipelines and access while someone else models and works with stakeholders — and an employer who has never considered that split is planning for you to do both indefinitely.

Ask what the intended team looks like in two years, and whether a second hire is budgeted or merely hoped for. A concrete answer, even a modest one ("a data engineer in the second year if the first use case delivers"), tells you the organisation understands what it started. A vague answer means the ceiling on what you can achieve is your own capacity.

Also ask who owns the systems you will depend on. If the ERP, the machine data and the customer platform each belong to a different internal owner or external supplier, part of your job is coordination, and that is easier with a sponsor who can convene those people than with goodwill alone.

The honest version of this role is a two-year project to build a capability. Employers who describe it that way are usually the ones where it works.

## Key Takeaways

- Many Dutch employers with valuable data problems have operational systems and no usable analytical data; the ambition often arrives before the foundation.
- The same vacancy can be a foundational opportunity or a disappointment; the difference is budget, sponsorship, realistic expectations and access to source systems.
- Ask to see a sample, find out who controls the source systems, check the infrastructure budget and listen carefully to what success after twelve months means to them.
- Expect the first year to be access, pipelines, definitions and trust rather than modelling — valuable work, and portable everywhere.
- Before accepting, scope the first six months in writing, name the sponsor, secure tooling budget and arrange external technical review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a first data role) Should I take a job at a company with no usable data?
It can be an excellent move if the foundation work is budgeted, sponsored and expected. It is a trap where AI results are expected within months without infrastructure investment.

### (Scenario: candidate in interviews) How do I test data maturity before accepting?
Ask to see a sample of real data, ask who controls the source systems, ask what previous attempts produced and what success after twelve months would look like.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about their CV) Will a year of data plumbing hurt my career?
Not if it produces something people use. "Built the foundation and then delivered a forecast used weekly" is a stronger story than incremental work on a mature platform.

### (Scenario: candidate concerned about isolation) What if nobody can review my work?
Ask the employer to fund an external mentor or peer review. Unreviewed technical decisions in a first year are the main risk in this kind of role.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) What should I put in writing before accepting?
The scope of the first six months as foundation work, the named sponsor, the tooling budget and a review point measured against those goals.
