The salary is a little below what you hoped, and the recruiter presents the rest of the package with visible enthusiasm: options in a fast-growing company, a real stake in what you are building. For candidates coming from larger employers, this is unfamiliar territory, and the natural response is either to ignore it entirely or to imagine the outcome where it is worth a great deal. Both are mistakes. Equity in a Dutch scale-up is neither a lottery ticket nor a salary substitute; it is a conditional, illiquid, tax-complicated instrument whose value depends almost entirely on terms most candidates never ask about.

This article provides general information, not financial, legal or tax advice. Rules on employee participation and their tax treatment have changed in recent years, so verify current conditions with the Dutch Tax Administration or an adviser before relying on any of it.

## The Structures You Will Encounter

**Stock options.** A right to buy shares later at a fixed price. Value exists only if the company is eventually worth more than that price and a moment arrives when you can sell.

**Depositary receipts through a STAK.** A common Dutch structure: a foundation holds the shares and issues certificates to employees, giving economic exposure while voting control stays with the foundation. You benefit financially without becoming a voting shareholder.

**Stock appreciation rights.** A contractual right to a cash payment based on the increase in value, without shares changing hands. Simpler administratively, and entirely dependent on the company's ability and willingness to pay.

**Phantom or bonus schemes** linked to a future exit, which are contractual promises rather than ownership.

The label matters less than the terms attached to it, and the terms are where the value lives.

## The Questions That Decide Whether It Is Worth Anything

Ask these before you weigh equity at all. A serious employer answers them without hesitation; hesitation is itself an answer.

- **What percentage of the company does this represent, fully diluted?** A number of options without a denominator is meaningless.
- **What is the current valuation, and from when?** A valuation from a funding round two years ago may bear little relation to today.
- **What is the strike price?** If it is close to the current valuation, the shares must appreciate substantially before you gain anything.
- **What is the vesting schedule and is there a cliff?** Four years with a one-year cliff is a common pattern; nothing vests before the cliff.
- **What happens if I leave?** How long do you have to exercise, and what happens to vested rights? This clause quietly decides most outcomes.
- **Is there a liquidation preference, and how large?** Investors are frequently paid first, and in a modest exit this can leave ordinary employees with little or nothing.
- **How much dilution is expected?** Future funding rounds reduce your percentage unless you are protected.
- **What is the realistic path to liquidity?** An acquisition, a later round with a secondary component, or nothing foreseeable.
- **Has anyone actually been paid out?** The most revealing question in the list.

## Tax: The Part That Surprises People

The Dutch tax treatment of employee share options has changed in recent years, particularly around the moment of taxation for options in companies whose shares are not freely tradable. This detail matters enormously: being taxed on paper value you cannot sell is the classic way equity becomes a liability rather than a benefit.

Because the rules have moved and depend on the structure, the only responsible advice is to check the current position with the Dutch Tax Administration or a tax adviser before accepting an offer where equity is a meaningful part of the package — and to ask the employer directly how their scheme is treated and whether employees have faced tax at a point where they could not sell.

## How to Weigh It Against Salary

The practical rule used by most experienced candidates is straightforward: decide whether the offer works on cash alone.

If the salary, pension, holiday allowance and other terms are acceptable by themselves, equity is genuine upside and you can be relaxed about it. If the offer only works because of the equity, you are accepting a pay cut in exchange for a conditional instrument whose value you cannot verify, cannot sell and may be taxed on. That is an investment decision, and it should be made as deliberately as any other.

Note also what pay transparency changes here. Employers must provide information about initial pay or its range before the interview, and pay includes other remuneration. Equity does not exempt an employer from explaining the cash component clearly.

## What Equity Does Well

It aligns you with the outcome, which changes how it feels to work somewhere: you are building something you own a piece of. At companies that genuinely grow, it has produced meaningful sums for early employees. And it can be a legitimate way for a company with limited cash to share future value rather than promising a salary it cannot sustain.

In a good scheme with clear terms at a company doing well, it is a real benefit and worth having.

## What It Does Badly

It is illiquid, often for many years. Its value depends on decisions you do not control, including future funding terms that can dilute or subordinate your position. In a modest exit, preferences can absorb the proceeds before ordinary holders see anything. Leaver provisions can shorten your window to act to a point where exercising is impractical. And the emotional cost is real: people stay in roles they have outgrown because leaving feels like abandoning something that might be worth a lot.

## A Practical Way to Decide

**Value the cash package on its own.** Salary, holiday allowance, pension, travel reimbursement, hours, leave, training budget.

**Treat equity as a bonus with an unknown multiplier.** If you must attach a number for comparison, use something deliberately conservative rather than the company's projection.

**Ask the nine questions above** and write down the answers. Their clarity tells you as much as their content.

**Get the documents.** The option agreement and scheme rules, not just the offer letter summary. If the employer is reluctant to share them before you accept, that is informative.

**Take advice** if equity is a significant part of the package, particularly on tax and leaver terms.

**Then decide on the work.** For almost everyone, the team, the problems and the growth you will get are worth more than any realistic equity outcome.

## Comparing Equity Between Two Companies

Occasionally both offers include equity, and the instinct is to compare the headline numbers. Those numbers are almost never comparable, because they describe different instruments in companies at different stages.

**Convert to percentage, not count.** A thousand options at one company and ten thousand at another mean nothing until you know the fully diluted share count. Percentage is the only common unit.

**Compare stages honestly.** A smaller percentage in a company that has raised several rounds and has revenue is a different risk profile from a larger percentage in one that has neither. Earlier stage means a larger share of something far less likely to exist.

**Compare the strike price to the last valuation.** Options granted at a price close to the current value need substantial growth before they are worth anything; options granted well below it already carry value on paper.

**Compare leaver terms.** A ninety-day exercise window and a ten-year window produce completely different outcomes for the same grant, because the shorter one often forces a choice between paying real money and walking away.

**Compare preferences.** Investor liquidation preferences determine who gets paid first in a modest exit. A company with several rounds of preferences may need a very large exit before ordinary holders see anything.

If this sounds like work, that is the point. Two equity packages are only comparable after these five adjustments, and an employer who cannot supply the inputs has told you how transparent they intend to be.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Amsterdam who asked the nine questions

An ML engineer receives an offer from a scale-up: a salary slightly below a competing offer from an established employer, plus options presented as "a real stake in the company".

He asks the questions. The percentage is small but not negligible. The valuation dates from a round eighteen months earlier. The strike price is close to that valuation. Vesting is four years with a one-year cliff. On leaving, vested options must be exercised within ninety days — which, given the strike price and the tax questions, would require real money he would not want to risk. There is a liquidation preference. Nobody has been paid out yet, because the company has not had an exit or a secondary.

None of this is unusual or dishonest. It simply means the equity is worth little today and everything depends on an outcome several years away.

He compares the cash packages, finds the gap meaningful, and negotiates. The scale-up cannot move much on salary but adds a training budget and a four-day week option. He accepts, for the work and the team — and treats the options as a lottery ticket he did not pay for, which is the healthiest way to hold them.

## What to Ask Current Employees

The employer's answers about equity are necessarily optimistic. Current employees are the better source, and asking them is entirely legitimate during a team conversation.

Three questions work well. Do people here generally understand the scheme? An honest "not really, most of us stopped trying to follow it" is informative about how the company communicates. Has anyone exercised options, and what happened? This surfaces both the practical process and any tax surprises. And do you think of the equity as part of your pay, or as a lottery ticket? The answer tells you how the scheme is regarded by people who have held it longer than you.

You can also ask something more neutral that reveals a great deal: has the scheme changed since you joined? Amendments, repricing or new classes of shares are normal in growing companies, and knowing that the terms move is useful context for how much weight to place on today's paperwork.

None of this requires an awkward conversation about money. You are asking how a benefit works, which is the same as asking how the pension scheme or the training budget works.

## Key Takeaways

- Common Dutch structures include options, depositary receipts via a STAK, stock appreciation rights and phantom schemes; the terms matter more than the label.
- Ask about percentage fully diluted, valuation date, strike price, vesting and cliff, leaver provisions, liquidation preference, expected dilution, liquidity path and whether anyone has been paid out.
- Tax treatment of employee options in the Netherlands has changed in recent years; check current rules before accepting a package that leans on equity.
- Decide whether the offer works on cash alone; if it only works with equity, you are making an investment decision.
- Equity aligns you with the outcome but is illiquid, dilutable, subordinate to investor preferences and emotionally sticky.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered options) Should I accept a lower salary in exchange for equity?
Only if the cash package works on its own. If the offer depends on the equity to be acceptable, you are making an unverifiable investment decision rather than a salary negotiation.

### (Scenario: candidate unfamiliar with Dutch structures) What is a STAK and why do Dutch companies use it?
A foundation that holds shares and issues depositary receipts to employees, giving economic exposure while keeping voting control centralised.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about tax) When is Dutch employee equity taxed?
The rules have changed in recent years and depend on the structure and tradability of the shares; check the current position with the Tax Administration or an adviser.

### (Scenario: candidate planning ahead) What happens to my options if I leave?
That depends on the leaver provisions, including how long you have to exercise. This clause decides most real-world outcomes, so read it before accepting.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing offers) How should I value equity when comparing two offers?
Conservatively. Compare cash packages first, treat equity as upside with an unknown multiplier, and ask whether anyone at the company has ever been paid out.
