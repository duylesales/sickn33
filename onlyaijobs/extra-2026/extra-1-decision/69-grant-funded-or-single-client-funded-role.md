Somewhere in the conversation about the contract, a sentence appears that changes the shape of the offer: the position runs for the duration of the programme, or is funded by the project, or exists because of one large customer. The work may be excellent — applied research, a consortium with interesting partners, a well-resourced innovation programme, a long engagement with a serious client. What has changed is that your employment is now tied to a specific pot of money with a specific end date, and understanding what happens when that money runs out is the whole decision.

## The Three Funding Shapes

**Programme or subsidy funding.** National, regional or European funds supporting research, innovation, energy transition, healthcare or digitalisation. Common at applied research organisations, universities of applied sciences, consortium partners and public bodies.

**Single-client funding.** Your salary is effectively paid by one customer contract. Common at consultancies, product companies with one dominant account, and engineering firms delivering a long programme.

**Internal project funding.** A budget approved for a defined initiative rather than for a permanent capability. Common in large organisations where a transformation programme hires people who are not part of the permanent establishment.

All three feel identical on day one. They differ in what happens in month thirty.

## Why This Is So Common in Dutch AI Work

The Netherlands funds a large amount of applied research and innovation through programmes rather than through permanent budgets, and much of the interesting cross-organisational work — health data, energy systems, mobility, agriculture, public services — exists as consortium projects with a defined lifetime.

That is not a warning; it is a description of where a good deal of the work is. Some of the most technically interesting AI roles in the country are funded this way, and treating grant-funded work as second-class would rule out much of the applied research landscape. The point is to enter with clear eyes.

## What It Means for Your Contract

Project-funded roles are usually fixed-term, aligned to the funding period. That has concrete consequences worth checking.

**Duration and renewal.** Ask what the contract term is, whether it matches the funding period, and what has happened to previous people in equivalent positions.

**Your position in the sequence of contracts.** Dutch rules limit how long successive fixed-term contracts can continue before the relationship becomes open-ended. Where you sit in that sequence affects what the employer can offer next, and employers sometimes decline to renew for exactly that reason. Read your contract and check the current rules for your situation.

**End-of-contract entitlements.** A transition payment is in principle due in defined circumstances when employment ends at the employer's initiative, including where a fixed-term contract is not continued. Check what applies to you.

**Mortgage capacity.** Lenders treat fixed-term contracts cautiously. An employer's declaration of intent can help, and the timing of a house purchase is worth planning around.

**Notice arrangements,** including whether the contract can be ended early if the funding is withdrawn, which is a clause worth reading carefully.

## What Happens When the Funding Ends

Three outcomes, and employers know which is likely long before candidates do.

**Absorption.** The capability proved valuable, a permanent budget was found, and you continue. The most common good outcome and the one to ask about directly.

**Extension.** A follow-on project, a second phase, a renewed grant. Normal in research environments, and dependent on a proposal being successful — which means your continuity depends on someone else's bid.

**Ending.** The project delivered, the client left, or the budget was not renewed. You have a transition period, a payment in some circumstances, and a search.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **How long is the funding secured, and how much of the period is left?** Joining eight months before the end of a four-year programme is a different job.
- **What happened to the people in the last comparable project here?** The single most predictive question.
- **Is there an intention to absorb this role into the permanent organisation, and what would that depend on?**
- **Who is preparing the follow-on proposal, and would I be involved?**
- **If it is client-funded, how long is the contract, what is the notice period on their side and what share of the employer's revenue does this client represent?**
- **What happens to what I build when the project ends?** Ownership, maintenance, whether it reaches production or a repository nobody opens.
- **Does the project have production ambitions, or only deliverables?**

## What the Work Is Actually Like

Grant and consortium work has a particular texture that suits some people very well.

There are deliverables and reporting obligations, which means real deadlines attached to documents as well as to systems. There are partners — universities, companies, public bodies — with different incentives, and coordination takes a meaningful share of the week. Publication is often expected or encouraged. The pace is steadier than commercial work and the horizon is longer, which is a relief to some and frustrating to others.

Client-funded work is the opposite in texture: fast, responsive, and shaped by a single customer's priorities, with the associated risk that their reorganisation becomes your problem.

## The Career Value

**Domain depth and a network.** Consortium work puts you in rooms with the people who define a field in the Netherlands, which is worth more than most candidates realise.

**Applied research credentials.** Published work, a track record on a named programme, and experience of formal evaluation.

**Novel problems.** Programme funding tends to go to things nobody has solved, which is why the work is interesting.

**A demonstrated ability to deliver against external obligations,** which employers in regulated sectors value.

## The Career Risk

The risk is a pattern rather than a single job: three consecutive project roles, each ending at the pilot stage, producing a CV of prototypes and no production systems. Interviewers notice, and the question "what happened to it afterwards" becomes hard to answer three times in a row.

The remedy is to ensure at least one thing you build is used by someone after the project ends, and to be able to describe it.

## When to Accept

- The funding has a substantial period left and the work is genuinely interesting.
- There is a credible path to absorption, or the employer has absorbed people before.
- The role builds portable skills and a network you want.
- You are comfortable with a fixed-term arrangement for this period of your life.
- Something you build has a route to actual use.
- The salary reflects the insecurity rather than ignoring it.

## When to Decline

- Less than a year of funding remains and there is no follow-on in preparation.
- Nobody can say what happened to previous project staff.
- The work stops at a report or a demonstration by design.
- You are on a mortgage timeline that a fixed-term contract would complicate.
- The employer depends on one client who is visibly reconsidering.
- You would be isolated, with no permanent colleagues around the project.

## How to Position Yourself From Day One

Treat the end date as a planning fact rather than an anxiety. Write it in your calendar, and set a point — six to nine months before — when you start looking, regardless of how the absorption conversation is going.

Build relationships in the permanent organisation, not only in the project. People get absorbed because a department wants them, and departments want people they know.

Choose work with transferable value and, where possible, a route to production. A deliverable that someone uses afterwards is worth three that satisfy a reporting obligation.

Keep your own record of what you built and what it achieved, since project documentation disperses when the consortium closes.

And ask, at the halfway point, in plain terms: what would have to be true for this role to continue? The answer is usually honest and always useful.

## Reading the Funding Before You Sign

Much of what you need is checkable, and a candidate who has done the checking asks noticeably better questions.

**Find out who the funder is and what the programme is called.** Publicly funded research and innovation programmes in the Netherlands and the EU generally publish their awards, including the participants, the duration and the project's aims. Reading the project description tells you what your employer has formally committed to deliver, which is not always what the vacancy describes.

**Establish where in the lifecycle you would join.** The first year of a programme is design and data collection; the middle years are the substantive work; the final year is writing, reporting and closing. A role starting in the last year is mostly documentation, whatever the title says.

**Check whether your employer is the coordinator or a partner.** Coordinators carry administrative load and influence; partners deliver a work package and have less control over direction. Both are fine; they are different jobs.

**Ask how many other funded projects the employer runs.** An organisation with a continuous portfolio moves people between projects when one ends, which is a considerable amount of informal security. An organisation with one project has nowhere to put you.

**Ask what share of the team is permanently employed.** A group where most people are on project contracts behaves differently from one where project staff are a minority around a permanent core.

Ten minutes of reading before the second interview turns a vague sense of risk into two or three specific questions, and the answers to those are what the decision actually rests on.

## Real example

### A researcher in Wageningen who set her own end date

A data scientist joins a four-year consortium on agricultural monitoring, employed by one of the partner organisations on a contract matching the project. She is told, cheerfully, that "these things usually continue".

She asks what happened to the people on the previous programme. Two of five were absorbed into permanent roles, two moved to partner organisations, one left the field. That is a reasonable record and a useful reality check.

She takes the role and does two things deliberately. She builds the data pipeline so that it runs in the host organisation's environment rather than in a project sandbox, which means it can outlive the funding. And she works with two of the industrial partners closely enough that they know her work.

At the three-year mark she asks what continuation would depend on. The answer is a follow-on proposal with an uncertain outcome. She starts looking nine months before the end.

She is offered a permanent role by one of the industrial partners two months before the project closes, and the pipeline she built is still running there today. The follow-on proposal, as it happens, was rejected.

## Key Takeaways

- Programme, client and internal project funding all produce fixed-term arrangements whose real question is what happens when the money ends.
- Check the contract term against the funding period, your position in the sequence of fixed-term contracts, end-of-contract entitlements and the effect on borrowing capacity.
- Ask what happened to people in the last comparable project — the most predictive question available.
- The career value is domain depth, a strong network, novel problems and applied research credentials; the risk is a CV of prototypes with nothing in production.
- Set your own search date six to nine months before the funding ends, build relationships in the permanent organisation and make sure something you build outlives the project.
