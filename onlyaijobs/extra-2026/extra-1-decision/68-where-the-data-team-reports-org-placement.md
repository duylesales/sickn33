Candidates ask about the stack, the team size and the projects. Very few ask which part of the organisation the data team belongs to, and yet that single fact predicts more about the daily reality of the job than any of the others. It determines whose problems count as priorities, whose budget pays for your tools, how quickly you can get access to a system, how far you sit from the people who decide things, and in many Dutch organisations even which salary scale applies to you. It is a question you can ask in the first conversation, and the answer repays the thirty seconds it takes.

## The Four Common Placements

**Under IT.** The data team is part of the technology organisation, alongside infrastructure, applications and support.

**Under finance.** Common where analytics grew out of reporting and control.

**Inside a business unit.** Operations, commerce, supply chain, claims, marketing — the team belongs to the department it serves.

**Standalone,** reporting to a chief data officer, a director of data, or occasionally directly to the executive board.

A fifth arrangement exists in large organisations: a central team plus embedded people in the business, which combines several of the advantages and a few of the problems.

## Under IT

**What you get.** Access to systems, infrastructure and engineering colleagues. Established practice around deployment, security and change management. Budget for tooling, because IT is where technology budget lives. Being in the same organisation as the people who control the platforms you depend on is worth a great deal.

**What you lose.** Distance from the business problem, and a service framing. In many IT organisations, work arrives as requests through a process, which is well suited to keeping systems running and poorly suited to figuring out what a planner actually needs.

**The characteristic failure.** The team builds what was requested, correctly, and nobody uses it. Priorities follow availability and uptime rather than value.

**Ask:** how work is initiated, whether the team has its own roadmap, and whether anyone sits with the business regularly.

## Under Finance

**What you get.** Budget, seriousness about numbers, and a direct line to people who make decisions with them. Finance functions are accustomed to being trusted and to defending their figures, which raises the standard around definitions and data quality.

**What you lose.** Gravity towards reporting. Finance's core need is periodic, accurate, explicable numbers, and predictive work that is probabilistic sits awkwardly beside that. Cost focus can also dominate, which suits efficiency projects and starves anything speculative.

**The characteristic failure.** The team becomes a reporting factory with a data science job title attached.

**Ask:** what proportion of the team's work is periodic reporting, and what predictive work has reached production.

## Inside a Business Unit

**What you get.** Proximity, which is the single biggest predictor of adoption. You sit with the people who have the problem, you hear about it informally, and your work gets used because the person who wanted it is nearby.

**What you lose.** Scope and platform. You are working on one department's problems with whatever infrastructure exists, often without engineering support, and sometimes duplicating what another unit built last year.

**The characteristic failure.** Excellent work that cannot be maintained, scaled or reused, and a career that narrows to one domain.

**Ask:** who provides engineering and platform support, and how work is shared across units.

## Standalone or Under a CDO

**What you get.** A mandate, visibility and the ability to work across the organisation. Governance, data quality and strategy sit naturally here, and the role often carries influence beyond its size.

**What you lose.** Stability, sometimes. Standalone data functions are discretionary, relatively new in many Dutch organisations, and vulnerable when budgets tighten or the sponsor leaves. They also frequently lack direct access to systems, which means depending on IT for everything without the leverage of being part of it.

**The characteristic failure.** A team with a strategy, a mandate and no hands on the systems, producing frameworks rather than working software.

**Ask:** how long the function has existed, who the sponsor is, and what it has delivered rather than defined.

## Dutch Specifics Worth Knowing

**Your scale may follow the placement.** Many Dutch employers, particularly larger ones and those under a collective agreement, grade roles by job family. The same work can be scaled as an IT function, an analytical function or a business function, with different pay bands and different progression. It is entirely reasonable to ask which family the role sits in and what the scale is.

**Shared service centres.** In large organisations and parts of the public sector, data capability sometimes sits in a shared service organisation serving several entities. This can mean stable work and considerable distance from the people affected by it.

**Works council involvement.** Reorganisations that move a team from one part of the organisation to another follow formal procedures, so a reorganisation in progress is something you can ask about.

**Public sector placement.** In government bodies, placement often reflects the history of the organisation rather than a design, and the data function may sit under information management or policy, each with a quite different character.

## What Matters More Than the Box

Placement is a strong predictor, not a verdict. Four things matter more, and they cut across every arrangement.

**The seniority of your sponsor.** A team under IT with a director who is respected by the business is in a stronger position than a standalone function reporting to someone without influence.

**Budget ownership.** Whether the team controls its own tooling and running costs, or depends on another department's goodwill each year.

**Access to systems and data.** How long it takes to get access to a source, in days rather than in principle.

**Decision rights.** Whether the team can decline work, set its own roadmap, and say that something is not ready.

Ask about these four regardless of the answer to the placement question. A team with all four functions well wherever it sits; a team with none struggles even under the most fashionable structure.

## Questions to Ask in the Interview

- **Where does the team sit, and who does the manager report to?**
- **Which job family and scale does this role fall under?**
- **Who decides what the team works on?**
- **Whose budget pays for infrastructure and tooling?**
- **How long does it take to get access to a new data source?**
- **Has the team moved between parts of the organisation recently?** Repeated moves indicate unresolved arguments about what the team is for.
- **Which department uses the team's work most, and are they in the same reporting line?**

## Warning Signs

- Nobody can explain clearly where the team sits or who decides its priorities.
- The team has been reorganised more than once in two years.
- Tooling requires approval from a department that has no interest in the outcome.
- The people who use the work are three reporting lines away.
- The function was created by an executive who has since left.

## When the Placement Changes

Data teams move. In most larger Dutch organisations, the analytics function has sat in at least two different places over the past decade, and there is a reasonable chance it will move again while you work there. Understanding what that means keeps it from feeling like a crisis.

**Why teams move.** A new executive arrives with a different model. A central function is created to reduce duplication, or dissolved to increase proximity. A cost programme consolidates support functions. A regulatory requirement pushes governance into a second-line structure.

**What actually changes for you.** Usually the priorities and the budget, occasionally the salary scale, sometimes the manager. What rarely changes is the work itself in the first months, which is why teams often underestimate the effect until a budget round arrives.

**The procedural side.** Significant reorganisations follow formal processes in the Netherlands, including consultation with the works council on major organisational decisions. That means these changes are usually visible internally well before they are announced, and it is legitimate to ask in an interview whether any reorganisation is currently in consultation.

**Reading a history of moves.** A team that has moved twice in three years is telling you that the organisation has not resolved what it wants the function to be. That is not always bad — sometimes the third placement is the right one — but it predicts further instability, and it makes the sponsor question even more important.

**What to ask.** Where the team has sat previously, why it moved, whether anything is currently under discussion, and what happened to people's scales and budgets when it last happened. Managers answer this more candidly than candidates expect, because it is an ordinary fact of organisational life rather than a secret.

## Real example

### An analyst in Apeldoorn who asked one question and changed her mind

An analyst has two offers at similar salaries. One is at a large insurer, in a central data team reporting to a newly appointed chief data officer. The other is at a mid-sized logistics company, in a team that sits inside operations.

The first sounds more impressive, so she asks her questions there first. The function is eighteen months old, the CDO arrived nine months ago, and access to source systems runs through IT with a request process that takes several weeks. When she asks what the team has put into production, the answer is a data catalogue and a governance framework.

At the logistics company, the team of five sits with planners, has its own tooling budget inside the operations line, and two models run daily in production. Access to data takes a day, because the systems belong to the same department. The weakness is platform support: there are no dedicated engineers, and the team maintains its own pipelines.

She takes the logistics role, having traded a better title for shorter feedback loops.

Her summary: both teams were competent, and only one of them could get anything done, and the difference was entirely about where they sat.

## Key Takeaways

- Where a data team reports predicts priorities, budget, system access, distance from decisions and often your salary scale.
- Under IT you gain access, infrastructure and engineering practice and risk a service-desk framing; under finance you gain budget and rigour and risk becoming a reporting function.
- Inside a business unit you gain adoption and lose platform and scope; standalone functions gain mandate and risk producing frameworks without hands on systems.
- In many Dutch organisations the job family attached to the placement determines the scale and progression, so ask which family the role sits in.
- Sponsor seniority, budget ownership, speed of data access and decision rights matter more than the structure itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing employers) Does it matter where the data team reports?
Considerably. It predicts whose priorities count, whose budget pays for tooling, how fast you get data access and how close you sit to the people making decisions.

### (Scenario: candidate offered a role under IT) Is reporting into IT a bad sign?
No. You gain system access, infrastructure and engineering practice. The risk is a service framing where work arrives as requests and adoption is nobody's responsibility.

### (Scenario: candidate offered a central data role) What is the risk of a standalone data function?
It can be strong or fragile. Ask how long it has existed, who sponsors it, whether it has direct access to systems and what it has delivered rather than defined.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating pay) Can placement affect my salary?
Yes. Many Dutch employers grade roles by job family, so the same work can sit in an IT, analytical or business scale. Ask which family and scale apply.

### (Scenario: candidate deciding) What matters more than the reporting line?
Sponsor seniority, whether the team owns its budget, how quickly data access is granted and whether the team can set its roadmap and decline work.
