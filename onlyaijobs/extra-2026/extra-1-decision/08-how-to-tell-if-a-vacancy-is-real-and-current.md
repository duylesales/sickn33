You find a vacancy that fits well, spend two hours tailoring a CV and a letter, submit it, and hear nothing. Weeks later you discover the role was filled before you applied — or was never really open. Every job seeker experiences this, and in a field where good applications take real effort, the cost is significant. A few minutes of checking before you write can save those hours, and the checks are straightforward once you know what to look at.

## The Four Types of Postings That Waste Your Time

**Already-filled roles.** The vacancy was genuine but is now closed, and nobody removed it from the aggregator or board that copied it.

**Ghost jobs.** Postings kept live without a real intention to hire soon — to build a candidate pipeline, to test the market, to appear to be growing, or because a hiring freeze arrived after publication.

**Agency fishing.** Listings published by intermediaries to collect CVs rather than to fill a specific assignment they have been given.

**Outright scams.** Rare in AI hiring but real, usually recognisable by requests for money, identity documents or bank details early in the process.

Only the last category is dishonest in a legal sense. The others are mostly the result of process sloppiness — which does not make the wasted evening less annoying.

## Check One: Age and Freshness

Start with the posting date, which most boards show and most aggregators approximate.

A vacancy published in the last two weeks is usually live. Something between two and six weeks old is normal for specialised roles, which take longer to fill. Beyond roughly two months, ask yourself why: a genuinely hard-to-fill specialist role, or a posting nobody maintains.

Two warnings. Aggregators sometimes show the date the listing was collected rather than published, making old roles look new. And some employers repost the same vacancy periodically, which resets the date without meaning the process restarted.

A practical response: filter by posting date when the board allows it, and treat anything older than two months as requiring a check before you invest effort.

## Check Two: Does It Exist at the Source?

The single most reliable test is to find the same vacancy on the employer's own website.

If it is there, with the same title and a working application route, the role is almost certainly live. If it is absent while the board listing remains, the role has probably been filled or withdrawn. If the employer's site lists it but with different details — another location, another seniority, a different closing date — trust the employer's version.

This check takes two minutes and resolves most uncertainty. It is also the reason that boards linking to the employer's own posting, as OnlyAIJobs does, are easier to verify than platforms that host a copy of the text.

## Check Three: Who Published It?

Look at whether the listing comes from the employer or an intermediary.

An employer-published listing names the organisation, the location and usually a contact person. An agency listing often describes "a leading player in the sector" without naming it, which may be a legitimate confidential search or may be a fishing expedition.

If an intermediary refuses to name the company after you express serious interest, you cannot check whether you have already applied there, and you cannot verify the role exists at all. That is a reasonable point at which to stop.

## Check Four: Does the Company Exist and Look Active?

For employers you do not know, a few minutes of research helps.

**The trade register.** Companies operating in the Netherlands are registered with the **Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK)**. A company that cannot be found there deserves scepticism.

**The website.** A functioning site with real content, named employees and a physical address is a good sign. A page created recently, with stock photography and no address, is not.

**People.** Employees with plausible professional profiles and a history at the company suggest a real organisation.

**Location.** On a board that shows the exact address, check whether the location matches the company's stated offices. Address-level listings make this much easier than a city label.

## Check Five: Internal Consistency of the Text

Genuine vacancies are usually specific. They describe a team, a problem, the tools in use and what the first months look like. They name a contact person or an application route inside the employer's own system.

Warning signs include: a description so generic it could apply to any company; a mismatch between the seniority in the title and the tasks described; no information about the team or the work, only requirements; and — increasingly common — text that reads as though it was generated without anyone checking it, such as contradictory statements about location or working hours.

## Check Six: Salary Information

Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, employers must provide information about the initial pay or its range before the job interview, and may not ask about pay history. As this is implemented, the absence of any salary information, combined with an unwillingness to give a range when asked, becomes a meaningful signal — not necessarily that the role is fake, but that the employer has not organised its hiring.

## Scam Signals That Justify Stopping Immediately

- Requests for payment of any kind: training, equipment, visa handling, administration.
- Requests for a copy of your passport, bank details or citizen service number before a genuine offer and contract.
- Interviews conducted only through chat applications, with no video call and no identifiable employees.
- Offers made without any real assessment.
- Requests to receive or forward money or goods.

If you encounter these, stop contact and report it to the **Fraudehelpdesk**, and to the police if you suffered damage.

## Reading the Answer You Get Back

If you send a short message asking whether a role is still open, the reply itself is evidence — often better evidence than the posting.

**A specific, prompt answer** naming the stage of the process ("we are interviewing next week and still accepting applications") indicates an organised employer who tracks their own vacancies. Apply with confidence.

**A vague answer** about "several positions" or "always looking for good people" usually means there is no defined role, or that the listing is a pipeline exercise. Treat it as low priority.

**A request for your CV before answering your question** is a sign you are talking to a channel collecting candidates rather than filling a specific role. Ask again before sending anything.

**Silence** is itself informative. An employer who cannot answer a three-sentence question in a week is unlikely to run a fast, communicative process afterwards.

**A response from a person with a name and function** — a hiring manager, a team lead, a recruiter who names the team — is worth more than a reply from a generic inbox.

Many Dutch employers follow the NVP Recruitment Code, which sets expectations about informing candidates and handling applications within reasonable timeframes. You are not being demanding by asking; you are checking whether the process is real before investing your evening in it.

## A Two-Minute Checklist Before You Apply

| Check | What you are looking for |
|---|---|
| Posting date | Published or refreshed within the last two months |
| Employer's own site | The same vacancy, live, with a working application route |
| Publisher | Employer or named intermediary, not anonymous |
| Company reality | Findable in the KVK register, real website, plausible employees |
| Location | Address matches the company's actual site |
| Text quality | Specific team, problem and tools; consistent details |
| Salary information | A range, or a willingness to give one when asked |
| Contact person | A named person or a proper application system |

## What to Do When You Are Unsure

Send a short message to the contact person: confirm the role is still open, ask when they expect to hold interviews and, if not stated, ask for the salary range. Three sentences.

This takes five minutes, and the response tells you a lot. A prompt, specific reply indicates an organised process. Silence, or a vague answer about "several positions", indicates the opposite. ## Why Postings Go Stale: What Happens Inside the Employer

Understanding why a vacancy stays online after it should have been removed makes the signals easier to interpret — and reduces the temptation to read bad intent into ordinary disorganisation.

**Distribution is fragmented.** A vacancy published once can end up on the employer's site, several boards, aggregators that copied it and agency pages. Taking it down in one place does not remove it from the others, and few organisations have a process for cleaning up every copy.

**Hiring pauses are common.** Budgets freeze, a manager leaves, a reorganisation is announced, a priority shifts. The role is not cancelled but not actively filled either, and nobody decides to withdraw the posting.

**Pipelines are built deliberately.** Some employers keep postings live for roles they expect to open, particularly for profiles that are consistently hard to find. This is legitimate in principle, and frustrating for candidates who are not told.

**Processes simply take long.** Specialist AI roles routinely take months to fill. A vacancy that is three months old may be entirely genuine and still open, with a candidate in the final round.

The distinction that matters to you is not whether the employer behaved well but whether your application has a realistic chance this month. That is exactly why the checks in this article focus on verifiable facts — presence on the employer's own site, a named contact, a company that demonstrably exists — rather than on guessing intentions.

## Real example

### A data analyst in Tilburg who started checking first

A data analyst in Tilburg applies to fourteen roles over five weeks and hears back from three. Frustrated, she reviews what happened. Four of the vacancies are no longer on the employers' own websites, and two were agency listings that never named the company.

She changes her routine. Before writing anything, she spends two minutes on each vacancy: check the date, find it on the employer's site, confirm the company in the trade register if she does not know it, and check the address against the location shown. Roles that fail these checks are skipped.

Over the next five weeks she applies to seven roles instead of fourteen and receives five responses, three of them interviews. She did not become a better candidate in five weeks. She stopped spending evenings on postings that were never going to lead anywhere.

## Key Takeaways

- Time-wasting postings fall into four groups: already filled, ghost jobs, agency fishing and scams.
- The most reliable check is finding the same vacancy live on the employer's own website.
- Treat postings older than about two months with caution, and remember aggregators can make old roles look new.
- Verify unknown companies in the KVK register, check the address, and be sceptical of anonymous listings.
- Stop immediately at any request for payment or identity documents before a genuine offer, and report suspected scams.
