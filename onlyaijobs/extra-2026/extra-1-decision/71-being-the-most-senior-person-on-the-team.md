The offer is a step up, and the reason it is a step up is that there is nobody above you. A team of three or five, all less experienced than you, a manager who is not technical, and every architectural decision, review and judgement call landing on your desk. Candidates usually see the authority first and the isolation later. The authority is real and so is the isolation, and the balance between them is what determines whether this role accelerates your career by three years or quietly stalls it while your title improves.

## How People End Up in This Position

**Hired directly as the senior or lead,** into a team that has grown without one.

**The seniors left.** You join a team that used to have three experienced people and now has none, which is a situation with a history worth asking about.

**The organisation is small.** In mid-sized Dutch companies, a data team of four with one senior is simply the normal shape.

**Promotion from within an existing team,** where you were the strongest of a group of peers.

Each has different implications. Joining a team whose seniors all left recently is a different proposition from joining one that never had any.

## What Changes Without Someone Above You

**Nobody checks your reasoning.** Design decisions, validation approaches, architectural choices — all yours, unreviewed. Mistakes do not surface until they are expensive.

**Your calibration drifts.** Without comparison you lose the ability to judge whether your work is strong, adequate or poor. People in this position routinely misjudge themselves in both directions.

**Every question arrives.** Juniors escalate to you, the manager asks you, the business asks you, and there is no one to escalate to yourself.

**Habits harden.** Whatever you do becomes the team's standard, including the parts you do badly.

**Your learning depends entirely on your own initiative.** The passive learning of working alongside stronger colleagues disappears, and nothing replaces it unless you arrange it.

## What You Gain

**Real authority over how things are done.** Standards, tooling, architecture, ways of working. This is genuinely satisfying and hard to get elsewhere.

**Fast growth in judgement.** Making decisions and living with them develops you quickly, provided you have enough experience to make reasonable ones.

**Visibility.** You become the person the organisation associates with data, which opens doors internally.

**Lead credentials.** Technical leadership experience is what most lead and principal roles require, and this is how people acquire it.

**Freedom from other people's bad decisions,** which, after a few years of inheriting them, is a pleasure.

## The Question That Decides It: Have You Been Reviewed Enough?

The honest test is not whether you can do the work. It is whether you have been held to a standard by people better than you for long enough to have internalised one.

If you have spent several years in a team with strong reviewers, you carry that standard with you and can apply it alone. If your experience has been largely unsupervised already, this role compounds the problem: you would be setting standards you never had the chance to learn.

Most people know which applies to them. A useful proxy is whether you can remember specific occasions when a colleague changed your mind about a technical approach. If several come to mind, you have been calibrated. If none do, get calibrated before taking a role where nobody will.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **Why is there no senior person now?** Especially if there used to be.
- **Who would review my work?** Listen for whether the question has occurred to them.
- **Is there budget for external review or an architecture partner?**
- **Who else in the organisation is technical?** Engineers elsewhere can provide much of what a senior data colleague would.
- **What is the training budget and time, and is it contractual?**
- **Is a second senior planned, and on what trigger?**
- **What happens when I say something will take three months and the business wants it in three weeks?** Ask for a real example.
- **Who decides technical direction — me, the manager, or IT?**

## Signals the Employer Understands the Risk

- They raise the review question before you do.
- There is budget for an external reviewer, an advisory arrangement or a consultancy day now and then.
- Engineers in other teams are available for architecture discussions.
- A second senior hire is planned and funded.
- Conference and training budget is real and used.
- The manager knows what they do not know, and says so.

An employer with none of these is not necessarily a bad employer. They simply have not thought about it, which means the responsibility for your development will be entirely yours.

## How to Replace the Senior You Do Not Have

**Buy review.** An external senior reviewer for a few days a quarter costs less than the mistakes it prevents, and it is easy to justify to an employer as risk reduction rather than as personal development.

**Find peers outside the organisation.** A meetup, a professional community, a small group of people in similar positions at other employers. This is the cheapest and most effective option, and it is the one people neglect.

**Use the engineers you have.** Software engineers elsewhere in the organisation can review your code, your deployment practice and your architecture even if they know nothing about modelling.

**Write decisions down and revisit them.** A short record of why you chose something, reread six months later, is a surprisingly effective substitute for a colleague asking why.

**Deliberately expose your work.** Present internally, write up approaches, submit a talk to a meetup. Explaining to strangers surfaces weaknesses that private work hides.

**Keep one thing hard.** Choose at least one project a year that stretches you technically rather than one you already know how to do.

## When to Accept

- You have several years of properly reviewed experience behind you.
- The employer has thought about review, or accepts your proposal to arrange it.
- You want lead experience and this is a realistic route to it.
- There are technical colleagues elsewhere in the organisation.
- A second senior is planned, or the team is growing.
- You have the temperament to seek out correction rather than avoid it.

## When to Decline

- Your own experience has been largely unsupervised already.
- The team lost its seniors recently and nobody will explain why.
- There is no budget for training or external input.
- The manager expects you to be the answer to every question with no support.
- You would be alone technically in an entirely non-technical organisation.
- You want to deepen your craft rather than broaden your responsibility right now.

## The Two-Year Check

Set a review point at two years and ask three questions honestly. Am I better than I was, or only more senior? Could I pass a technical interview at a strong employer today? Have I had my mind changed about anything technical in the past year?

If the answers are uncomfortable, the remedy is not necessarily to leave. It is to introduce the review and challenge that the role does not supply — which is easier while you are still in the job than afterwards, when a search exposes the gap for you.

## Managing Upwards Becomes Part of the Job

When you are the most senior technical person, your manager is usually not technical, and the quality of that relationship determines how much of your judgement survives contact with the organisation.

**Estimates will be treated as commitments.** Give ranges with reasons, and explain what would make the work faster or slower. A manager who cannot assess the technical content will anchor on the first number they hear.

**You will be asked to approve things you did not design.** Vendor proposals, architecture suggestions from IT, a consultancy's plan. Being the only person who can evaluate them is real influence, and it requires you to say clearly when you do not know enough to judge.

**Hiring decisions become yours in substance.** Your manager cannot assess technical candidates, so your assessment decides. This is a skill in itself, and getting it wrong in a small team is expensive for years.

**Quality standards need translating.** "We need monitoring before this goes live" means nothing to a non-technical manager as stated. "If this fails silently, the planners will make decisions on stale numbers for weeks before anyone notices" does.

**Bad news must travel early.** Managers who cannot see the work rely entirely on what you tell them, and a surprise at the deadline damages trust in a way that an early warning never does.

Candidates sometimes treat this as overhead. It is closer to the opposite: in a team with no senior above you, managing upwards well is what buys the time, budget and external review that keep the role from becoming a dead end.

## Real example

### A lead in Deventer who hired her own reviewer

A data scientist with six years of experience, four of them in a well-reviewed team at a large insurer, takes a lead role at a mid-sized manufacturer. The team is three people, all with under two years of experience, and the manager is an operations director.

She asks who would review her work. Nobody had considered it. She proposes a budget for an external senior data scientist, two days a quarter, framed as risk reduction for a team with a single point of technical failure. It is approved without difficulty.

The first year goes well. The external reviewer finds two significant problems: a validation design that leaked information across time, and a deployment approach that would have made rollback impossible.

She also joins a monthly group of people in similar roles at other employers, which she describes as more useful than any training course.

Three years later the team is six people with two mid-level engineers, and she is applying for principal roles. In interviews, the thing that lands best is not the team she built but the fact that she arranged her own review when nobody asked her to.

## Key Takeaways

- Being the most senior person brings authority, ownership and fast growth in judgement — and removes review, calibration and passive learning.
- The honest test is whether you have been held to a standard by better colleagues long enough to have internalised one.
- Ask why there is no senior now, who would review your work, and whether there is budget for external input or a second senior hire.
- Replace the missing colleague deliberately: buy external review, find peers outside the organisation, use engineers elsewhere and expose your work publicly.
- Set a two-year check and ask whether you are actually better, not merely more senior.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a lead role) Is it risky to be the most senior person on a data team?
The risk is not the responsibility but the absence of review. Without someone to correct you, mistakes become habits and your sense of your own level drifts.

### (Scenario: candidate judging readiness) How do I know whether I am ready?
Ask whether you can remember specific times a colleague changed your mind about a technical approach. Several examples means you have been calibrated; none means you should get calibrated first.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) What should I ask the employer for?
Budget for an external reviewer or advisory arrangement, a contractual training allowance, access to engineers elsewhere in the organisation and a plan for a second senior hire.

### (Scenario: candidate already in such a role) How do I keep developing?
Buy external review, join a peer group outside the organisation, have your code reviewed by software engineers, write decisions down and present your work publicly.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about the team's history) What if the previous seniors all left?
Ask why, specifically. A team that lost its experienced people recently has a reason, and it is usually about the organisation rather than about the individuals.
