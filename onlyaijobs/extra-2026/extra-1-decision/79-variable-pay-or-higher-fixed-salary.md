Two offers, and the one that looks bigger is built differently. The first is a straightforward fixed salary. The second is a slightly lower fixed salary with a target bonus attached, described in the conversation as "usually paid in full". Add the numbers and the second wins. Look at what is actually guaranteed and it may not, and in data and AI roles the arithmetic tilts further than most candidates realise — because the things that determine whether a bonus is paid are rarely things you control.

## The Shapes Variable Pay Takes

**Individual performance bonus.** Tied to objectives agreed with your manager. The most common, and the most dependent on how well those objectives are written.

**Company or team result bonus.** Paid if the organisation or unit hits its targets, regardless of your contribution.

**Profit sharing.** A share of results distributed across staff, sometimes under a collective arrangement, sometimes discretionary.

**Long-term incentives.** Payments or awards vesting over several years, common in larger and listed companies.

**A thirteenth month,** which is worth separating out: where it is contractual, it is deferred fixed pay rather than variable pay, and should be counted as such.

## The Dutch Context

Bonus culture in the Netherlands is modest by international standards. In most sectors, variable pay for technical staff is a small share of total compensation, and many employers — particularly under collective agreements, in the public sector, in healthcare and in education — have no individual bonus at all.

The financial sector is a specific case. Dutch legislation on remuneration policy in financial undertakings imposes a cap on variable pay as a proportion of fixed pay for most staff, with limited exceptions. If you are considering a role at a bank, insurer or asset manager, ask how the cap applies to your position, and verify current rules rather than relying on a summary.

Pay transparency obligations are also changing the conversation. Employers are increasingly expected to be explicit about pay ranges and the criteria behind pay decisions, which makes it more reasonable than it used to be to ask how bonus targets are set and how they have actually paid out.

## What to Check Before You Value a Bonus at Anything

- **Is it discretionary or contractual?** A discretionary bonus can be reduced or skipped. Ask where it is described: the contract, a scheme document, or nowhere.
- **What has it actually paid out for the last three years?** For your team, not company-wide. Employers who answer this readily usually have good news; vagueness is itself the answer.
- **Who sets the targets, and when?** Targets set in March for a year that started in January, by someone who does not understand your work, are not targets you control.
- **Individual, team or company?** A company-wide trigger means your bonus depends on sales in a division you have never met.
- **What happens if you join mid-year or leave mid-year?** Many schemes pay pro rata on joining and nothing at all if you have resigned before the payment date — which can lock you in for months.
- **Is there a clawback provision?**
- **Is the bonus pensionable?** Frequently it is not, which quietly reduces its long-term value.

## Why Fixed Usually Wins in Data Roles

**Your outcomes are not cleanly attributable.** A model's business impact depends on adoption, data quality, a department's willingness to change and a dozen decisions made by other people.

**The cycles are long.** Meaningful data work often spans more than a year, while bonus periods are annual, so the work you do this year pays off in someone else's bonus year.

**Targets get written badly.** Because managers struggle to specify good objectives for technical work, targets frequently default to activity — projects delivered, models built — which rewards the wrong things and pushes you towards shipping rather than shipping well.

**Foundational work is invisible to bonus schemes.** Fixing pipelines, writing documentation, retiring systems and improving monitoring are exactly the work that does not appear in a target and exactly the work that makes a data function viable.

## The Knock-On Effects Nobody Mentions

Fixed salary does more than arrive every month.

**Pension accrual** is usually based on fixed salary, and often excludes bonuses.

**Holiday allowance** is calculated on fixed salary.

**Mortgage capacity.** Lenders treat variable income cautiously, typically using an average of past years and sometimes discounting it. Two offers with identical totals can produce noticeably different borrowing capacity.

**Statutory calculations,** such as the basis for a transition payment, follow defined rules in which fixed salary and certain fixed allowances carry more weight than uncertain variable elements.

**Future increases** are usually expressed as a percentage of fixed salary, so a lower base compounds against you for years.

## When Variable Pay Is Genuinely Worth Taking

- The scheme is contractual, with a documented calculation you can follow.
- It has paid at or near target for several consecutive years, and the employer will say so.
- The targets are within your influence and are set at the start of the period.
- The organisation is financially strong and the variable part is a modest share of the total.
- You are senior enough that your contribution is genuinely attributable.
- You would take the role regardless, and the bonus is upside rather than the reason.

## When to Push for Fixed

- The bonus is discretionary and nobody will discuss past payouts.
- Targets are company-wide and unrelated to your work.
- You need borrowing capacity in the next couple of years.
- The employer is using the target figure to close a gap in the fixed offer.
- You are early in your career, when compounding the base matters most.
- The scheme conditions payment on still being employed on a date months after the period ends.

## How to Negotiate the Swap

Ask plainly whether the target bonus can be converted, in whole or in part, into fixed salary. Many employers will do it, because predictable fixed cost is easier to plan than a variable one, and because the bonus pool is often separate from the salary budget — which is also why some employers cannot.

If conversion is refused, ask for the scheme document, ask for the payout history in writing, and then value the offer using the lower of the target and the historical average rather than the headline figure.

And compare the two offers on what is guaranteed. If the fixed-salary offer wins on guaranteed pay, the variable offer is asking you to accept risk in exchange for a possibility — which is a trade worth making consciously, if at all.

## Deferred Awards and Long-Term Incentives

Larger employers sometimes offer a third element: an award that pays out over several years rather than annually. Listed companies use share plans, and some organisations defer part of a bonus by policy or by regulatory requirement, particularly in financial services.

These are not the same as an annual bonus and should be assessed separately.

**Vesting is the whole question.** An award that pays over three or four years is worth nothing if you leave in year two, and the conditions attached to leaving matter more than the headline value. Ask what happens if you resign, if you are made redundant, and if the employer ends the contract for other reasons — the treatment often differs.

**Deferral is a retention device.** That is its purpose, and it works: people stay for the vesting date. Understand that you are being asked to trade future flexibility for value you may not receive.

**Performance conditions may sit on top.** Some awards vest only if the organisation meets targets, which adds a second uncertainty to the first.

**Tax timing varies.** When an award is taxed, and on what value, depends on its structure and the current rules — verify with an adviser rather than assuming, because the outcome can differ substantially between plan types.

**Value it conservatively.** A reasonable approach is to treat deferred awards as worth nothing in the comparison, and to be pleased if they arrive. If an offer only wins once you count them at face value, the offer is asking you to pay for the privilege of staying.

**Check what happens at the next job change.** Deferred value is also the thing that makes leaving expensive later, which is precisely the point of it. Before accepting, work out what you would forfeit if a better role appeared eighteen months in, and decide whether you are comfortable with that constraint. Candidates who skip this step discover it at exactly the moment they most want flexibility, and some stay a year longer than they wanted to for an amount that looked larger on paper than it felt in practice.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Amsterdam who asked for three years of payout history

A data scientist receives two offers. One is a straightforward fixed salary at a logistics company. The other, at a larger commercial organisation, is slightly lower fixed with a target bonus that would make it the better package if paid.

She asks three questions about the bonus. Is it contractual — it is discretionary. What did it pay for her prospective team in the last three years — full one year, half the next, nothing the third, because a different division missed its target. And is it pensionable — it is not.

She asks whether the target can be converted into fixed salary. The employer declines, explaining that the bonus comes from a different budget.

She then values the offer using the historical average rather than the target, which puts it below the logistics offer on guaranteed pay, below it on pension accrual, and below it again for the mortgage application she is planning next year.

She takes the logistics role. Two years later she hears that the bonus paid nothing again, because of a division she would never have worked with.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate contractual deferred pay, such as a thirteenth month, from genuinely variable pay, and value them differently.
- Before valuing a bonus, establish whether it is discretionary, what it has paid for your team in the last three years, who sets targets, and what happens if you join or leave mid-year.
- Data roles suit fixed pay because outcomes are not cleanly attributable, cycles are long and foundational work never appears in a target.
- Fixed salary drives pension accrual, holiday allowance, mortgage capacity, statutory calculation bases and future percentage increases.
- Ask whether the target bonus can be converted into fixed salary; if not, value the offer at the historical average rather than the headline target.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two offers) Should I prefer a bonus or a higher fixed salary?
In data roles, usually fixed. Your outcomes depend heavily on other people, work cycles exceed bonus periods, and fixed salary drives pension, holiday allowance, borrowing capacity and future increases.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing a scheme) What should I ask about a bonus?
Whether it is contractual or discretionary, what it paid for that team in the last three years, who sets the targets and when, and whether it is pensionable.

### (Scenario: candidate in finance) Are there limits on bonuses in the Netherlands?
In the financial sector, legislation caps variable pay as a proportion of fixed pay for most staff, with limited exceptions. Ask how it applies to your role and check current rules.

### (Scenario: candidate planning a mortgage) Does variable pay affect borrowing?
Usually yes. Lenders treat variable income cautiously, often averaging past years or discounting it, so two packages with the same total can support different borrowing.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) Can I swap a bonus for fixed salary?
Often, and it is a normal request. Where it is refused, ask for the scheme document and the payout history, then value the offer at the historical average rather than the target.
