The role is attractive and the team is not here. Your manager sits in Dublin, or Boston, or Singapore, and you would be one of a small number of people — sometimes the only person — working from the Netherlands. Everything about the job description looks familiar. What changes is the shape of your day, how quickly you get answers, who sees your work, and whether the evening belongs to you. Distributed teams work extremely well in some configurations and grind people down in others, and the difference is largely arithmetic: how many hours you genuinely share with the people you depend on.

## The Configurations, From Easiest to Hardest

**Western Europe.** An hour either way at most. Effectively a local team with travel involved. Rarely an issue.

**United Kingdom and Ireland.** One hour behind. Almost a full working day of overlap, and the practical friction is minimal.

**Eastern seaboard of the United States.** Five to six hours behind. Your afternoon is their morning, so roughly three to four hours overlap if you work into the early evening. This is the most common difficult case for Dutch professionals, and it is workable with rules.

**United States west coast.** Eight to nine hours behind. Overlap only exists if you work evenings or they work early. Sustained, this is hard.

**India and South East Asia.** Four to eight hours ahead. Your morning is their afternoon. Overlap exists at the start of your day, which many people find easier than evenings.

**Australia and New Zealand.** Almost no natural overlap. Genuinely asynchronous or genuinely painful, with little in between.

Before anything else, write down how many hours you would actually share with the colleagues you need most. That number predicts your experience better than any description of company culture.

## The Overlap Window Is the Whole Question

What matters is not the size of the gap but what has to happen inside the shared hours.

If the overlap is used for decisions, reviews and unblocking, three hours can be plenty. If the overlap is filled with standing meetings, you have no shared time left for the things that actually need a conversation, and every question costs a day.

Ask specifically: which meetings are in the overlap window, who chairs them, and what proportion of the shared time they consume. A team with two hours of overlap and ninety minutes of recurring meetings is telling you something about how the rest of your questions will be answered.

## What Works Asynchronously in AI Work, and What Does Not

**Works well.** Training runs, data processing, exploratory analysis, writing code, documentation, structured reviews with written comments, dashboards and reports. Much of the technical work is naturally solitary.

**Works with effort.** Code review, design discussion, incident response, prioritisation. Possible asynchronously with good written practice, slower than in person.

**Works badly.** Debugging something nobody understands yet, disentangling why a dataset is wrong, early problem definition with a stakeholder, and any conversation that requires several rounds of "what if" in quick succession. These are the moments where a half-day round trip turns an afternoon into a week.

A role heavy on the first category can be excellent across time zones. A role heavy on the third will frustrate you regardless of how well the company writes things down.

## The Hidden Costs

**Evenings become negotiable.** Even a good arrangement produces occasional calls at nineteen hundred. Occasional is fine; three nights a week is a different job from the one you accepted.

**Decisions happen while you sleep.** You wake up to a decision already made and often already being implemented. Influencing it means being in the conversation, which means being awake for it.

**Review latency slows your growth.** A one-day round trip on feedback is tolerable once. Repeated through a project, it changes how much you learn.

**Visibility concentrates at the centre of gravity.** Promotions, interesting projects and informal trust accumulate where most of the team sits. This is not malice; it is proximity.

**Isolation compounds with remoteness.** If you are also working from home, with no local colleagues at all, the social floor of the job is very low.

## Ask Where the Centre of Gravity Is

The most useful question in the interview is where most of the team sits and where your manager sits. The answers place you in one of three situations.

**A genuinely distributed team** with people spread across several regions and no dominant location. These teams usually have developed real asynchronous practice, because they had no choice. The best case.

**A centred team with satellites.** Most people in one location, you elsewhere. Practices are built around the majority; you adapt. Workable, with explicit arrangements.

**A centred team with one satellite, which is you.** The hardest version. Every meeting is scheduled for their convenience, and you are the exception in every process.

Ask how many people work outside the main location, and how long they have been there. Longevity is a good indicator that the arrangement works.

## Dutch Practicalities Worth Settling

If you are employed in the Netherlands, Dutch working time rules apply to you regardless of where your colleagues sit, including provisions on maximum working hours and daily and weekly rest. Evening calls that push into your rest period are not a neutral matter of preference.

Your employer also has duty-of-care obligations for your working conditions. A pattern of late-evening meetings is a workload issue, not merely a scheduling one, and it is legitimate to raise it as such.

Two other things are worth settling in writing before you start: whether evening meetings are compensated in time or otherwise, and how on-call or incident response works when the rest of the team is asleep. If incidents are handled by whoever is awake, being the only person in this time zone can mean being permanently first responder for European hours.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **How many hours of overlap would I have with the people I depend on?**
- **What is scheduled inside that window, and what is left for actual conversation?**
- **How many people work outside the main location, and for how long?**
- **Where does my manager sit, and how often would we speak?**
- **How are decisions recorded for people who were not present?**
- **What is the expectation for evening availability, and how is it compensated?**
- **How does incident response work across the time difference?**
- **How often would I travel, and who pays?**
- **How are performance and promotion decided for people outside the main location?**

## When It Works Well

- The team is genuinely distributed rather than centred elsewhere with you attached.
- Your work has long autonomous stretches and clear interfaces.
- Overlap is protected for decisions and unblocking rather than filled with meetings.
- There is at least one other person in or near your time zone.
- Your manager is deliberate about written communication and one-to-one time.
- The organisation funds regular travel, and says so in writing.

## When to Decline

- The overlap is under two hours and the work requires frequent collaboration.
- You would be the only person outside the main location, with no arrangements made.
- Evening meetings are described as "occasionally, when needed" without any limit.
- Nothing is written down anywhere.
- You are early in your career and depend on fast feedback.
- Incident response would fall to you by default because of when you are awake.

## How to Make It Work If You Accept

Define core hours and publish them. A fixed window when you are reliably available, and a clear end to your day, prevents the slow creep of availability.

Batch your questions. In a distributed team, five questions asked together at the start of their day beat five questions asked as they occur to you.

Over-document. Decisions, context, what you tried. Writing is how people who were asleep stay included, and it is how you stay included when it is your turn to sleep.

Ask for rotation. If one recurring meeting always lands in your evening, propose alternating times so the inconvenience is shared. Reasonable teams agree; the reaction tells you a lot.

Meet in person early. One trip in the first months does more for your standing than a year of good calls.

Protect the boundary from the start. The first quarter establishes what is normal, and it is far easier to set expectations then than to withdraw availability later.

## Communication Norms Travel Badly

Time zones are the visible difference. The invisible one is that teams communicate differently, and Dutch professionals working in foreign-centred teams encounter this constantly.

**Directness reads differently abroad.** Dutch working culture prizes saying plainly that an approach will not work. In several other business cultures the same sentence lands as an attack on the person who proposed it, and the reaction — polite agreement followed by nothing changing — is easy to misread as consent. If your feedback is not producing change, consider that it may not have been heard as feedback.

**Silence means different things.** In some teams, no response means agreement; in others it means the question was not understood and nobody wants to say so. Across time zones you cannot read the room, so ask explicitly for a yes or a no rather than for objections.

**Written communication carries the tone.** A three-line message that is efficient in Dutch working style can read as curt to colleagues elsewhere, and a message that is warm in another culture can read to you as evasive padding. Neither side is doing anything wrong.

**Hierarchy expectations vary.** In flat Dutch teams, challenging a senior person's proposal is normal. In more hierarchical settings, a junior colleague's agreement may mean nothing about what they actually think, and decisions travel through people rather than through open discussion.

**Meetings mean different things.** In some cultures a meeting is where a decision is made; in others it is where a decision already made is announced, with the real discussion happening beforehand in private conversations you are not part of because you were asleep.

The practical response is not to change who you are. It is to be explicit: state what you mean, ask directly whether people agree, confirm decisions in writing, and ask a colleague at the main location to tell you when you are being misread. Most of the friction people attribute to time zones is actually this, and unlike the clock it can be fixed by asking.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Utrecht with a team in Boston

A data scientist joins a company whose data team sits in Boston, six hours behind. The work is interesting and the pay is above the local market. The overlap is her afternoon and their morning: about four hours.

The first two months go badly. Two standing meetings occupy most of the overlap, her code reviews come back overnight, and she finds herself working until eight to catch people.

She raises it as a structure problem rather than a complaint. Three things change. The daily team meeting moves to the start of their day and becomes written on two days a week. Her manager takes a standing thirty minutes with her at the beginning of the overlap window, before anything else can consume it. And the team agrees that reviews requested before her end of day are handled that same evening in Boston, so she has feedback when she starts.

She also flies over once a quarter for a week, paid by the employer, and the difference in how her work is discussed afterwards is obvious to her.

Two years in, the arrangement works. Her conclusion is that the time difference was never the real problem — the problem was that nobody had designed for it, and asking for that design was entirely reasonable.

## Key Takeaways

- Count the hours of genuine overlap with the people you depend on; that number predicts your experience better than any statement about culture.
- What occupies the overlap matters as much as its size — meetings that fill it leave nothing for the conversations that actually need it.
- Autonomous technical work travels well across time zones; debugging, early problem definition and messy data investigation do not.
- Dutch working time and duty-of-care rules apply to you wherever your colleagues sit; settle evening availability and incident response in writing.
- Establish core hours, batch questions, over-document, rotate awkward meeting times and travel early to build standing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a role with a foreign team) How much overlap do I need?
It depends on the work, but under two hours makes collaborative roles difficult. Count the hours you would share with the people you actually depend on, not with the company as a whole.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about evenings) Can my employer expect evening calls?
Dutch working time rules on maximum hours and rest apply regardless of where colleagues sit, and workload is a duty-of-care matter. Agree expectations and compensation in writing before starting.

### (Scenario: candidate concerned about career) Does working outside the main location affect promotion?
It can, because visibility accumulates where most of the team sits. Ask how performance and promotion are decided for remote locations, and travel to the centre periodically.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the team) What is the best question to ask in the interview?
Where most of the team and your manager sit, how many people work outside that location and how long they have stayed. Longevity indicates the arrangement genuinely works.

### (Scenario: candidate already in such a role) How do I fix a bad arrangement?
Raise it as a structure problem: protect the overlap for decisions, move or halve recurring meetings, agree review turnaround and rotate inconvenient meeting times.
