Three years into a data career, the question arrives in some form: should you go deep into one thing or stay broad enough to take whatever comes? In a large market the answer leans towards specialisation, because there are enough employers to sustain any niche. The Netherlands is not a large market. It is a small, dense, highly sectoral one, where a specialism can mean a dozen realistic employers nationally — or three within your commuting radius. That changes the calculation, and it makes the usual advice about "finding your niche" incomplete rather than wrong.

## What Each Path Actually Means

**Specialising** means going deep on a technical area — computer vision, NLP, optimisation, MLOps, causal inference, time series — or on a domain: medical imaging, energy markets, insurance risk, greenhouse horticulture. Your value comes from knowing things most candidates do not.

**Staying generalist** means remaining competent across the pipeline: framing a problem, working with data, modelling, evaluating, deploying, explaining. Your value comes from being usable on whatever the organisation needs next.

Most careers are not one or the other. The useful version of the question is: which direction should your next two years lean?

## The Dutch Market Constraint

Two numbers shape this decision, and only you can estimate them: how many employers in your specialism exist nationally, and how many exist within the distance you are willing to travel.

For some specialisms the answer is comfortable. MLOps, forecasting, geospatial data and general applied machine learning appear across manufacturing, logistics, energy, insurance, healthcare and government in every region. For others it is narrow: medical imaging AI concentrates around a handful of clusters, naval and defence systems around specific locations, chemical process AI in particular industrial areas.

Narrow is not bad. Narrow plus geographically concentrated plus far from where you live is what creates problems, because your next job may require moving, and your negotiating position depends on employers you cannot reach.

The practical test is straightforward: search your specialism by category and distance and count the employers, not the vacancies. If the number within your radius is fewer than three, treat specialisation as a decision about geography as much as about skills.

## What Specialisation Buys You

**Scarcity value.** Fewer qualified candidates means better offers and more attention from employers who need exactly your profile.

**Faster hiring.** Specialists are often approached directly rather than competing through general processes.

**Depth of work.** You get the interesting problems rather than the entry-level parts of many problems.

**A recognisable identity.** "The person who does radar signal processing" is easier to remember and recommend than "a data scientist".

**Compounding knowledge.** Domain understanding accumulates in a way general skills do not; five years in insurance data is genuinely hard to replicate.

## What Specialisation Costs You

**Fewer employers.** Your options shrink, and with them your leverage.

**Geographic dependence.** Many specialisms cluster, which can turn a job change into a house move.

**Technology risk.** A specialism tied to a particular approach can shrink when the approach changes. Anyone who specialised narrowly in one NLP technique before the arrival of large language models learned this.

**Sector risk.** If your domain contracts — a regulatory change, a market shift, a company closing — the specialism moves with it.

**Harder re-entry.** Coming back to generalist work after years of depth is possible but often means explaining away the specialism.

## What Staying Generalist Buys and Costs

Generalists keep options open, move between sectors easily, and are the obvious hire for the very common situation of an organisation needing its first or second data person. In the Dutch market, where a large share of demand sits at SMEs, regional employers and public bodies that need someone to do everything, generalists are genuinely well served.

The cost is competition and a ceiling. Generalist roles attract many applicants, and at senior levels employers increasingly want evidence of depth in something. A generalist with ten years of experience and no distinctive strength competes with candidates who have five and a clear one.

## The Shape That Works Best in a Small Market

The most robust profile in the Netherlands is usually not pure depth or pure breadth, but breadth with one or two pronounced strengths — competent across the pipeline, genuinely strong in something specific, and ideally with domain knowledge in a sector that exists in many places.

The combination that travels particularly well is a technical strength plus a portable domain. Forecasting plus retail, optimisation plus logistics, computer vision plus manufacturing quality, data governance plus a regulated sector. These pairs exist in most provinces, so your specialism does not dictate where you live.

## A Decision Framework

| Your situation | Lean towards |
|---|---|
| Fewer than three employers in your niche within your radius | Breadth, or accept relocation as part of the plan |
| You are early in your career with no clear preference | Breadth, while noticing what you enjoy |
| You have a domain you know deeply from a previous career | Specialise on that intersection |
| Your specialism depends on one technology trend | Broaden the foundations underneath it |
| You want senior technical roles | Depth in something, evidenced |
| You want to lead teams | Breadth plus enough depth to earn respect |
| You are in a region with one dominant industry | Specialise into it, or plan for mobility |
| You want maximum employability nearby | Generalist with strong engineering practice |

## Specialisations With Unusually Good Prospects Here

Some directions are worth naming, because Dutch demand for them is structural rather than fashionable.

**Data governance and AI compliance.** Obligations under the EU AI Act, combined with GDPR and sector rules, have created roles that barely existed a few years ago, in almost every sector and region.

**Production engineering for machine learning.** The gap between models that work and models that run is the most consistently unmet need across Dutch employers.

**Forecasting and optimisation.** Logistics, energy, retail and healthcare all need it, everywhere.

**Geospatial data.** Water management, spatial planning, agriculture and energy infrastructure make this a distinctly Dutch strength.

**Domain-heavy imaging.** Manufacturing inspection, medical imaging and agricultural vision cluster, but the underlying skills transfer between them.

## How to Test a Specialism Before Committing

You do not have to decide in the abstract.

**Do one project in it, properly.** Six months on a real problem tells you more than a year of thinking about it.

**Talk to three people who do it daily.** Ask what the boring eighty percent looks like, not the interesting twenty.

**Check the vacancies.** Read ten postings in the specialism and see whether the daily work described appeals to you.

**Check the geography.** Count the employers within your radius. Do this before, not after.

**Notice your own behaviour.** Which problems do you volunteer for? What do you read when nobody is assigning it? That is usually a better signal than market analysis.

## Specialising Inside Your Current Job

Changing employer is the obvious way to change direction, and often the slowest. Most people can begin specialising where they already are, at considerably lower risk.

**Volunteer for the projects in that direction.** In almost every organisation, some work goes unclaimed. Taking the vision project, the optimisation question or the governance documentation nobody wants is the cheapest entry into a specialism.

**Say it out loud to your manager.** Managers allocate work partly on stated preferences. A single conversation — "over the next year I would like to move towards forecasting work" — changes what lands on your desk more often than people expect.

**Use the development budget deliberately.** Most Dutch collective agreements and employers provide one. Spending it on a coherent direction beats a scattered collection of courses.

**Build one visible artefact.** A model in production, a documented pipeline, an internal guideline others follow. Specialisation becomes credible when someone else uses your work.

**Then move, if you need to.** After a year of deliberate steps you apply as someone with demonstrated direction rather than stated interest, which is a materially stronger position.

The exception is an organisation where the specialism genuinely does not exist — no imaging work at an insurer, no optimisation at a small agency. There, changing employer is the only route, and recognising that early saves a wasted year.

## Real example

### An engineer in Eindhoven who specialised deliberately

A data scientist in the Eindhoven region works as a generalist for four years: dashboards, a churn model, a forecasting project, some data engineering. She is competent at everything and distinctive at nothing, and she notices that interviews increasingly ask what she is strong in.

She looks at her surroundings honestly. Within thirty minutes of home there are high-tech manufacturers, a medical technology cluster and several suppliers — all of them needing vision and inspection work. She has enjoyed the two image-related projects she did more than anything else.

She specialises deliberately: one internal project on visual inspection, a course in image processing fundamentals, and an explicit conversation with her manager about direction. Eighteen months later she moves to a manufacturer twenty minutes away as a computer vision engineer, at a level she could not have reached as a generalist.

The specialism was narrow nationally and abundant locally. That combination is what made it safe.

## Depth Without Narrowing: What the T-Shape Means in Practice

The familiar advice is to build a "T-shaped" profile: broad across the field, deep in one area. It is good advice and usually too vague to act on. In practice it means three specific things.

**The horizontal bar is the pipeline, not a list of tools.** Being able to frame a problem, assess whether the data supports it, build something reasonable, evaluate it honestly, get it into production and explain it to the people affected. That sequence is what makes you usable anywhere, and it does not go out of date when frameworks change.

**The vertical bar should be deep enough to be visible.** Depth that only you know about is not depth. It becomes real when someone else uses your work, when you are the person asked about it internally, or when you can point to a system running in production that you designed.

**The join matters more than either bar.** The valuable position is usually the intersection: forecasting plus logistics, vision plus manufacturing quality, governance plus a regulated sector. Intersections are less crowded than either component, and they are what employers describe when they say they cannot find anyone.

A practical test: if your current role disappeared tomorrow, could you name three employers within your radius who would want specifically what you are strong at? If yes, the vertical bar is real. If not, it is an interest rather than a specialism — which is fine, provided you know which one you have.

## Key Takeaways

- In a market the size of the Netherlands, specialisation is partly a geographic decision: count employers within your radius, not vacancies nationally.
- Specialising buys scarcity value, depth and identity; it costs optionality, mobility and resilience to technology or sector shifts.
- Generalists suit the very common Dutch situation of first and second data hires at SMEs, regional employers and public bodies.
- The most robust profile is breadth with one or two pronounced strengths, ideally pairing a technical strength with a portable domain.
- Test a specialism with a real project, conversations and a count of local employers before committing your next two years to it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: mid-career data scientist) Should I specialise or stay a generalist?
Lean towards breadth with one or two pronounced strengths. Pure specialisation works well when enough employers in that niche exist within your travel radius.

### (Scenario: candidate in a smaller region) Is specialising risky outside the Randstad?
It can be, if the specialism clusters elsewhere. Check how many employers within your radius need it before committing, and consider pairing it with a portable domain.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about technology shifts) What happens if my specialism becomes obsolete?
Specialisms tied to a single technique are the most fragile. Keep the foundations underneath — statistics, engineering, evaluation — strong enough to move.

### (Scenario: senior candidate) Do employers expect depth at senior level?
Increasingly yes. A senior candidate with no distinctive strength competes poorly against one with clear, evidenced depth in something.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure which niche) How do I choose a specialism?
Do one real project in it, read ten vacancies, speak to three practitioners about the unglamorous parts, and count the employers near you.
