The offer arrives on Tuesday and they would like an answer by Friday. It is a good offer. It is also not the one you were hoping for, because a second employer — the one you actually want — has you at second-interview stage with a final round somewhere next week. Candidates in this position tend to do one of three things: panic and accept, stall clumsily and damage both processes, or accept and then renege, which is worse than it sounds. There is a fourth approach, and it is mostly a matter of asking two straightforward questions quickly rather than agonising privately for three days.

## Why Deadlines Exist

Some reasons are genuine. A team has budget for one hire this quarter and a second candidate waiting. A project starts on a fixed date. A manager has been given an approval window that expires. Recruitment consumes an enormous amount of a hiring manager's time, and they want it to end.

Other reasons are tactical. A short deadline prevents you from comparing, limits negotiation and stops you from using the offer elsewhere. A deadline of forty-eight hours on a permanent role, after a process that took two months, is usually about pressure rather than logistics.

The distinction matters because it tells you what kind of employer you are dealing with — which is itself information about the job.

## What Is Normal in the Netherlands

There is no general statutory waiting period for considering a job offer, and practice varies. That said, expectations here are reasonably settled: a few working days to consider a written offer is ordinary and rarely questioned, and a week is common for senior roles or where a partner and a relocation are involved.

Recruitment practice in the Netherlands is also guided by the **NVP recruitment code**, which sets expectations for how employers handle applicants — transparency about the process and timely communication among them. It is not law, and many employers follow it in spirit.

Two practical points. First, a written offer with the terms set out is what you consider — an enthusiastic phone call is not something you can weigh. Second, with pay transparency rules increasingly shaping how ranges are communicated, more employers now put the salary position in writing early, which removes one reason for delay.

## Your Actual Leverage

Candidates consistently underestimate it. The employer has spent weeks interviewing, has probably rejected other candidates, and has a team waiting. Restarting the process costs them far more than giving you four extra days.

A reasonable request for time, made once, politely and with a clear date, is almost never refused by an employer worth working for. It also signals that you are deliberate rather than desperate, which does you no harm at all.

The leverage disappears in exactly one situation: when you ask repeatedly, or when you ask without committing to a date. Vagueness reads as shopping around; a specific date reads as a plan.

## How to Ask for Time

Keep it short and concrete. Thank them, confirm your genuine interest, say you want to give the decision the seriousness it deserves, and propose a specific date — ideally one that is only a few days later than theirs.

You do not have to explain that another process is running. You may, and there are two schools of thought. Saying so is honest and sometimes accelerates everything; it also tells them you may leave, which in rare cases changes their enthusiasm. A middle path works well: "I am in the final stage of one other process and want to be able to accept your offer without reservation. I can confirm on Wednesday."

Ask once. Deliver on the date you named, whatever the answer.

## How to Accelerate the Other Process

This is the part candidates neglect, and it is where most of the resolution actually comes from.

Contact the second employer immediately — the same day, not after two days of deliberation. Be direct: you have received a written offer with a deadline, you are genuinely interested in their role, and you wanted to tell them rather than disappear. Ask whether it is possible to bring their remaining steps forward.

Employers respond to this far more often than candidates expect. Interviews get compressed, final rounds move up, and decisions that would have taken two weeks take three days. A hiring manager who wants you will move; one who will not move has told you something useful about how much they want you, and that is worth knowing before you turn down a real offer for a hypothetical one.

Do not exaggerate the deadline or invent an offer. Recruitment circles in Dutch AI are small, and this kind of thing surfaces.

## If You Must Decide Without the Second Answer

Sometimes nothing moves and you have to choose. In that case, judge the offer in your hand on its own merits, not against an imagined version of the other role.

Ask yourself one question: if the second process did not exist, would I be pleased to accept this? If the answer is yes, accept it. A concrete good offer beats a hypothetical better one, and the second process may well have ended in a rejection you never saw coming.

If the answer is no — if you would only take it as insurance — then declining is the more honest choice, and usually the better one. Accepting a role you do not want, planning to keep looking, costs you the next six months and your reputation with that employer.

## What Not to Do

**Do not accept and then renege.** Once you sign, a contract exists, and withdrawing before you start is a breach with potential consequences. Beyond the legal question, the Dutch AI market is small enough that the manager you did this to will be at another employer within a few years.

**Do not use a probationary period as an escape plan.** A probationary period is a genuine mutual trial, and it exists within limits set by law and the type of contract. Treating it as a way to hedge two offers is both cynical and visible.

**Do not stall without answering.** Silence past a deadline reads as disrespect and sometimes ends the offer.

**Do not play the two employers against each other explicitly.** Using one to negotiate the other is different from being open about your timeline, and experienced hiring managers can tell the difference immediately.

## If You Accept and the Other Offer Arrives Afterwards

It happens. Handle it in the order that costs least.

If you have not yet signed anything, tell the first employer promptly and honestly. Awkward, but clean.

If you have signed, the position is different. You have an agreement, and there is a start date and a team planning around you. In most cases the right course is to honour it, work there properly, and accept that the other opportunity came too late. Employers you decline gracefully often come back; employers you abandon after signing rarely do.

If the circumstances are exceptional, speak to the employer directly and early rather than disappearing, and take advice about your obligations before doing anything.

## Red Flags in a Pressured Offer

- A deadline of one or two days after a lengthy process.
- Refusal to put the offer in writing before you commit.
- Pressure framed as a test of your enthusiasm.
- Terms that change between the verbal offer and the document.
- Reluctance to let you speak to a future colleague before deciding.

These are not merely negotiating tactics. Employers who treat candidates this way at the most careful moment of the relationship tend to behave similarly afterwards, and that is worth weighing in the decision itself.

## What a Written Offer Should Contain

You cannot meaningfully consider an offer you have not seen in full, and a deadline attached to an incomplete offer is not a real deadline. Before the clock starts, ask for these in writing.

**Salary and position in the scale.** Not only the amount but where it sits in the range, since that determines how much room exists for growth without a promotion.

**Contract type and duration.** Permanent or fixed-term, and if fixed-term, for how long and with what intention afterwards.

**Hours and working pattern.** Contracted hours, expected working days, any arrangement about home working and whether it is contractual or a policy.

**Holiday, holiday allowance and any additional payments,** including a thirteenth month or bonus scheme and the conditions attached to it.

**Pension.** Which scheme, when accrual starts and what the employer contributes. This is the item most often omitted and least often asked about.

**Travel and expenses.** Reimbursement for commuting, home working costs and equipment.

**Start date and notice expectations.**

**Probationary period,** if any, and its length.

**Anything you negotiated.** A training budget, a specific project, a review point, a day off per week, a commitment about scope. A term agreed verbally and absent from the document does not exist, and the person who promised it may not be your manager in a year.

If the employer cannot produce this within a day or two, that is worth noticing — and it is also a legitimate reason to ask for the deadline to start from when the complete offer arrives, which most employers accept without argument.

## Real example

### An engineer in Tilburg who asked two questions in one afternoon

An ML engineer receives a written offer from a logistics company on a Tuesday, with a request to respond by Friday. He is also in a process with a research-oriented employer whose final interview is provisionally in ten days.

He does two things that afternoon. He replies to the logistics company thanking them, confirming his interest, and asking whether he can respond by the following Wednesday because he wants to accept without reservation. They agree the same day. He then emails the second employer, explains he has a written offer with a deadline, says he is genuinely interested in their role, and asks whether the final stage can be brought forward.

The second employer moves the interview to Friday and promises a decision by Monday. On Monday they decline him — the role goes to a candidate with more domain experience.

On Wednesday he accepts the logistics offer, without the lingering question of what the other one would have been, and with a hiring manager who noticed that he asked once, named a date and kept to it.

## Key Takeaways

- Distinguish genuine deadlines from tactical ones; a very short deadline after a long process is usually about pressure.
- A few working days to consider a written offer is ordinary in the Netherlands, and a single polite request with a specific date is rarely refused.
- Contact the second employer the same day and ask them to accelerate — this is where most of the resolution comes from.
- If you must choose blind, judge the offer in hand on its own merits: would you be pleased to accept it if the other process did not exist?
- Never accept and renege, and do not treat a probationary period as a hedge; the Dutch AI market is small and reputations travel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate with a short deadline) How much time can I ask for?
A few working days is ordinary, and a week is common for senior roles or where relocation is involved. Ask once, politely, and name a specific date you will keep.

### (Scenario: candidate in a second process) Should I tell the other employer I have an offer?
Yes, the same day. Being direct about a written offer and a deadline frequently accelerates the remaining steps, and an employer who will not move has told you something useful.

### (Scenario: candidate tempted to hedge) Can I accept and change my mind later?
Once you sign, an agreement exists and withdrawing is a breach with potential consequences. Beyond that, the Dutch AI market is small and it will be remembered.

### (Scenario: candidate forced to decide) What if the second process cannot be sped up?
Judge the offer in hand alone: if you would be pleased to accept it were the other process not running, accept it. Otherwise decline honestly rather than taking it as insurance.

### (Scenario: candidate under pressure) Is a very short deadline a warning sign?
Often. A one- or two-day deadline after a long process, refusal to put terms in writing, or pressure framed as a test of enthusiasm all say something about how the employer will behave later.
