You resigned this morning. By the afternoon your manager has been to HR, and a counteroffer is on the table: more salary, perhaps a new title, a promise about the project you have been asking for. It feels good — being wanted usually does — and it is also the moment when a well-considered decision can quietly unravel. Counteroffers are common for AI and data professionals, because replacing someone who knows the company's data landscape is genuinely expensive. That does not make them a bad deal. It makes them a deal that deserves the same scrutiny as the offer you were about to accept.

This article provides general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, consult an employment lawyer, your union or the free information service **Juridisch Loket**.

## Why Counteroffers Happen

**Replacement cost is real.** Recruiting a replacement takes months, costs recruitment fees or internal time, and the new person needs a long ramp-up before they understand the systems and data you know.

**Continuity risk.** If you own a model in production, a pipeline nobody else understands or a relationship with a business unit, your departure creates operational exposure.

**Timing pressure.** A departure during a critical project, an audit or a system migration is inconvenient in a way that money can temporarily solve.

**It is easier than fixing the cause.** Raising a salary is a single decision. Changing a team structure, a roadmap or a manager is not.

None of these reasons is dishonourable. But notice that most are about the employer's short-term needs, not about your long-term development.

## The Question That Decides It

Write down why you started looking, before the counteroffer entered the picture. Then ask whether the counteroffer addresses that reason.

| Why you were leaving | Does money fix it? |
|---|---|
| Underpaid relative to the market | Often yes, if the correction is real and lasting |
| No path to more senior work | Rarely — unless a concrete role change is agreed in writing |
| Models never reach production; no investment in data | No — this is an organisational choice |
| Manager or team culture | Almost never |
| Commute or inflexibility | Only if working arrangements change concretely |
| Boredom with the domain | No |
| Company direction or financial instability | No |

If the counteroffer solves the actual reason, staying can be entirely rational. If it solves a different problem, you are being paid to postpone the decision by a year.

## The Claims You Will Hear, and What They Are Worth

Recruiters often quote statistics suggesting that most people who accept a counteroffer leave within a year anyway. That claim is widely repeated and poorly evidenced, and the people quoting it have an obvious interest in your leaving. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a fact.

The mechanism behind it is more credible than the number. If the underlying reason for looking was not pay, and it is not addressed, the dissatisfaction returns — usually within months. And your employer now knows you were prepared to leave, which changes how they plan around you.

Equally, the opposite claim — that accepting is always fine because you got what you asked for — ignores that a salary correction offered only under threat of resignation says something about how pay decisions are normally made.

## Practical Dutch Considerations

**Notice periods.** The statutory notice period for an employee is one month unless a longer period is agreed in the contract or a collective agreement. This is the window in which counteroffers usually appear.

**Withdrawing a resignation.** Resignation is a unilateral act. Once it has been given clearly and unambiguously, you cannot simply take it back — continuing employment requires the employer's agreement. In practice an employer making a counteroffer will agree, but get the continuation confirmed in writing rather than assuming your old contract silently continues.

**Nothing is lost by waiting a day.** You are not obliged to answer a counteroffer immediately, and a day's reflection is normal and professional.

**Unemployment benefits.** If you resign without new employment, entitlement to benefits through the **UWV** can be affected. This is rarely relevant when moving directly to a new job, but it matters if you were considering leaving without a signed contract elsewhere.

**Transition payment.** A transition payment applies in principle when the employer ends the employment, not when you resign.

**Pay transparency.** As the EU Pay Transparency Directive is implemented, employers must provide pay information before interviews and must base pay on objective, gender-neutral criteria. If your counteroffer is a sudden large correction, it is fair to ask how the original figure was set.

## If You Are Considering Accepting

Make the counteroffer concrete before you say yes.

**Get it in writing,** including the new salary, effective date, any title change and any commitments about scope or projects.

**Attach dates to promises.** "You will lead the platform project" becomes meaningful only with a start date and a defined scope.

**Ask what changes structurally.** If the complaint was that models never reach production, what will be different — budget, a new engineer, a changed priority?

**Ask why now.** A calm, honest answer ("we knew you were underpaid and were slow to fix it") is a better sign than an improvised one.

**Check the ripple effects.** If your raise was unbudgeted, does it come out of team budget, and what does that do to your colleagues?

## If You Are Declining

Decline gracefully, because the Dutch AI market is small and interconnected.

Thank them genuinely, state that the decision is about direction rather than money, and keep the reason short and non-accusatory. Offer a proper handover: documentation, a walkthrough of the systems you own, a list of the things only you know. That handover is what people remember about you two years later, and it is also the reason former colleagues recommend you.

Expect a little awkwardness for a week or two. It passes, and the relationship usually survives.

## If You Have Already Signed Elsewhere

Sometimes the counteroffer arrives after you have signed a contract with the new employer. This is a different and much more delicate situation than a counteroffer before signing.

A signed employment contract is a binding agreement, even if the start date is weeks away. Withdrawing from it is not simply a change of mind: the new employer has stopped its recruitment process, possibly rejected other candidates and may have made commitments internally. Depending on what was agreed, there can be legal consequences, so if you are seriously considering it, take advice rather than acting on a phone call.

The reputational side is at least as important. The Dutch AI market is small, hiring managers move between employers and recruiters remember. Cancelling a signed contract to stay for more money is remembered for years, and the story travels further than most people expect.

The practical conclusion is to front-load the decision. Before you sign anywhere, ask yourself what you would do if your current employer offered more money the next morning. If the honest answer is "I would think about it", that conversation belongs with your current manager before you sign, not after.

## The Third Option Nobody Mentions

There is a middle route that candidates often overlook: use the new offer to negotiate with the new employer instead. If the counteroffer is financially better but the new role is better for your development, it is entirely legitimate to go back to the new employer once, politely, with a specific and reasonable request.

The worst outcome is that they decline and you accept anyway. The likely outcome is a smaller adjustment than the counteroffer, in a role that actually addresses why you were looking. Under pay transparency rules the new employer must in any case be able to justify pay against objective criteria, so ask where your offer sits in the range and why.

## Before the Counteroffer Arrives

Two habits make this decision much easier when it comes.

**Decide your conditions before you resign.** Write down what would have to change for you to stay, and share it with your manager before you start interviewing rather than after. If the answer is nothing, you have your answer without a counteroffer.

**Know the market.** A counteroffer is only evaluable against real alternatives. Candidates who have looked at what exists nearby — which employers within their radius are hiring, at what level, for what kind of work — judge a counteroffer far better than those comparing it to an imagined market.

## What Changes at Work After You Accept

Candidates rarely think past the decision itself, but accepting a counteroffer changes your position inside the organisation in ways worth anticipating.

Your manager now knows you were prepared to leave. In healthy organisations this leads to more attention: a serious development conversation, a project you had been asking for, a genuine attempt to keep you engaged. In less healthy ones it leads to quiet exclusion from long-term plans, on the assumption that you will go eventually anyway.

Colleagues may notice. If your raise is visible, or if the team is small enough that pay is inferred, resentment is possible — particularly if others have asked for increases through normal channels and been refused. This is not a reason to decline, but it is a reason to keep the details private.

Your own perception changes too. Many people describe a period of doubt after staying, in which every frustration is re-examined against the job they did not take. That passes faster when the counteroffer included concrete structural changes, and lingers when it was only money.

The practical protection is a review date. Agree a conversation three or six months out to check whether the promised changes happened. If they did, you made a good decision. If they did not, you have information rather than a vague feeling — and the market will still be there.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Utrecht who wrote it down first

An ML engineer in Utrecht has been frustrated for a year: two models built, neither deployed, no engineering support, repeated promises about a platform investment. He starts looking, finds a role at an energy-sector employer twenty-five minutes away and resigns.

Within a day his employer offers a substantial raise and the title of lead data scientist. It is more money than the new role. He asks for a day.

He rereads what he wrote before interviewing: *I am leaving because nothing I build reaches production and nobody is accountable for changing that.* The counteroffer contains a raise and a title, and nothing about deployment, engineering support or accountability. When he asks what will change structurally, the answer is that they will "look at it in the new year".

He declines, does a thorough handover and leaves on good terms. Eight months later a former colleague joins him at the new employer. The counteroffer had been genuine and generous; it simply did not address the problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Counteroffers usually reflect the employer's replacement cost and continuity risk, not a change in how they will treat you.
- Write down why you started looking before the counteroffer appears, and judge the offer against that reason alone.
- Money reliably fixes underpayment and rarely fixes management, direction, stagnation or a lack of investment.
- In the Netherlands the statutory notice period is one month; a resignation cannot simply be withdrawn without the employer's agreement, so get any continuation in writing.
- Consider the third option — negotiating once with the new employer — and decline gracefully with a thorough handover if you leave.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employee who just resigned) Should I accept a counteroffer from my current employer?
Only if it addresses the reason you started looking. A raise reliably fixes underpayment but rarely fixes management, stagnation or a lack of investment in data work.

### (Scenario: employee unsure about timing) Can I take time to consider a counteroffer?
Yes — a day or two for reflection is normal and professional, and no reasonable employer will withdraw an offer because you thought about it.

### (Scenario: employee who wants to stay after resigning) Can I withdraw my resignation?
Not unilaterally. Once given clearly, a resignation requires the employer's agreement to continue employment, so confirm any continuation in writing.

### (Scenario: employee weighing two offers) Is it acceptable to go back to the new employer for a better offer?
Yes, once, politely and with a specific request. Under pay transparency rules employers must justify pay against objective criteria, so ask where the offer sits in the range.

### (Scenario: employee planning to resign) How can I avoid the counteroffer dilemma entirely?
Tell your manager what would have to change before you start interviewing. If nothing changes, you have your answer without the pressure of a counteroffer.
