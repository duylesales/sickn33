There is one data person, and the organisation has decided it needs two. On paper this is the easiest kind of small-team role: someone has already proven the value, the tooling exists, the stakeholders are familiar with what a data function does, and you will not be alone. In practice, the second hire is a specific and slightly delicate situation, because everything in the function was built by one person, according to their preferences, and your arrival changes their job as much as it defines yours. Handled well, it is one of the best environments to work in. Handled badly, it produces two people working in parallel who quietly disagree about how everything should be done.

## Why the Second Hire Is Different

The first hire built something from nothing and made hundreds of decisions alone: tooling, conventions, how models are validated, which requests get accepted, what "done" means. None of it was written down, because there was nobody to write it for.

You arrive into that. Every convention is somebody's personal choice rather than a team standard, and every improvement you suggest is, implicitly, a comment on their work. That dynamic is manageable and it is real, and it is the main thing that distinguishes this role from joining an established team.

## The Three Configurations

**You are their peer.** Similar level, complementary skills. The most common and usually the best: two people can review each other's work, which is the single biggest gain over the previous situation.

**You are senior to them.** You are hired to lead, or simply have more experience. Workable, provided the organisation has been clear with them about what the arrangement is. If it has not, you will discover that on day one, and so will they.

**You are junior to them.** You would be learning from them. Straightforward, and worth checking that they actually want to mentor rather than having asked for a peer and been given an apprentice.

Ask directly which of these the organisation intends, and ask whether the first hire was involved in the decision to recruit you and in the interview process. If they were, most of the risk is already handled. If they were not, ask why.

## What the Organisation Expects

Two expectations exist, and they are rarely stated together.

**Double the output.** Management sees a backlog and adds a person. The assumption is linear, and the first months are not: you need onboarding from the one person whose time was already scarce, so throughput goes down before it goes up.

**Reduce key person risk.** Someone finally noticed that if the data person is ill or resigns, nothing works. This is a very good reason to hire a second person, and it makes knowledge transfer an explicit part of your job rather than an inconvenience.

Ask which of these drove the decision. The second produces a much more sensible first six months than the first.

## What Actually Happens in Year One

**Ownership gets divided,** usually badly at first. Two people touching the same pipelines without an agreement produces conflicting changes within weeks.

**Conventions collide.** Their way of structuring projects meets your way, and unless you settle it explicitly, the codebase ends up with two dialects.

**The bus factor gets fixed, slowly.** Documenting what one person knows takes months, and it is the most valuable thing you do in year one even though nobody will celebrate it.

**Stakeholders keep going to them.** Relationships do not transfer because an organisation chart changed. Expect to earn your own.

**The function becomes a team,** which means meetings, planning and shared standards where previously there was one person's judgement. Some first hires welcome this and some experience it as bureaucracy arriving.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **Was the current data person involved in creating this role and in interviewing?**
- **What is the intended relationship — peer, senior, junior?** And do both of you understand it the same way?
- **Why now?** Backlog, key person risk, a new mandate or a departure you have not been told about.
- **Is the first hire happy, and how long have they been there?** Ask them, not only the manager.
- **How is work currently divided, and how would that change?**
- **What is documented?** The honest answer is usually "not much", which is fine if acknowledged.
- **Who decides technical standards once there are two of us?**
- **Is a third hire planned?** A function growing towards a team is a better prospect than one that stops at two.

## Meet Them Properly Before You Decide

If the process has not included a real conversation with the person you would work alongside — not a panel, a conversation — ask for one. It is the most informative half hour available to you.

Listen for whether they describe the role as something they asked for, whether they talk about what they want to hand over, and whether they seem relieved or guarded. A first hire who has been asking for a colleague for a year will tell you so immediately, and that is the strongest possible signal.

## Warning Signs

- The first hire was not involved and seems surprised by the scope of your role.
- They are visibly overloaded and have no capacity to onboard you.
- They are leaving, and nobody has said so.
- Management describes them critically, which suggests you are being hired as a replacement rather than as a colleague.
- Nothing is documented and there is no intention to change that.
- The expectation is that you will "modernise" their work, which is a conflict waiting to happen.

## The Advantage You Have

The second hire arrives into conditions the first one did not have, and it is worth appreciating them.

The value of the function is proven, so you do not spend a year justifying your existence. Tooling and access exist. Stakeholders understand roughly what to ask for. There is someone who knows why the data looks the way it does. And there is finally a second pair of eyes, which means your work can be reviewed for the first time in that organisation's history.

That combination — a proven function with room to professionalise it — is a genuinely strong position, and it is why many people find this the most satisfying role of their career so far.

## When to Accept

- The first hire wanted a colleague and helped choose you.
- The relationship between you is clearly defined and mutually understood.
- Key person risk is an acknowledged reason for the hire.
- There is a sponsor and a plan for the function to grow.
- You are comfortable adapting to existing conventions before improving them.
- You would have work of your own rather than only supporting theirs.

## When to Decline

- The first hire is disengaged, overloaded or on their way out.
- You are being brought in to fix someone whose manager will not address it directly.
- Nothing is documented and nobody wants it to be.
- The expectation is that output doubles immediately.
- Both of you would be alone technically in an organisation with no engineers.
- The role exists to support one person rather than to build a function.

## How to Start Well

Adopt their conventions first, then propose changes. Nothing damages this relationship faster than reorganising someone's repository in week two.

Divide ownership explicitly and early: which systems are yours, which are theirs, what requires agreement. Write it down, however informal it feels.

Take the unloved work. Every one-person function has something the incumbent dislikes — a reporting pipeline, a governance task, a difficult stakeholder. Taking it deliberately buys enormous goodwill.

Start reviewing each other's work immediately, in both directions. Framing it as mutual removes any suggestion that you are checking them.

Document as you learn. You have a temporary advantage — you can see what is undocumented, because you are the one who cannot find it — and that advantage disappears within a few months.

And settle standards together rather than by decree, even if you are the senior one. A function of two that agrees on conventions becomes a team; one that does not becomes two people sharing a backlog.

## If They Leave Shortly After You Arrive

It happens often enough to plan for: the first hire was tired, the second hire arrives, and within six months they take a job elsewhere. Sometimes your arrival is precisely what made leaving possible for them.

If it happens, the situation changes from "second hire" to "only data person", which is a materially different job. Three things help.

**Accelerate the handover immediately.** Ask for structured sessions rather than informal osmosis, and write down what you learn as you go. Their notice period is the most valuable resource you will have that year.

**Renegotiate scope, explicitly.** You cannot do two people's work, and the moment to say so is now rather than after three months of trying. Agree with your manager what gets paused or dropped.

**Ask for the replacement hire to be started at once,** and ask to be involved in defining and interviewing for it. Recruitment takes months, and starting in month three rather than month six is the difference between a difficult year and a very bad one.

It is also worth asking yourself honestly whether the role you accepted still exists. If you joined specifically to have a colleague and to be reviewed, and that reason has gone, that is legitimate information — and a conversation to have with your manager while you still have goodwill, rather than a reason to quietly start looking.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Almelo who took the reporting job nobody wanted

A data scientist joins a manufacturer as the second data hire, alongside an analyst who has been alone for three years. The interview process includes an hour with him, at his request rather than the manager's.

He tells her plainly that he asked for a colleague twice and was refused, that the last refusal was reversed after he was ill for two weeks and nothing ran, and that what he most wants to hand over is a monthly reporting cycle he finds tedious.

She takes the reporting cycle in her first month, automates a third of it by month three, and asks him to review everything she writes. He starts sending her his own work in return, which he says nobody has ever looked at.

They agree ownership in a one-page document: he keeps the production planning models, she takes reporting and the new quality work, and the shared pipelines require both to agree.

Eighteen months later the team is four people and he leads it. Her summary is that the entire relationship was set by two decisions: asking to meet him before accepting, and taking the work he disliked most.

## Key Takeaways

- The second hire arrives into a function built by one person, where every convention is a personal choice and every improvement implies a judgement on their work.
- Establish the intended relationship — peer, senior or junior — and confirm that both of you understand it the same way.
- Ask why now: reducing key person risk produces a far more sensible first six months than an expectation of doubled output.
- Insist on a real conversation with the current data person before deciding; whether they asked for a colleague is the strongest signal available.
- Start by adopting their conventions, dividing ownership in writing, taking the unloved work and reviewing each other's work in both directions.
