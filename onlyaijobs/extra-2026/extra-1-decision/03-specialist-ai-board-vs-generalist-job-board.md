Job searching is a time-allocation problem. You have a limited number of evenings, and every hour spent scrolling through results you would never accept is an hour not spent preparing a strong application for a role you would. The choice between a generalist job board and a specialist one is, at its core, a decision about where that time produces the most interviews. It is not a question of loyalty to a platform, and it is not permanent — the right answer changes depending on what stage of the search you are in and what kind of role you want.

## The Structural Difference

A generalist board serves every profession. Its value proposition is coverage: whatever you do, there is something for you. To make that manageable it relies on search, filters and ranking algorithms, and typically on employers paying to promote listings so they stand out in a very crowded catalogue.

A specialist board serves one field. Its value proposition is relevance: everything here is the kind of work you do. It needs fewer ranking tricks because the catalogue is already filtered by definition. OnlyAIJobs takes this further by showing each vacancy at exact address level with the distance from your home, and by not selling higher placement in the listing order.

The two models fail differently. A generalist board fails by burying relevant roles in irrelevant ones. A specialist board fails by simply not having a role that a broader platform would have shown you.

## What "Relevance" Actually Means in AI Hiring

The relevance problem in this field is worse than in most, for three reasons.

**"AI" is a marketing keyword.** Employers add it to postings to widen reach. A generalist search for AI roles returns sales positions at AI companies, marketing roles that mention AI tools and general software engineering roles where machine learning appears in one bullet.

**The real work often avoids the word.** In manufacturing, pharmaceutical, energy and public organisations, genuinely technical roles are titled process engineer, methodologist, planning analyst, actuarial analyst or systems engineer. A keyword search for "machine learning" never sees them.

**Categories beat titles.** Because titles are unreliable, filtering by category — machine learning, development, research, infrastructure, computer vision, and so on — is the only filter that maps to the type of work rather than the vocabulary the employer happened to use.

## A Decision Framework

| Your situation | Best use of your time |
|---|---|
| You know you want AI, ML or data work | Specialist board first; it is the whole catalogue you care about |
| You are open to adjacent roles (product, analytics-light, teaching) | Generalist board, with broad filters |
| You want to work within cycling or short driving distance | Specialist board with distance-based search |
| You are searching in a small town or unusual sector | Both — generalist aggregation catches employers who submit nowhere else |
| You are exploring what exists before deciding | Specialist board to see the shape of the market, then generalist for breadth |
| You want employers to approach you | Neither board — that is what professional networks do |
| You want alerts across a huge catalogue | Generalist board |

## The Hidden Cost of a Bad Search Session

A search session that returns three hundred results, of which perhaps eight are relevant, has a cost beyond the wasted minutes. It creates the impression that the market is either enormous and impersonal or hostile and competitive. Candidates who experience this repeatedly tend to respond in one of two unhelpful ways: they apply to everything quickly, which lowers the quality of each application, or they conclude the market is closed and reduce their search effort.

A smaller, precise result set produces a different response. When five roles appear and three are genuinely interesting, the natural next step is to research those three employers and write three good applications. That is the behaviour that produces interviews.

## The Regional Argument

OnlyAIJobs exists because of a specific observation about the Dutch labour market: many job seekers leave their home region for the Randstad not because they want to, but because they do not know which interesting companies are located near them. That is an information problem, and it is exactly what generalist boards handle badly.

On a generalist platform, a well-known employer in Amsterdam outranks a specialised manufacturer in Noord-Brabant, a grid operator in Gelderland or a research institute in Groningen — not because the roles are better, but because visibility follows recruitment budgets and familiar names. Candidates conclude there is "nothing here" and search the Randstad instead.

Employers listed on OnlyAIJobs include large consultancies such as Accenture and Cegeka Nederland alongside regional companies such as Heijmans, which is precisely the mix that generalist ranking tends to flatten. A platform where placement is not for sale, with distance from your home as the sorting principle, changes which employers you see first.

## What You Give Up With a Specialist Board

Honesty about trade-offs makes the decision easier.

**Fewer listings.** One field in one country means a smaller catalogue than a global platform. If your criteria are very narrow — a specific niche, a specific city, senior level only — there may be nothing today that fits.

**No network effects.** No profile, no connections, no passive discovery. A specialist board does not work for you while you sleep.

**Coverage gaps.** Employers who never submit vacancies to specialist channels are invisible there, however good their roles are.

The practical response is not to choose one and abandon the other, but to use each for the thing it does well.

## How Much Time Should Each Get?

A reasonable split for someone actively searching for AI, ML or data work in the Netherlands:

- **Roughly two thirds of active search time on the specialist board,** browsing by category and distance, saving interesting roles.
- **Roughly one third on generalist channels,** running alerts, checking adjacent roles and researching employers.
- **The majority of your total effort on applications, not searching.** Three tailored applications will almost always beat fifteen quick ones.

Reassess after three or four weeks using a simple measure: which channel produced interviews, not which produced applications.

## Why Niche Boards Exist at All

It is worth understanding the economics, because it explains what you can expect from each type of platform.

A generalist board needs enormous traffic across every profession, which it funds through employers paying for visibility and volume. Its incentive is scale: more listings, more applications, more sponsored placements.

A niche board serves a much smaller audience that has already self-selected. Employers come to it not for reach but for relevance — an audience where almost every visitor is a plausible candidate. That is why niche boards can afford to keep the candidate side simple and free, and why OnlyAIJobs offers employers a first listing at no cost: the platform's value depends on having enough real vacancies for the audience it serves, and on candidates trusting that what they see is not ordered by budget.

For you, this has a practical consequence. On a niche board, the absence of a role today is information — it means no employer in that field submitted one, not that it was buried on page nine. On a generalist board, the absence of a role usually means your filter or the ranking hid it.

## A Weekly Search Routine That Uses Both

A routine beats intensity. One that works for most people in a specialised field looks like this:

**Once a week, browse the specialist board properly.** Filter by category rather than title, sort by distance, and look at everything inside your radius — including vacancies whose titles do not sound like your job. Save anything worth a second look.

**Once a week, check the broader channels.** Review alerts from the generalist board, scan adjacent categories and note employers worth researching.

**Twice a week, work on applications rather than searching.** Two tailored applications per week is a sustainable pace that produces better results than fifteen rushed ones.

**Once a month, review the evidence.** Which channel produced interviews, not applications? Adjust the split accordingly, and widen either your radius or your definition of relevant roles if nothing is moving.

## Real example

### A career switcher in Eindhoven who changed her measure of progress

A business analyst in Eindhoven moving towards data work spends her first month on a generalist board. She applies to twenty-two roles, nearly all in Amsterdam and Utrecht, and reaches two first conversations. Her conclusion is that she needs to move to the Randstad.

Before deciding, she browses a specialist board by category, sorted by distance from her home. The result set is small — around a dozen roles within thirty minutes — but three of them are at high-tech manufacturers and one at a healthcare technology company, all within a fifteen-minute cycle or a short drive. Two of the postings never use the word AI; they describe quality data and process optimisation work that matches her background better than the Amsterdam roles did.

She applies to four, each directly on the employer's site, referencing the specific process described in the posting. She receives three interview invitations. The market had not changed. Her measure of progress had: from applications sent to relevant applications sent.

## What to Do When the Specialist Board Has Nothing Today

At some point you will open a specialist board, filter by your category and radius, and find nothing new. On a generalist platform this rarely happens, because something always matches loosely. The absence is uncomfortable, but it is also useful information, and there are productive responses.

**Widen the radius before widening the field.** Another fifteen minutes of travel often doubles the number of employers within reach, especially outside the largest cities.

**Widen the category, not the standard.** If you filtered only on machine learning, add data engineering, research or infrastructure. The same work appears under different categories depending on how the employer sees the role.

**Look at older listings you skipped.** A vacancy published five weeks ago may still be open, particularly for specialised roles that take longer to fill.

**Use the quiet week for preparation.** Research employers within your radius for later, improve one project, or write the tailored CV variants you will need when something does appear.

**Check again weekly rather than daily.** In a specialised field, the catalogue changes slowly. Daily checking produces frustration without information.

An empty result set on a specialist board means no employer in that field submitted a matching role this week. That is a fact about the market, not about your prospects — and it is more honest than a generalist page full of results you would never accept.

## Key Takeaways

- Generalist boards optimise for coverage and rely on ranking and paid promotion; specialist boards optimise for relevance in one field.
- In AI hiring, "AI" is used loosely in some postings and avoided entirely in others, so category filters beat title searches.
- Large, irrelevant result sets encourage rushed applications or discouragement; small, relevant sets encourage tailored applications.
- Generalist ranking tends to favour well-known and better-funded employers, which hides regional and smaller AI employers.
- Use specialist boards for focused searching and distance, generalist channels for breadth, alerts and research.
