Two job seekers start the same week. One sends twenty-five applications, mostly through quick-apply flows, adjusting nothing. The other sends six, each with a CV adapted to the specific problem in the posting and a short letter that names why this employer. A month later, the first has three responses and no interviews; the second has four responses and two interviews. Both worked hard. Only one worked in a way that matches how AI hiring actually functions. The question "how many applications per week" has no universal answer, but it has a method — and the method starts by noticing that applications are not the thing you are trying to maximise.

## Why More Applications Stops Working Quickly

For specialised roles, the bottleneck is rarely the number of vacancies you reach. It is whether each application demonstrates that you can do the specific work described.

AI and data vacancies attract many applicants, a large share of whom apply untailored. Reviewers respond by scanning for evidence of fit in seconds and rejecting anything generic. In that environment, the marginal value of your twentieth untailored application is close to zero, while the marginal value of your third tailored one is high.

There is also a hidden cost. Applications create obligations: follow-ups, interview preparation, assessments. Twenty-five open applications you cannot service properly turn into missed responses and half-prepared interviews.

## What You Are Actually Trying to Maximise

Not applications. Not even responses. The measure that matters is **relevant interviews per week of effort** — conversations with employers whose roles you would genuinely accept.

That reframing changes decisions immediately. Applying to a role you would decline adds an application and zero expected interviews. Spending forty minutes tailoring an application for a role you want raises the chance of an interview substantially. A search session that finds two suitable local roles is more productive than one that finds forty distant ones.

## A Realistic Weekly Rhythm

For most people searching for AI, ML or data work in the Netherlands, a sustainable rhythm looks roughly like this:

| Activity | Time per week | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Searching | 1–2 hours | One proper session, browsing by category and distance, saving candidates |
| Researching employers | 1 hour | Website, people, recent news, location |
| Tailoring and applying | 2–4 hours | Two to four applications, depending on how close the fit is |
| Interview preparation | Variable | Rises sharply once processes start |
| Follow-ups and admin | 30 minutes | Chasing, tracking, replying |

Two to four tailored applications per week is a pace most people can sustain for months. It produces roughly ten to fifteen applications per month, which for specialised roles is a substantial pipeline.

## When Volume Is the Right Strategy

Volume is not always wrong. Increase the number when:

**You are early in your career.** Junior roles receive many applicants, individual outcomes are noisier, and the work of tailoring is smaller because your relevant experience is more compact.

**Your target roles are broad.** If data analyst, BI developer, analytics engineer and junior data scientist are all acceptable, the pool is larger and less differentiation is needed per application.

**You are relocating or open to a wide region.** More geography means more vacancies and less ability to research each employer deeply.

**The market is quiet in your niche.** If only three suitable roles appeared this month, applying to adjacent ones widens your options.

**You have a hard deadline.** A permit expiring or a contract ending changes the calculation towards breadth.

## When Selectivity Is the Right Strategy

Reduce the number and increase the depth when:

**You are experienced or senior.** Fewer roles fit, and those that do reward evidence of specific accomplishments.

**Your specialism is narrow.** Computer vision for medical devices, optimisation for energy markets or Dutch-language NLP have small candidate pools and high expectations.

**You are employed and selective.** With no urgency, the only applications worth making are for roles clearly better than your current one.

**You are targeting known employers.** A handful of specific companies deserve proper research and genuinely tailored applications.

**Your responses are poor.** If twenty applications produced nothing, sending forty more of the same is unlikely to change the pattern.

## Make Tailoring Cheap Instead of Skipping It

The argument for volume usually rests on tailoring being expensive. It does not have to be.

**Keep a master CV** containing every project, result and technology, then delete rather than write for each application.

**Build three or four CV variants** for the role types you target — engineering-leaning, analysis-leaning, research-leaning — and adjust only the top third per application.

**Write modular letter paragraphs:** one about your strongest relevant experience, one about the domain, one about why this employer. Reassemble rather than rewrite.

**Time-box it.** Thirty to forty minutes per application, including research, is enough once the components exist.

Tailoring that takes two hours per application is unsustainable, and tailoring that takes two minutes is invisible to the reader. Forty minutes is the practical middle.

## Track the Funnel, Not the Total

A simple record turns a frustrating search into a diagnosable process. For each application, note the company, date, role, channel and outcome. Then watch the ratios:

- **Applications to responses.** Very low response rates point to targeting, CV clarity or applying to poorly matched roles.
- **Responses to first interviews.** Losses here suggest the CV overstates fit or the motivation is unclear.
- **First interviews to later rounds.** Losses here point to how you explain your work.
- **Later rounds to offers.** Losses here are often about specific technical depth or competition, and sometimes about nothing you control.

Ten applications is enough to see the first ratio. Twenty-five is usually enough to see the second. That is the real argument for a steady pace: it produces interpretable data.

Saved jobs on a board plus a simple spreadsheet is enough infrastructure. It also prevents the two most common accidents — applying twice to the same employer through different routes, and losing track of a deadline.

## Where the Time Goes Wrong

Three patterns waste the majority of most people's search time.

**Endless searching.** Browsing many platforms daily feels productive and produces almost nothing after the first session of the week.

**Applying to roles you would decline.** Every one of these costs the time of a real application and yields nothing.

**Neglecting preparation.** Applications create interviews; interviews create offers. Candidates who spend all their time applying and none preparing lose at the stage that matters most.

## A Note on Automation

Tools that auto-apply to many vacancies exist. For specialised roles they are usually counterproductive: they send generic applications into processes where tailoring is the differentiator, and they can damage your standing with employers you might genuinely want.

There is also a regulatory dimension on the other side of the table: under the EU AI Act, AI systems used to filter applications or evaluate candidates are high-risk, with requirements including human oversight, and under the GDPR you have rights concerning decisions based solely on automated processing. Many Dutch employers also follow the NVP Recruitment Code, which sets expectations on informing candidates and handling applications within reasonable timeframes. In other words, the answer to automated screening is not automated applying; it is applications a human reviewer recognises as genuinely relevant.

## Protecting Your Energy

A search that runs for months is an endurance problem. Set process goals rather than outcome goals — "three tailored applications and one search session this week" rather than "get an interview" — because the first is under your control and the second is not.

Keep one day a week free of job searching entirely. Batch applications into two evenings rather than spreading them across seven. And review the funnel monthly rather than agonising over individual rejections, which are noisy and mostly uninformative on their own.

## Adjust the Rate Once Processes Start

The right number is not constant through a search. It should fall as processes progress, and most people forget to let it.

In the first weeks, applications are your only activity, and two to four per week fills the time comfortably. Once three or four processes are live, the picture changes: each one brings preparation, a technical assessment, scheduling and follow-up. A single take-home assignment can consume an entire weekend. Continuing to send new applications at the same rate produces half-prepared interviews for roles you already have — which is the expensive mistake, because an interview reached is worth far more than an application sent.

A reasonable rule: reduce new applications by one for every active process beyond the second. With four live processes, pause new applications entirely for a week and protect the preparation time.

The exception is the final stage. When you are in last rounds at one or two employers, it is worth keeping a small stream of new applications going, because offers fall through, roles get frozen and negotiating from a position with alternatives is very different from negotiating with none. Stopping entirely the moment a process looks promising is how candidates end up restarting from zero three weeks later.

## Real example

### A data engineer in Arnhem who cut his applications in half

A data engineer in Arnhem applies to roughly twelve roles a week for a month: forty-seven applications, six responses, one interview, no offer. He concludes the market is bad.

Reviewing his log, he notices two things. Nearly two thirds of the applications were for roles in Amsterdam and Utrecht with long commutes he would probably have declined. And every application used the same CV, written around a previous role that no longer represents his strongest work.

He changes the approach: a maximum of three applications per week, only within forty-five minutes of home, each with a CV whose top third is rewritten for the role, and thirty minutes of research per employer. In the following month he sends eleven applications. Seven produce responses, four lead to first interviews, two reach final rounds and one becomes an offer at a manufacturer twenty minutes away.

The market had not changed. The ratio between effort and relevance had.

## Key Takeaways

- Maximise relevant interviews per week of effort, not applications sent.
- Two to four tailored applications per week is a sustainable pace for most specialised searches.
- Increase volume when you are junior, broad, relocating or under deadline; increase selectivity when senior, niche or already employed.
- Make tailoring cheap with a master CV, role-specific variants and modular letter paragraphs, time-boxed to about forty minutes.
- Track the funnel — applications, responses, interviews, offers — and diagnose the stage that is failing instead of increasing volume blindly.

## Where to Start

Decide your weekly number before you next open a job board, and make it small enough that every application can be tailored — then spend the time you save on research and interview preparation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate unsure about pace) How many applications per week should I send?
For specialised AI, ML and data roles, two to four genuinely tailored applications per week is a sustainable and effective pace for most candidates.

### (Scenario: junior candidate) Should I apply to more roles if I am just starting out?
Often yes — junior roles are noisier and tailoring is faster when your relevant experience is compact, so a somewhat higher volume is reasonable.

### (Scenario: candidate with no responses) I applied to thirty roles and heard almost nothing. What should I change?
Look at the first ratio in your funnel: very low response rates usually indicate poor targeting or a CV that does not show fit in the top third, not insufficient volume.

### (Scenario: candidate considering auto-apply tools) Are automated application tools worth using?
For specialised roles, generally not — they send generic applications into processes where tailoring is the differentiator, and they can harm your standing with employers.

### (Scenario: candidate searching for months) How do I keep going during a long search?
Set process goals you control, batch applications into fixed evenings, keep one day free of searching and review your funnel monthly rather than reacting to individual rejections.
