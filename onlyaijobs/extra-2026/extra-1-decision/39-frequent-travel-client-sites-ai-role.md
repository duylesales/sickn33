Some of the most interesting applied AI work in the Netherlands happens where the problem physically is: a production line in Brabant, a greenhouse in Westland, a substation in Gelderland, a customer's warehouse in Limburg, a test facility that cannot be reached by video call. Roles at machine builders, engineering firms, consultancies and multi-site employers therefore come with travel attached — sometimes a few days a month, sometimes most of the week. Job descriptions describe this in a single line, usually as a percentage that nobody can define precisely, and candidates rarely think it through until the third week of driving. It deserves the same scrutiny as salary, because it is the part of the job you cannot renegotiate afterwards without changing roles.

## The Kinds of Travel, Which Are Not Alike

**Client visits from a home base.** Occasional days at customers, returning home each evening. Common in consultancy and software vendors.

**Secondment to one client.** You work at a single client site for months, effectively commuting there rather than to your employer. Predictable, and effectively a different commute than the one in your contract.

**Site and field work.** Visits to factories, greenhouses, depots, vessels or infrastructure, often to collect data, install sensors or validate a model against reality. Irregular and hard to plan.

**Commissioning trips.** Longer stays when a system goes live, sometimes abroad, sometimes at short notice.

**Multi-site internal travel.** Employers with several locations expect presence at more than one, which quietly doubles your commute on some days.

The percentage in the vacancy tells you almost nothing about which of these you are being offered.

## The Questions That Make It Concrete

- **How many nights away per month, on average and at peak?** Days out are inconvenient; nights away change your life.
- **How far in advance is travel planned?** A schedule known a month ahead is a different proposition from being asked on Tuesday to be somewhere on Thursday.
- **Where, geographically?** Two hours by train within the Netherlands is not the same as short-notice flights.
- **Does travel time count as working time?** Practice varies, and collective agreements sometimes address it. Ask explicitly.
- **What is reimbursed, and how?** Company car, mobility budget, full public transport card, kilometre allowance, hotel and meal policy.
- **Who decides when a visit is necessary?** You, the client, or a planner with a utilisation target?
- **What happens in weeks with no travel?** Office attendance or home working.

An employer used to travelling roles answers all of this without hesitation. Vague answers usually mean travel expands to fill whatever the client asks.

## What the Work Gains From Being There

The honest case for travel is not incidental. For applied AI, physical presence produces information that no dataset contains.

You see how the process actually runs rather than how the documentation says it runs. You notice the manual workaround an operator invented three years ago that explains the anomaly in your data. You meet the people who will use your model and learn whether they trust it. You discover that the sensor is mounted differently on two machines, which explains a discrepancy you would otherwise have modelled as noise.

Data professionals who visit sites consistently produce better models than those who do not, in manufacturing, agriculture, logistics and energy alike. That is the argument for accepting travel, and it is a strong one.

## What It Costs

**Time that is not leisure or work.** Two hours in a car is neither.

**Fragmented deep work.** Modelling and engineering need uninterrupted blocks, and a travel-heavy week rarely contains one.

**Unpredictability.** Short-notice trips complicate childcare, caring responsibilities, sport, study and anything with a fixed schedule.

**Physical toll.** Frequent early starts and late returns accumulate, and the effect is easy to underestimate in the first enthusiastic months.

**Uneven distribution.** Travel often concentrates on the person who does not object, which is a pattern worth noticing early.

## Practical Dutch Points

**Reimbursement.** Many Dutch employers provide a travel allowance, a public transport card, a mobility budget or a company car for roles with structural travel. What is customary depends on the sector and any collective agreement, and the differences between employers are substantial — this is a legitimate negotiation point.

**Working time.** Working hours and rest are regulated under the Working Hours Act, and whether travel time counts as working time is often addressed in collective agreements or company policy rather than being obvious. Ask how it is handled here, particularly for long days.

**Secondment.** If you would be placed at a client for a longer period, the arrangement may fall under intermediary rules, and your effective commute is to the client rather than to your employer's office. Check where placements are typically located before accepting.

**Hybrid expectations.** Travel days and office days are often counted separately, which can mean a nominally hybrid role in which you are rarely at home.

## Who It Suits

- People who find offices draining and enjoy variety.
- People who like seeing the physical reality behind the data.
- People whose personal situation is flexible right now.
- People early in a career who want exposure to many organisations quickly.
- People who can work productively on a train.

## Who Should Be Careful

- Anyone with fixed caring responsibilities or a partner who also travels.
- People who need long uninterrupted blocks to do their best work.
- People with a long commute already, since travel is added on top rather than replacing it.
- People whose energy depends on routine, sleep and regular exercise.
- Anyone accepting a travel-heavy role mainly for the salary.

## Negotiating It

Travel is one of the more negotiable elements of an offer, because it is a scheduling question rather than a budget line.

Ask for a cap: a maximum number of nights away per month, or a guarantee of at least a certain number of travel-free weeks per quarter. Ask for notice periods on trips wherever the client relationship allows. Ask about the reimbursement package explicitly and compare it with what similar employers offer. And ask whether the pattern can be revisited annually, since personal circumstances change and a role that suits you now may not in three years.

Get whatever is agreed in writing. Informal understandings about travel are the first thing to erode when a client escalates.

## Travel Abroad Is a Different Calculation

Domestic travel in the Netherlands rarely costs you a night. International travel changes the arithmetic entirely, and vacancies frequently blur the two into one percentage.

Ask where, how often and for how long. A day trip to Antwerp or the Ruhr area is a long day. A commissioning trip to a customer plant outside Europe is a week, with travel days at either end that are neither work nor rest.

Ask about practical arrangements: who books, what standard of travel applies, whether weekend travel is compensated and what happens when a trip overruns because a system is not ready. Commissioning trips overrun routinely.

For some people international travel is a genuine attraction, particularly early in a career. For others it becomes the reason they leave three years later. The honest question is not whether you would enjoy it once, but whether you would still want it in the fourth year, when the novelty has gone and the same airport lounge is waiting.

If the international component is occasional and interesting, it is usually a benefit. If it is structural and unpredictable, treat it as a fundamental feature of the role rather than a detail in the package.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Tilburg who set one number

A data scientist joins a machine builder in a role advertised as "approximately 30% travel". The work is exactly what she wants: computer vision for inspection systems, validated on customer lines across the country and occasionally abroad.

Before signing, she asks the concrete questions. The answers: typically four to six days a month at customer sites, two or three nights away per quarter, occasional commissioning trips of a week, planned two to four weeks ahead, with a company car and travel time counted as working time for long journeys.

She negotiates one addition: a maximum of four nights away per month except during commissioning, agreed in writing, with commissioning trips discussed in advance.

Two years later she describes the site visits as the reason her models work. The cap has been tested twice and held. The part she underestimated was not the nights away but the fragmentation — she now blocks two fixed days a week with no travel, which the team has come to respect.

## Travel and the Rest of Your Life

The decision is rarely only about the job. Frequent travel interacts with everything around it, and the interactions are easy to underestimate during an enthusiastic first month.

**Childcare and school logistics** assume predictability. A partner who also travels, or a fixed pickup time, makes short-notice trips genuinely difficult rather than merely inconvenient.

**Anything with a weekly rhythm suffers first** — sport, evening classes, volunteering, a standing commitment with friends. These are the things people quietly drop, and their absence accumulates.

**Recovery time is real.** A day that starts at half past five and ends at eight in the evening is not a normal working day, even if only eight hours were billable.

**Energy is unevenly distributed.** Travel-heavy weeks leave little for the deep technical work that also has to happen, which is why many people end up doing modelling at the edges of the day.

Before accepting, look at an ordinary month in your actual life and place the travel into it. If the arithmetic only works when nothing unexpected happens, it does not work.

## Making Travel Days Productive

If you take the role, a few habits recover a surprising amount of the lost time. Travel by train where the route allows and treat the journey as a work block rather than dead time. Batch site visits so that one trip covers several purposes — data collection, stakeholder conversations, and the informal observation that produces the best insights. Keep a written note per visit, because details blur after the third site in a week. And protect at least one full day a week with no travel for work that needs continuity.

## Key Takeaways

- Travel comes in distinct forms — client visits, secondment, field work, commissioning, multi-site — and a percentage in a vacancy tells you almost nothing about which.
- Ask about nights away, planning horizon, geography, whether travel counts as working time, reimbursement and who decides a visit is necessary.
- Physical presence genuinely improves applied AI work: processes, workarounds, sensor differences and user trust are not visible in the data.
- The costs are fragmented deep work, unpredictability, physical toll and uneven distribution across a team.
- Negotiate a cap on nights away, notice for trips and a clear reimbursement package, and get it in writing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate reading "30% travel") What does a travel percentage in a vacancy actually mean?
Very little on its own. Ask for days per month, nights away, how far in advance trips are planned and whether travel is domestic or international.

### (Scenario: candidate calculating hours) Does travel time count as working time in the Netherlands?
It depends on the employer, the collective agreement and the nature of the journey. Ask explicitly how long travel days are handled before accepting.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing packages) What is usually reimbursed for travel-heavy roles?
Commonly a travel allowance, public transport card, mobility budget or company car, plus hotel and meal expenses. The differences between employers are significant and negotiable.

### (Scenario: candidate with family commitments) Can I negotiate a limit on travel?
Yes — a cap on nights away per month, guaranteed travel-free weeks and advance notice are all reasonable requests, and should be confirmed in writing.

### (Scenario: candidate weighing the benefit) Is site visiting worth the disruption?
Often yes for applied AI: seeing the real process, the workarounds and how people use your output produces better models than working from data alone.
