Two offers arrive in the same week. One is from a consultancy or secondment firm: varied clients, fast learning, a cohort of people at your level, a structured training budget. The other is from an organisation with its own data team: one domain, one set of systems, models you will still be responsible for in three years. The salaries are close enough that money will not decide it. What will decide it is which kind of professional you want to become, because these two paths build genuinely different capabilities — and the differences are largest in exactly the areas that AI work depends on.

## The Two Working Lives, Side by Side

| | Consultancy or secondment | In-house team |
|---|---|---|
| **Variety** | Several domains and stacks per year | One domain, deeply |
| **Ownership** | Usually ends at handover | You maintain what you build |
| **Feedback loop** | Rarely see long-term model behaviour | Watch models degrade and fix them |
| **Peers** | Strong internal cohort, often remote from the client | Whoever is in the team |
| **Travel** | Often across the country | Fixed location |
| **Learning** | Structured budget, formal programmes | Depends entirely on the team |
| **Politics** | Client's politics, without the standing to change them | Your own organisation's, with standing |
| **Security** | Depends on the pipeline of assignments | Depends on the organisation |

Neither column is better. They suit different people and different moments in a career.

## What Consultancy Actually Gives You

**Exposure at speed.** Three clients in two years teaches you more about how organisations differ than five years inside one of them. You learn to recognise patterns: the data warehouse nobody trusts, the model that was never monitored, the stakeholder who blocks everything until consulted.

**Starting from nothing, repeatedly.** The skill of walking into an unfamiliar environment, finding out where the data actually lives and delivering something within weeks is genuinely valuable and hard to acquire in-house.

**A professional cohort.** Good consultancies invest in their people because their people are the product. Internal knowledge sessions, a training budget, colleagues at your level to compare notes with, and a career ladder that is at least written down.

**A safer landing for career changers.** Consultancies hire on potential more often than in-house teams do, and their training programmes give structure to people moving into AI from adjacent fields.

**Range on your CV.** Several sectors on paper widens the set of employers who will consider you later.

## What In-House Actually Gives You

**Consequences.** You are still there when the model drifts, when the retraining pipeline breaks, when the assumption you made in month two turns out to be wrong in month fourteen. That loop is the single most valuable teacher in applied AI, and consultancy rarely provides it.

**Domain depth.** After two years you know why the data looks the way it does, which sources lie and which stakeholders are right when they disagree with the model. That knowledge is what separates useful systems from technically correct ones.

**Standing.** You can change how things are done, because you are part of the organisation rather than a guest in it. Improving a data platform, establishing governance or changing a process is realistic in a way it seldom is for an external.

**Compound relationships.** The trust you build with a business stakeholder in year one pays off in year three, when they bring you the problem before deciding what to do about it.

**A predictable week.** One location, one set of colleagues, no travel roulette, no assignment ending with a month's notice.

## Utilisation Is the Word That Explains Consultancy

Every consultancy and secondment firm runs on billable hours. That single fact explains most of what candidates later find surprising.

It explains why time for internal development can evaporate when demand is high, why an assignment you find unrewarding may continue anyway, why people get moved between projects, and why "bench" periods — unassigned time — are viewed nervously by both sides even though you remain employed and paid.

It also explains the positive side: the firm has a strong interest in your skills being current and marketable, because that is what it sells.

Ask directly what the target utilisation is, how much non-billable time is protected for development, and what actually happens when someone is between assignments. Firms that answer clearly are usually the ones that manage it well.

## The Dutch Specifics Worth Knowing

Secondment and staffing arrangements in the Netherlands are regulated, including under the **Waadi**, which covers matters such as the right of seconded workers to equal terms in certain respects and the prohibition on charging workers for placement. If you are being placed with clients, ask which arrangement applies to you.

Check the contract for **non-solicitation and client clauses**. It is common for consultancy contracts to restrict joining a client directly for a period after an assignment. That matters, because being offered a permanent role by a client is one of the most common ways consultancy careers end — usually happily.

Check **travel arrangements** as well: which region assignments are drawn from, whether travel time counts as working time, and what is reimbursed. In a country where a client on the other side of the Randstad can mean three hours of daily travel, this is not a detail.

## Questions to Ask a Consultancy

- **What is the target utilisation, and how much development time is protected?**
- **What are the last three assignments people at my level worked on?** Ask for specifics, not sector names.
- **How long is a typical assignment, and how much say do I have in which one?**
- **What happens between assignments?**
- **How far do people travel, and what is reimbursed?**
- **What restrictions apply if a client offers me a permanent role?**
- **What proportion of assignments involve production systems rather than advice and prototypes?**

## Questions to Ask an In-House Employer

- **What is already in production, and who maintains it?**
- **Who else is on the team, and at what level?** Being the only senior person is a different job.
- **Where does the team sit in the organisation, and who decides priorities?**
- **What is the training budget and time allowance?** In-house teams vary enormously here.
- **How do people progress?** Small teams sometimes have no ladder at all.
- **What was the last thing that failed, and what happened afterwards?**

## Which Suits Which Career Stage

**Early career.** Consultancy is often excellent: structured training, a cohort, exposure to many environments, and mentors who have seen more than one way of doing things. The risk is never seeing a system live long enough to learn from it.

**Mid career.** This is where in-house usually wins, because ownership and depth are what turn a competent practitioner into a senior one. Interviewers at this level ask what happened to your models after deployment.

**Senior.** Both work. Consultancy offers variety, influence across organisations and a route into advisory work; in-house offers the ability to build a team and a platform that outlasts you.

**Switching back and forth.** Common and entirely sensible. A few years of each is a strong combination: breadth plus the credibility that comes from having lived with your own decisions.

## The Middle Options

Several arrangements sit between the two poles. **Secondment into a single client for a long period** gives some of the depth of in-house with the employment structure of a firm. **Internal consulting teams** inside large organisations — a central data team serving business units — combine variety with belonging. **Product companies serving one sector** give domain depth with several customers rather than one.

If both offers appeal for different reasons, it is worth asking whether one of these hybrids exists near you before treating the choice as binary.

## How Each Path Reads on a CV Later

It is worth knowing how hiring managers interpret these two backgrounds, because the interpretation shapes which roles you are offered afterwards.

**Consultancy backgrounds read as adaptable.** Several sectors, several stacks, evidence that you can orient yourself quickly. The suspicion attached to it is depth: interviewers probe whether you have ever lived with the consequences of your own design decisions.

**In-house backgrounds read as reliable.** Systems that ran, models that were maintained, a domain understood properly. The suspicion attached to it is narrowness: interviewers probe whether you can work outside the environment you know.

**The strongest answer in either case is a system with a history.** "I built this, it went wrong in this specific way, here is what I changed and what happened next." Consultancy careers make that answer harder to produce, which is why assignments with a follow-up phase are worth seeking out.

**Titles travel unevenly.** Consultancy ladders often promote faster in title than in scope, and in-house employers discount that when they see it. The reverse also happens: a small in-house team may leave you at one title for years while your actual responsibility grows well past it. In both cases, describe scope rather than relying on the label.

**Sector credibility compounds in-house.** After three years in energy, insurance or healthcare, employers in that sector see you as one of their own, which opens roles that are otherwise hard to reach. Consultancy gives breadth instead — useful for optionality, weaker for belonging.

If you are choosing partly on how the next step will look, ask yourself which suspicion you would rather have to answer, because you will be answering one of them.

## Real example

### An engineer in Eindhoven who did both, in the right order

An engineer joins a consultancy from university. In three years she works at a bank, a manufacturer and a healthcare provider, and learns to orient herself quickly in unfamiliar systems. She also notices a pattern: every assignment ends at the demonstration or the handover, and she has never once seen what happened to a model six months later.

She moves in-house, to a logistics company forty minutes from home, taking a role with a slightly smaller title. The first year is uncomfortable — the variety is gone, and the problems are the same problems.

Then the compounding starts. The forecasting system she built in year one is retrained, criticised, repaired and extended in year two, and by year three she has changed how the planning department works. When she interviews later, every conversation focuses on that period rather than on the three consultancy years.

Her own summary: consultancy taught her how to start, in-house taught her how to finish, and she would do them in that order again.

## Key Takeaways

- Consultancy builds breadth, orientation speed and a professional cohort; in-house builds ownership, domain depth and the feedback loop of living with your own systems.
- Utilisation explains most of consultancy's behaviour — ask about targets, protected development time, bench periods, travel and assignment choice.
- Check secondment terms, including Waadi-related arrangements and any restriction on joining a client directly.
- In-house teams vary enormously; ask what is in production, who else is on the team and how people progress.
- Consultancy suits early career, in-house usually suits mid-career, and doing both in that order is a strong combination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate weighing two offers) Is consultancy or in-house better for an AI career?
Neither is better in general. Consultancy builds breadth and orientation speed; in-house builds ownership and domain depth. Choose by what your career currently lacks.

### (Scenario: candidate considering a consultancy) What should I ask a consultancy before accepting?
Target utilisation, protected development time, what happens between assignments, travel expectations, how much say you have in assignments and any restriction on joining a client.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about depth) Does consultancy work hurt me at mid-career interviews?
It can if you have never maintained a system. Interviewers ask what happened after deployment, so aim for assignments that include production work and follow-up.

### (Scenario: candidate seconded to a client) What rules apply to secondment in the Netherlands?
Secondment and staffing are regulated, including under the Waadi. Ask which arrangement applies to you and check client and non-solicitation clauses in your contract.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting both) Can I combine the two paths?
Yes. Many people do consultancy early for breadth and move in-house for depth, and some return to advisory work later with far more credibility.
