The role is at a manufacturer, an insurer or a municipality, and somewhere in the final conversation it becomes clear: your manager would be the operations director, the finance lead or an IT manager whose background is infrastructure. They are enthusiastic, they understand the business problem well and they have never trained a model or reviewed a pull request. For a large share of AI and data roles in the Netherlands this is simply how it is, particularly outside technology companies — and candidates tend to either dismiss it as irrelevant or treat it as disqualifying. Both reactions skip the question that matters, which is not whether your manager can read your code but where the things a technical manager normally provides will come from instead.

## Why This Is So Common Here

A large share of Dutch data work sits at SMEs, regional manufacturers, care organisations, water boards, insurers and public bodies. Many of them are hiring their first or second data professional, which means there is no technical data leader to report to — the function did not exist last year.

Even in larger organisations, data teams frequently sit under business units rather than engineering, on the reasonable theory that proximity to the problem matters more than proximity to the code. And AI literacy obligations have pushed managers to engage with the subject without turning them into practitioners.

So this is not a niche situation to be avoided. It is the majority case outside technology companies, and the useful skill is recognising the good versions of it.

## What a Non-Technical Manager Genuinely Gives You

**Access.** They know who owns which process, whose cooperation you need and how to get a meeting with the person blocking your data request. For a first data hire, this is worth more than technical supervision.

**Sponsorship.** A manager who sits in the management team can protect your time, defend the budget and make sure your results are used rather than filed.

**Business judgement.** They can tell you which of three technically interesting problems is actually worth solving, which is the most common failure of technically-led data teams.

**Translation upwards.** Your work needs to be explained to people who will never read your documentation. A manager fluent in that language is an asset.

**Freedom.** Nobody second-guesses your architecture choices. For experienced professionals this is genuinely pleasant.

## What It Costs You

**No technical review.** Nobody checks your code, your evaluation approach or your assumptions. Early in a career this is the most serious risk; mid-career it is a slow erosion.

**Estimation is unanchored.** A manager who cannot judge whether something takes two days or two months either accepts your estimates uncritically or presses on them arbitrarily.

**Invisible technical debt.** Shortcuts do not show up in any report your manager reads, so the pressure is always towards the visible result rather than the sustainable one.

**Weak advocacy for technical growth.** Training budget, conference attendance, tooling and engineering practice are easier to justify to someone who has needed them.

**Evaluation by proxy.** Your performance review is based on stakeholder satisfaction and visible output, which rewards responsiveness over quality. Over years, that shapes what you become good at.

**No natural next step.** In a technical hierarchy there is a senior role above you. Here, the ladder may be into management of something else entirely.

## The Questions That Decide It

- **Who will review my technical work?** The single most important question. An internal peer, an architect from IT, an external mentor or a consultancy on retainer are all acceptable answers. "Nobody, we trust you" is not.
- **How will my performance be assessed, and by whom?** Listen for whether technical quality appears at all.
- **What happens when I say something will take three months?** Ask how they would test that claim. A thoughtful manager admits they would ask someone else.
- **Is there a budget for training, tooling and external expertise?** This is where a non-technical manager's support becomes concrete.
- **Who decides technical priorities when the business wants something quickly?** If the answer is always the business, you will accumulate debt you cannot pay down.
- **What does progression look like for me here?** A vague answer is common; an honest "we have not thought about it, let us work it out" is better than an invented one.

## How to Make It Work

**Arrange your own technical review.** Ask for it as part of the offer: a budget for an external mentor, a few hours a month from a consultancy, or membership of a peer network. Frame it as risk reduction for the employer, which is exactly what it is.

**Translate deliberately.** Report in the manager's units — hours saved, waste reduced, forecast error, complaints handled — and keep the technical detail for documentation. Managers who understand the value defend the work.

**Make the invisible visible.** Put technical debt, monitoring gaps and data quality problems into the same reporting where you put results, with a cost attached. If it never appears, it will never be prioritised.

**Set expectations early and repeatedly.** The first three months establish whether a request is answered in a day or estimated properly. That pattern is hard to change later.

**Find your peers elsewhere.** Meetups, communities and former colleagues become your technical environment. This takes deliberate effort and is the difference between growing and stagnating.

**Document as if you will leave.** Not out of pessimism, but because in an organisation without technical management, undocumented systems become a trap for you specifically.

## When to Decline

- Nobody can review your work and the employer will not fund anyone who can, particularly if you have fewer than about five years of experience.
- The manager shows no curiosity about the subject during interviews, or treats your questions about data as detail.
- Performance will be judged purely on responsiveness to requests.
- You would be the only data person and also the only person who cares about engineering practice.
- Previous data professionals left quickly and nobody can explain why.

## When It Is a Good Job Anyway

- The manager is genuinely curious, asks good questions and admits what they do not know.
- There is a named budget for external review, training and tooling.
- You are experienced enough that autonomy is an advantage rather than a risk.
- The business access and sponsorship are strong — which is where these roles outperform technically-led teams that nobody listens to.
- The work reaches real users, which forces quality more effectively than any reviewer.

## Where the Reporting Line Sits Also Matters

Beyond whether your manager is technical, the department you report into shapes the job in ways worth checking before accepting.

**Under a business unit** — operations, finance, commerce — you sit close to the problem and the sponsor. Access to domain experts is easy and your work is used. The risk is narrowness: you serve one department's priorities, and enterprise-wide data problems are someone else's concern.

**Under IT** you get infrastructure, security and engineering practice, and often better tooling. The risk is distance from the business, and being treated as a delivery function that receives requirements rather than shapes them.

**Under a central data team** you get peers and standards, which is the best case for technical growth. The risk is being seen as an internal consultant with no ownership of outcomes.

**Directly under a director or the board** happens at smaller organisations. Sponsorship is excellent and structure is absent; everything depends on that one relationship.

None is wrong, but they produce different jobs under the same title. Ask which one you would sit in, who sets your priorities, and — the revealing question — who you would go to if your manager and a stakeholder disagreed about what you should work on.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Zwolle who asked one question and got a mentor

A data scientist with six years of experience is offered a role at a regional care organisation, reporting to the operations director. The problem is genuinely interesting: planning care visits with highly variable durations. The director is engaged, articulate about the process and entirely non-technical.

In the final conversation she asks who would review her technical work. The honest answer is nobody. Rather than declining, she proposes an arrangement: a modest budget for an external mentor, four hours a month, to review her approach and code.

The director agrees, partly because it costs less than a fortnight of consultancy and visibly reduces the risk of hiring someone they cannot evaluate. She takes the role.

Two years later the planning model is in daily use, she has presented it at a sector event, and the mentor arrangement has become a standing item in the department budget. The director still cannot read her code. He has, however, defended her time in three budget rounds — which is the part she could not have done herself.

## What to Watch During the Interview Itself

The interview is a sample of how this manager will work with you, and a few moments reveal more than any answer.

**Notice what they ask about.** A manager who asks how you would approach the problem, what could go wrong and what you would need is engaged. One who only asks about tools and timelines is buying a service.

**Notice how they handle not knowing.** "I do not know what that means, explain it to me" is an excellent sign. Nodding along at terminology they clearly do not follow predicts a relationship where you cannot raise problems.

**Notice whether they describe failure.** Managers who can name something that did not work, and what they learned, tend to be reasonable when your first model disappoints.

**Notice who else they involve.** Bringing a domain expert or an IT colleague into the conversation shows they understand that your success depends on people beyond them.

**Notice the estimation conversation.** If you say something takes three months and they simply accept it without curiosity, that same absence of pushback will later become an absence of defence when someone demands it in three weeks.

None of these is disqualifying alone. Together they tell you whether you would be working with someone who understands the shape of the work, even without understanding the content.

## Key Takeaways

- Reporting to a non-technical manager is the normal case outside Dutch technology companies, especially for first and second data hires.
- They provide access, sponsorship, business judgement and translation; they cannot provide technical review, credible estimation pressure or advocacy for engineering practice.
- The decisive question is who will review your technical work — and "nobody, we trust you" is not an acceptable answer early in a career.
- Make it work by arranging external review, reporting in business units, making technical debt visible and building a peer network outside the organisation.
- Decline when there is no reviewer, no budget for one, no curiosity from the manager and no explanation for why previous data hires left.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a role under a business manager) Is it bad to report to a non-technical manager?
Not inherently. It is the normal situation outside technology companies and brings real advantages in access and sponsorship, provided technical review comes from somewhere.

### (Scenario: candidate with a few years of experience) What is the biggest risk?
Having nobody to review your work. Early in a career this shapes habits you may spend years correcting, which is why external review should be part of the offer.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) How do I ask for a technical mentor?
Frame it as risk reduction: a few hours a month from an external reviewer costs far less than a consultancy engagement and protects the employer's investment in you.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about promotion) How does career progression work without a technical manager?
Ask explicitly. Where no technical ladder exists, agree what growth means in concrete terms — scope, budget, mentoring others — rather than relying on titles.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the manager) What should I look for in the interview?
Curiosity and honesty about what they do not know, a willingness to fund review and training, and a clear answer on who decides priorities when the business wants something fast.
