Somewhere in the second interview it comes out. They tried this before. There was a project, or a consultancy, or a predecessor in your chair, and it did not work — the model was never used, the platform was abandoned, the pilot was quietly stopped after eighteen months and a significant budget. Candidates typically react in one of two ways: treating it as disqualifying, or ignoring it because the role sounds good. Both are mistakes. A failed project is the most useful piece of information an employer can give you, because it tells you exactly how this organisation behaves when data work meets reality. The question is not whether they failed. It is what kind of failure it was, and what changed afterwards.

## Why These Projects Fail

The pattern in Dutch organisations is consistent and rarely technical.

**The problem was never defined.** Somebody wanted to do something with AI, a use case was found to justify the ambition, and nobody could say what decision would change as a result.

**No owner in the business.** The data team built it, the department that was supposed to use it never asked for it, and adoption was treated as someone else's problem.

**The data was not ready.** Two years of effort revealed that the underlying records were inconsistent, incomplete or meaningless in ways nobody had checked first.

**The sponsor left.** Reorganisation, a new manager with different priorities, or a budget round. This kills more data projects in large Dutch organisations than any technical cause.

**Compliance stopped it.** Privacy, works council objections or governance questions that were raised too late — and that should have been raised at the start.

**The pilot was never meant to become anything.** It succeeded as a demonstration and had no path to production, no budget for running costs and no owner.

Every one of these has a different implication for the role you are being offered.

## Five Failures and What Each Means for You

**Technical naivety.** They underestimated the work, hired too junior, or bought something that did not fit. Implication: fixable, and you are the fix. Good sign if they now understand what they were missing.

**Adoption failure.** The system worked and nobody used it. Implication: the hard problem is organisational, and hiring another technical person changes nothing unless the business now owns it. Ask what is different.

**Data foundations.** They discovered their data could not support the ambition. Implication: your first eighteen months are data engineering and governance, whatever the vacancy says. Fine if you want that and are told honestly; miserable if you expected modelling.

**Governance halt.** Privacy or oversight questions stopped it. Implication: the organisation now knows those questions exist, which is genuinely good — provided compliance is resourced rather than resented.

**Political failure.** The sponsor left, the department resisted, or two executives disagreed. Implication: the most dangerous kind, because nothing technical was wrong and nothing has necessarily changed. Ask who sponsors this attempt and how secure they are.

## Why Joining Now Can Be Excellent

Organisations that have failed once are often much better employers for the second attempt.

**Expectations have been reset.** Nobody expects a transformation in six months anymore, and being allowed to spend a quarter on data foundations is a luxury that first-attempt organisations rarely grant.

**The budget frequently survives.** Money already approved for a capability is easier to keep than to obtain, and you may inherit tooling and infrastructure paid for by the failed attempt.

**Somebody learned something.** If there was a proper post-mortem, the organisation now knows things about itself that most employers never examine.

**Appetite for realism.** After a failure, plain speaking is welcomed rather than treated as negativity. That is an unusually good environment for a careful practitioner.

**Lower competition for the role.** Candidates avoid these employers, which occasionally puts a strong job within reach of a candidate who would otherwise be outbid.

## Why It Can Be a Trap

**Nothing changed except the names.** Same sponsor, same undefined problem, same department that did not want it, now with a new hire and the same expectations.

**Burned stakeholders.** People who lost time on the previous attempt will not engage with the next one, and their cooperation is exactly what you need.

**Blame culture.** If the failure is explained entirely as someone else's incompetence, you are hearing how they will describe you.

**Exhausted appetite.** Management says it wants to try again and will not fund anything. Watch what is budgeted, not what is said.

**A second failure is expensive for you.** Two years with nothing delivered is harder to explain in interviews than a year of unglamorous work that shipped.

## The Questions That Matter

- **What exactly was attempted, and what happened?** Ask for the story in detail. Vagueness is informative.
- **Was there a post-mortem, and what did it conclude?**
- **What has changed since?** The single most important question. "We are hiring you" is not an answer.
- **Who sponsored it then, and who sponsors this now?** If it is the same person with the same expectations, be careful.
- **Which department would use what I build, and did they ask for it?**
- **What is the budget for running things, not only building them?**
- **What happened to the people involved?** Whether they were blamed, reassigned or listened to tells you the culture precisely.
- **What would we do differently the first time something is harder than expected?**

## Listen for Analysis, Not Blame

The way the story is told matters more than the story.

**Analysis sounds like:** we scoped it too broadly; we did not involve the planners until too late; our data was worse than we believed; we treated a pilot as a product; the sponsor left and we did not re-establish ownership.

**Blame sounds like:** the consultancy oversold it; the previous person could not communicate; the business would not cooperate; IT blocked everything.

Some blame is accurate — suppliers do oversell, and some colleagues are obstructive. But an organisation that can describe its own contribution to a failure has done the work that makes a second attempt viable. One that cannot has arranged the story so that nothing needs to change.

## The Stakeholder Recovery Problem

Whatever the technical situation, you inherit a trust deficit. Someone in operations gave twelve meetings to the last attempt and got nothing, and they will not volunteer their time again on the strength of your enthusiasm.

The only remedy is a small, fast, useful delivery for the people who were burned most. Not a platform, not a roadmap — something they asked for, within weeks, that saves them effort. Trust in these situations is rebuilt by usefulness and by nothing else.

Plan for this explicitly, and say so in the interview. Hiring managers who have lived through a failure recognise the approach immediately, and it separates you from candidates promising a strategy.

## When to Accept

- The failure is clearly explained, with the organisation's own contribution acknowledged.
- Something concrete has changed: a new sponsor, a defined problem, a business owner, a budget for operations.
- The failure was technical or foundational rather than political.
- There is appetite for a modest first delivery rather than a grand plan.
- You would have access to the people affected, and they are willing to try again.
- You are experienced enough to set expectations and hold them.

## When to Decline

- Nobody will describe what happened in detail.
- The story is entirely about other people's failings.
- The same expectations are attached to the same unchanged situation.
- The department that would use your work is openly uninterested.
- There is no budget beyond your salary.
- You would be the second person alone in a role that already defeated someone.

## How to Structure Your First Year

Start with an honest inventory: what exists, what still runs, what can be salvaged and what should be retired formally so that it stops haunting conversations.

Deliver something small within the first quarter, for the most sceptical stakeholder available.

Write down the constraints you find, and share them early. Organisations recovering from failure respond well to written realism, and it protects you if the same constraints bite later.

Re-establish ownership in the business for anything you build, with a named person who wants it. The absence of that is what killed the last attempt more often than not.

And agree what success looks like in twelve months, in writing, with the person who sponsors you. Failed-project organisations are prone to unspoken expectations, and unspoken expectations are what you inherited.

## Real example

### An engineer in Tilburg who asked for the post-mortem

An engineer interviews at a healthcare provider that spent two years and a substantial budget on a capacity forecasting project that was never used. The vacancy describes a fresh start.

He asks what happened. The story is candid: the model was built with a consultancy, it performed reasonably, and the planners never adopted it because it produced numbers they could not reconcile with their own experience and nobody had involved them in defining what "good" meant. The project ended when the sponsoring director moved on.

He asks what has changed. Two things, concretely: the planning manager is now the owner of any forecasting work and has asked for it herself, and there is budget for a person rather than a project.

He accepts, and spends his first six weeks sitting with planners rather than modelling. His first delivery is not a forecast: it is a reconciliation showing where the old model's numbers and the planners' figures diverged, and why. It takes three weeks and changes the relationship entirely.

The forecasting work that follows is adopted, partly because it is better and mostly because the people using it helped define what it had to do.

## Key Takeaways

- A failed project is information, not a verdict: the kind of failure determines whether the role is a good one.
- Technical, foundational and governance failures are usually recoverable; political failures often are not, because nothing has necessarily changed.
- Organisations that have failed once frequently offer reset expectations, surviving budget, hard-won self-knowledge and appetite for realism.
- Listen for analysis rather than blame — an employer who cannot describe its own contribution has arranged the story so nothing needs to change.
- Plan for stakeholder recovery with one small, fast, useful delivery for the people who were burned, and re-establish business ownership for everything you build.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate told about a previous failure) Is a failed AI project a reason to avoid an employer?
No. It is a reason to ask precisely what failed and what changed. Organisations that failed once often make better employers for the second attempt.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the story) Which failures are most dangerous to walk into?
Political ones — a sponsor who left, a department that resisted, two executives who disagreed. Nothing technical was wrong, so nothing has necessarily changed.

### (Scenario: candidate in the interview) What is the single best question to ask?
What has changed since. "We are hiring you" is not an answer; a new sponsor, a defined problem, a business owner or an operating budget is.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about stakeholders) How do I deal with people burned by the last attempt?
Deliver something small, fast and genuinely useful to the most sceptical of them before proposing anything larger. Trust returns through usefulness, not through plans.

### (Scenario: candidate protecting their CV) What is the risk if it fails again?
Two years with nothing delivered is hard to explain. Reduce it by agreeing written success criteria and by shipping something modest early.
