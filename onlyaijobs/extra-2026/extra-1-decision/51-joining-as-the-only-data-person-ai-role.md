The offer is flattering in a way that should make you careful. You would be the first data hire, reporting to a director, with freedom to choose tools and priorities and a mandate to "build the data function". There is no data team because you would be the data team. For some people at some career stages this is the best job they ever take. For others it is two years of building things nobody reviews, in habits nobody corrects, ending with a CV that is hard to interpret. The difference is largely predictable in advance, and it has less to do with your ability than with what the organisation around you provides.

## What You Genuinely Gain

**Ownership from end to end.** You see the whole chain — the question, the data, the model, the deployment, the person using it — which few people in larger teams experience early.

**Visibility.** You work directly with decision makers, and the effect of your work is visible to them. In a large organisation the same contribution disappears into a reporting line.

**Breadth, fast.** Data engineering, analysis, modelling, dashboards, governance conversations, vendor selection. You become genuinely versatile, which is valuable and hard to acquire elsewhere.

**Judgement.** With nobody to defer to, you learn to make decisions and live with them. That maturity shows in later interviews.

**Room to shape things.** Choosing the stack, setting conventions and defining how the organisation works with data is a rare opportunity, and it is real work worth doing well.

## What You Lose, and Why It Compounds

**Review.** Nobody checks your approach before you spend three weeks on it, or notices the leakage in your validation set, or asks why you chose that metric. Mistakes are not caught; they become habits.

**Calibration.** Without colleagues, you cannot tell whether your work is good. People in solo roles routinely over- or underestimate their own level, and both are costly at the next interview.

**Learning speed.** Most professional growth comes from watching people better than you work. Courses do not substitute for it.

**Cover.** Holidays, illness and busy periods have no absorber. Systems you built are yours to support, permanently.

**Engineering practice.** Testing, monitoring, deployment and documentation are the disciplines most easily skipped when nobody depends on them — and most heavily assessed when you next apply.

The compounding is the part to take seriously. Two years of unreviewed work does not equal two years of reviewed work, and the gap widens.

## The Question That Decides It: What Surrounds You?

"Only data person" covers two entirely different situations, and this distinction matters more than anything else in the decision.

**Solo among technical colleagues.** There are software engineers, a platform team, perhaps a BI specialist. You have no data peers, but you have people who review code, run deployments, discuss architecture and hold you to engineering standards. The gap is domain-specific mentoring, which is manageable.

**Solo in a non-technical organisation.** Nobody can assess your work, nobody runs infrastructure, and every technical decision is yours alone, including ones you have never made. This is where solo roles genuinely damage careers — especially early ones.

If the organisation has engineers, this decision is often a yes. If it does not, the bar is much higher.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **Who else here writes code, and would they review mine?**
- **What happens to what I build when I am on holiday?** Listen for whether anyone has thought about it.
- **Who decides priorities, and who says no on my behalf?** A solo data person with five internal customers and no protection is a request queue, not a role.
- **What is the budget for tooling, infrastructure and training?** Solo roles without budget become manual reporting jobs.
- **Is there external support available** — a consultancy, an architect, an advisory arrangement — for decisions I should not make alone?
- **What data exists already, and who owns it?** If governance, quality and access are unresolved, most of year one will be spent there rather than on modelling.
- **Who handles privacy and compliance?** Under the GDPR and the AI Act, someone must, and if the answer is "you", that is a substantial part of the job.
- **Would you hire a second person, and on what trigger?**

## When a Solo Role Is a Good Move

- You have three or more years of experience and have already been reviewed properly by good people.
- There are technical colleagues who can look at your work.
- The organisation has a real, defined problem rather than a wish to "do something with data".
- There is budget, a sponsor with authority and a stated intention to grow the function.
- You want the breadth and the ownership, and you have a horizon in mind.
- The employer accepts external support for decisions outside your experience.

## When to Decline

- It is your first or second role, and nobody can teach you.
- The organisation is entirely non-technical and expects you to build and run production systems alone.
- There is no budget, and the tooling question is answered with "we will see".
- Five departments expect service and nobody prioritises.
- The previous person left within a year and nobody will explain why.
- Compliance responsibility is being handed to you without support or authority.

## How to Protect Your Development If You Accept

Solo roles are survivable and often excellent, provided you deliberately replace what a team would have given you.

**Buy review.** Ask for a budget for an external senior reviewer or a consultancy arrangement, even a few days a quarter. Frame it to the employer as risk reduction, because that is exactly what it is.

**Join something external.** A meetup, a professional community, a peer group of other solo practitioners. Calibration comes from seeing other people's work.

**Keep engineering standards as if someone were watching.** Version control, tests, documentation, monitoring. This is the discipline that decays fastest alone and matters most later.

**Write down what you build and why.** Decision records serve the organisation and give you interview material that is otherwise hard to reconstruct.

**Ask for a training budget in the contract,** not in the policy.

**Set a review point.** After eighteen months, ask honestly whether you are still learning. If the answer is no, the ownership is no longer compensating for the isolation.

## What It Does to Your Next Application

Hiring managers read solo experience carefully, and they look for specific things: whether you shipped anything to real users, whether you can explain your technical choices and their alternatives, whether you know what you did not do well, and whether your standards survived without enforcement.

Candidates who can answer those come across as unusually mature. Candidates who cannot come across as unassessed, which is the risk the interviewer is trying to price. The difference is made during the role, not in the application — which is the strongest argument for keeping external review and professional contact alive throughout.

## The First Ninety Days Alone

A solo role has no induction, because there is nobody whose job it is to give you one. What you do in the first three months sets the pattern for everything after, and the common mistake is to start building immediately.

**Weeks one to three: inventory.** Find out what data exists, in which systems, who owns each one and who currently produces the numbers people rely on. There is almost always a spreadsheet somewhere that the organisation actually runs on, maintained by someone in finance or operations. Find that person first; they know more about the data than the systems documentation does.

**Weeks three to six: one visible, small delivery.** Something a specific person asked for and will use within the month. Not the platform, not the architecture — credibility. In an organisation with no data function, nobody yet knows what you are for, and one useful thing answers that question better than a roadmap.

**Weeks six to twelve: establish the boundaries.** Agree with your sponsor who may request work and how priorities are set. Solo roles that skip this become a helpdesk within a year, and it is much harder to reverse than to prevent.

**Throughout: resist rebuilding everything.** The temptation to replace the reporting spreadsheets with a proper platform in month two is strong and usually wrong. Improve what exists, document as you go, and propose bigger changes once you have credit to spend.

**Also throughout: write the decision log.** Every choice — this tool, that structure, this definition — with the reason. In a solo role you are the only institutional memory, and the log is what protects the organisation and your own account of your work.

By day ninety you want three things in place: one delivered thing people use, one named sponsor who protects your priorities, and a written picture of the data landscape nobody had before.

## Real example

### An analyst in Zwolle who negotiated a reviewer

An analyst with four years of experience is offered a first-data-hire role at a mid-sized manufacturer, half an hour from home. Good salary, direct line to the operations director, genuine problems in planning and maintenance.

She asks the questions. There are two software developers maintaining internal applications, which means code review is possible. There is a tooling budget. Nobody has thought about holidays.

She accepts with three conditions: the developers review her code monthly, a small annual budget funds two days a quarter with an external senior data scientist, and the maintenance system she builds gets a documented handover so the developers can support it while she is away.

Year one is mostly data quality and governance work, which she had not expected. Year two produces a maintenance model that runs in production. The quarterly external reviews catch two significant mistakes, including a validation problem that would have made her results look far better than they were.

Three years later she moves to a larger team as a senior. In the interview, the external review arrangement is what convinces them she can be trusted to work unsupervised — because it showed she knew she should not be.

## Key Takeaways

- Solo data roles offer ownership, visibility and breadth, and remove review, calibration, mentoring and cover.
- The decisive question is what surrounds you: technical colleagues make a solo role workable; a wholly non-technical organisation makes it risky, especially early in a career.
- Ask who reviews your work, who covers your absence, who prioritises, what the budget is and who handles privacy and compliance obligations.
- Decline when it is an early-career role with nobody to learn from, when there is no budget, or when several departments expect service without prioritisation.
- If you accept, buy external review, keep engineering standards, document decisions and set an honest review point at eighteen months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: early-career candidate) Is a solo data role bad for someone starting out?
Usually, yes, unless technical colleagues can review your work. Early growth depends on watching and being corrected by people who are better than you.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the organisation) What matters most in deciding?
Whether anyone else there is technical. Solo among engineers is workable; solo in a wholly non-technical organisation is where most careers stall.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) What should I ask for before accepting?
Code review from technical colleagues, budget for external senior review, a training budget in the contract, clear prioritisation and a documented handover for absence.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about their CV) How do employers read solo experience?
Carefully. They look for shipped work, explainable technical choices, awareness of your own mistakes and standards maintained without supervision.

### (Scenario: candidate already in a solo role) How do I know when to move on?
When you are no longer learning. Set a review point around eighteen months and answer honestly rather than letting ownership substitute for development.
