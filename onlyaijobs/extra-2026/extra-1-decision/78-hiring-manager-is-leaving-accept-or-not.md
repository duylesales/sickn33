You accepted the role partly because of them. They understood the work, they were honest about the difficulties, they agreed to the things you asked for, and you left the final interview thinking this is someone you could learn from. Then the message arrives — before your start date, or in your second week — that they are moving to another team, another company, or a role two levels up. Your contract is unchanged and your job may not be. Managers matter more than almost anything else in how a role turns out, and a departure at this moment removes the one person who knew what they hired you to do.

## The Situations Are Not Equivalent

**Promoted internally, staying close.** The best case. The commitments they made are likely to be honoured, they remain an ally, and they may still influence the team.

**Moving to another part of the same organisation.** Reasonable. They can vouch for you internally and explain the agreements, but they will no longer control your priorities or your budget.

**Leaving the company entirely.** The most serious case. Everything verbal goes with them, and the team's direction is now whatever their successor decides.

**Parental leave or a temporary absence.** Usually fine. Ask who covers and for how long, and get commitments confirmed by that person.

**Retiring.** Often accompanied by a longer handover and a successor already chosen, which makes it easier to assess.

Establish which one this is before doing anything else.

## What Survives, and What Does Not

**Your contract survives.** Salary, hours, contract type, holiday and anything else written in it are obligations of the employer, not of the manager.

**Verbal commitments do not.** The project they promised you, the training budget agreed in conversation, the understanding about home working, the plan to hire a second engineer — all of that lives in one person's memory, and that person is leaving.

**The team's direction is open.** A new manager may reorganise priorities, and the mandate that made the role attractive may not be their mandate.

**Your onboarding is at risk.** The person who knew why you were hired, and what you were supposed to do first, will not be there to direct it.

**Colleagues may follow.** Strong managers often take people with them, or their departure triggers others who were staying for them.

## Questions to Ask Immediately

Ask them, directly and quickly. Managers in this position are usually candid, partly out of guilt.

- **Why are you leaving, and how long has this been in motion?** A resignation that predates your acceptance is a different matter from one decided last week.
- **Who will manage the team, and when will that be decided?**
- **What happens to what we agreed?** Ask them to write the commitments in an email and copy their manager or HR.
- **Is the team's mandate secure, or does it depend on you?**
- **Would you still recommend joining, honestly?** Most people answer this truthfully.
- **Is anyone else leaving?**

And ask the organisation: who decides in the meantime, when will a successor be appointed, and can you speak with the person who will make that decision.

## Speak to the Successor, or to the Decision Maker

If a successor is known, ask to meet them before your start date. It is a completely reasonable request, and how the organisation handles it is informative: an employer that arranges it quickly is treating you as someone worth retaining.

If no successor exists yet, ask to speak with the manager one level up — the person who will choose. That conversation should cover whether the role as described still stands, whether the budget and mandate are intact, and whether your agreed commitments are recognised at that level.

If both requests are refused, you have learned something significant about how much support the role actually has.

## Get It in Writing, Now

This is the practical heart of the matter. Whatever was agreed verbally should be confirmed in writing before your start date, ideally by someone who is staying.

Scope of the role and the first project. Salary and any agreed review point. Training budget and time. Home working or hybrid arrangements. Equipment. Any commitment about a second hire, a specific technology, or a protected share of technical work.

A short email from the departing manager, copied to their own manager and to HR, is usually enough. Nobody will find this strange; departing managers do it routinely, and the request signals professionalism rather than distrust.

## When to Start Anyway

- The organisation, not the individual, is why the role is attractive — the domain, the data, the systems, the location.
- The team is strong and the work is defined independently of the manager.
- A successor is named and the conversation with them goes well.
- Your commitments have been confirmed in writing by someone who is staying.
- The manager was promoted internally and remains reachable.
- You have met colleagues you would want to work with regardless.

## When to Withdraw or Delay

- The manager is leaving the organisation and the role's mandate was personally theirs.
- No successor is planned for months and nobody will discuss the interim.
- Several team members are leaving at the same time.
- The organisation refuses to confirm anything in writing.
- The role was explicitly built around a strategy that departs with them.
- You accepted mainly to work with that person, and you know it.

Withdrawing before starting is uncomfortable and it is better than resigning in month four. Do it politely and promptly, and be honest about the reason — employers understand this situation better than candidates expect.

## If You Have Already Started

You have more information and slightly less freedom. Give it a fair period — a quarter is reasonable — while doing three things.

Establish a relationship with whoever is acting as your manager, and with the person one level up. Get your commitments reconfirmed by them, in writing, framed as making sure nothing was lost in the handover. And deliver something visible early, because in the absence of a sponsor, your work is the only thing making your case.

If, after a quarter, the role has become something you would not have accepted, that is legitimate information rather than a failure. A probationary period, where one applies, exists precisely because either side may find the arrangement is not what they expected — and leaving early from a role that changed underneath you is far easier to explain to a future employer than most candidates fear.

## The Wider Signal: Why Are They Going?

A departure is also information about the organisation, and it is worth reading before you decide whether the role is still the one you wanted.

**A better opportunity.** People leave good employers for good reasons. If they are candid about it and speak warmly about the team, this says little about the place.

**Frustration with the organisation.** Listen for what they do not say. A manager who cannot describe why the role will succeed under someone else is often telling you that it was succeeding only because they were shielding it.

**A reorganisation they saw coming.** Managers usually know about restructuring before it is announced. If the departure coincides with vague answers about the team's future, ask the level above directly whether any reorganisation is in preparation.

**They were not supported.** A manager who asked for budget, headcount or a mandate and did not get it is leaving a role that will be equally unsupported for their successor — and for you.

**A pattern.** Ask how many managers this team has had in three years. Two is unremarkable. Four means the problem is above the manager, and a successor will not fix it.

It is worth asking the team the same question separately. Managers describe their own departure carefully, for understandable reasons, while colleagues who are staying tend to be more direct about whether this is one person's opportunity or the third exit in eighteen months. If the process has already included a team conversation, a short follow-up message to one of them is entirely reasonable at this point — you are not asking them to criticise anyone, only to tell you whether the team feels stable.

The useful question to the level above is not "why is he leaving" but "what will you do differently for the next person in this role". A thoughtful answer suggests the organisation has understood something. A dismissive one suggests the next departure is already in motion, and that you would be starting under a manager who is also, eventually, on their way out.

## Real example

### An engineer in Nijmegen who asked for one email

An engineer accepts a role at a healthcare organisation, largely because the data lead is impressive and has agreed to two specific things: a protected day a week for engineering work, and a budget for external model review.

Three weeks before his start date, the lead is appointed to a role at another employer.

He calls her. She is candid: the move had been in discussion for two months and she should have told him. She agrees to write both commitments in an email, copied to her director and to HR, and tells him honestly that she thinks the team is worth joining and that the director is the person who actually controls the budget.

He then asks to meet the director. In that conversation he learns a successor will be recruited within three months, an interim lead has been appointed from within the team, and both commitments are recognised and confirmed.

He starts. The first quarter is unsettled, and his protected day survives because it exists in an email that two people other than the departing manager have read.

The new lead arrives five months later and turns out to be, in his words, less inspiring and more reliable — which he says he would take again.

## Key Takeaways

- Establish which situation this is: internal promotion, internal move, leaving the organisation, temporary absence or retirement, because they carry very different risk.
- Your contract survives; verbal commitments about scope, training, projects, hires and working arrangements do not.
- Ask the departing manager directly why and when, and ask them to confirm every agreement in writing, copied to someone who is staying.
- Ask to meet the successor or the decision maker one level up; refusal of both requests is itself an answer.
- Withdrawing before starting is far less costly than resigning in month four, and employers understand the situation better than candidates expect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate before the start date) Should I withdraw if the hiring manager leaves?
Not automatically. Withdraw if the role's mandate was personally theirs, if no successor is planned, if others are leaving too, or if nothing can be confirmed in writing.

### (Scenario: candidate with verbal agreements) What happens to what we agreed?
Anything in your contract stands. Verbal commitments — scope, training budget, a protected day, a second hire — leave with the manager unless confirmed in writing by someone who is staying.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting reassurance) Who should I speak to?
The successor if one is named, and otherwise the manager one level up who will choose them. An employer that arranges this quickly is treating you as worth keeping.

### (Scenario: candidate who already started) I have started and my manager left — what now?
Give it a quarter. Build a relationship with the interim and the level above, get commitments reconfirmed in writing, and deliver something visible early.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about their record) Does leaving early look bad?
Less than people fear when the role changed underneath you. It is far easier to explain than a year spent in a job that was never what you accepted.
