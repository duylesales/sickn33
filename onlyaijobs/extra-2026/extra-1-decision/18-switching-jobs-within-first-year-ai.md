Four months in, the pattern is clear. The machine learning role is mostly building dashboards. The data platform that was promised has no budget. The manager who hired you has left, and their replacement has different priorities. You are not miserable, exactly, but you are not doing what you moved for, and the thought of two more years like this is heavy. Leaving a job within the first year feels like an admission of failure and a mark on your CV. Sometimes it is the right decision anyway — and the way to tell is to separate what can still change from what cannot.

This article provides general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, consult an employment lawyer, your union or the free information service **Juridisch Loket**.

## First, Name What Actually Went Wrong

Early disappointment usually has one of five causes, and they have very different prognoses.

**The role was misrepresented.** You were hired for modelling and the work is reporting, or the "team" turned out to be you alone. Prognosis: poor unless someone with authority agrees to change the scope.

**The foundations are missing.** Data access, infrastructure or budget never materialised. Prognosis: depends entirely on whether leadership treats this as a problem or as normal.

**The sponsor left.** The person who wanted your work has gone, and the replacement has other priorities. Prognosis: sometimes recoverable by finding a new sponsor internally.

**The manager is the problem.** Prognosis: poor in the short term; managers rarely change quickly, though internal moves exist.

**Your own expectations were off.** The job is what was described, and you discovered you want something different. Prognosis: honest reflection needed before you repeat the pattern elsewhere.

Only the first two are about the employer misjudging or misrepresenting. The rest may still justify leaving, but they change what you say in interviews.

## The Three-Month Test

Before deciding, run a short structured attempt at fixing it.

**Write down what would need to be true** for this job to be worth staying in: a specific project, access to specific data, one senior colleague, a change of scope.

**Raise it once, clearly, with the person who can change it.** Not a complaint — a proposal. "I was hired for X; currently I am doing Y. Here is what I could deliver if we shifted the balance."

**Set a date.** Three months is usually enough to see whether words become action.

**Watch what happens, not what is said.** A budget line, a calendar invitation, a data access request approved — these are evidence. "We are looking into it" repeated twice is also evidence.

Candidates who do this rarely regret it. Either something changes, or you leave with a clear conscience and a much better answer to the inevitable interview question.

## What Dutch Rules Mean for Timing

**Probation period.** Dutch probation periods are short and capped: none in contracts of six months or less, one month for fixed-term contracts under two years, two months for longer or permanent contracts. During probation either side can end the contract immediately. If you are still inside it and already certain, leaving is administratively simple.

**Notice period.** After probation, the statutory notice period for an employee is one month unless a longer period is agreed in the contract or a collective agreement. You cannot simply stop coming.

**Resignation and benefits.** If you resign without new employment, entitlement to unemployment benefits through the **UWV** can be affected. This is why most people line up the next role first.

**Fixed-term contracts.** A fixed-term contract can only be ended early if the contract explicitly allows it; otherwise you may owe compensation.

**Residence permits.** For non-EU professionals, residence depends on sponsored employment. Changing employer requires the new employer to be a recognised sponsor, and gaps have consequences. Check current conditions with the **IND** before resigning.

**Health.** If the situation is affecting your health, speak to the **occupational physician (bedrijfsarts)** rather than resigning impulsively; different rules and protections apply.

## When Leaving Early Is Clearly Right

- **The work is fundamentally different from what was agreed,** and nobody with authority will change it.
- **There is no data and no plan to get any.** A data role without data does not improve with patience.
- **You are being asked to do something you consider unethical or unlawful** — for example, deploying a system you believe is discriminatory, or processing personal data without a proper basis.
- **The organisation is in visible financial trouble** and your role is not core.
- **Your health is suffering.**
- **A genuinely better opportunity appears** and the current role has no realistic path.

## When Staying a Year Is Usually Wiser

- **The work is decent but slower than hoped.** Most AI roles take six months before you are effective.
- **The gap is fixable and someone is trying.** Give the effort time to show.
- **You have not yet delivered anything.** Leaving with nothing to show makes the next search harder.
- **You changed field or country recently.** Adjustment is part of it, and a second short tenure is harder to explain than a first.
- **The only alternative is a lateral move with the same risks.**

## How Short Tenure Actually Reads

One short tenure in an otherwise stable history is unremarkable. Interviewers ask about it and move on, provided your explanation is calm, brief and free of blame.

Two or three in a row create a genuine pattern, and hiring managers start asking whether the common factor is the candidate. This is the strongest argument for making the second decision more carefully than the first.

A workable explanation has three parts: what you were hired to do, what the role turned out to be, and what you did about it before leaving. "I was hired to build forecasting models; after the reorganisation the role became operational reporting. I proposed a split and worked on it for three months, but the priorities did not change, so I decided to move." That is a professional answer, and it demonstrates exactly the judgement employers want.

Avoid criticising individuals, avoid irony, and never suggest that the previous employer was incompetent — interviewers hear that as a preview of how you will describe them.

## Searching While in a New Job

Discretion matters, especially in a small market.

Use personal email and your own devices. Schedule interviews outside working hours or with leave where possible. Be careful about visible activity on professional networks, which colleagues notice. And do not resign until a contract is signed — verbal offers occasionally evaporate.

You also have a normal working relationship to maintain in the meantime. Doing your current job properly until the last day is what produces a usable reference, and in the Dutch AI market you will meet these people again.

## What to Change in Your Next Choice

An early exit is only wasted if you repeat the pattern. Before accepting the next role, ask the questions whose absence caused this one: who reviews my work, which data exists today and can I see a sample, who is the sponsor and what happens if they leave, what is the first project concretely, and what has changed in this team over the last year.

Ask to speak to a future colleague as well as the manager. Ask what happened to the person who held the role before. These conversations cost thirty minutes and catch most of the mismatches that cause first-year exits.

## When the Role Changed Rather Than Being Misdescribed

There is a difference between an employer who oversold a role and one where circumstances changed after you joined — a reorganisation, a lost budget, a departed sponsor. Both leave you doing different work, but the conversation is different.

Employers in the Netherlands can direct the work within reasonable limits, and roles evolve. A substantial unilateral change to the content of your job is a different matter: what your employer may change, and on what grounds, depends on your contract, any collective agreement and whether the change is reasonable in the circumstances. If a large part of your agreed role has disappeared, it is legitimate to say so explicitly and ask what the plan is, and worth taking advice if the answer is unsatisfactory.

Practically, ask three things. What is the expected duration of this situation? What is my role once it resolves? What can I work on in the meantime that keeps my skills current?

Reasonable employers answer concretely, because they know they risk losing you. Employers who treat the question as disloyalty have answered it in a different way.

Document what you agree, briefly and in writing — an email summarising the conversation is enough. If you later leave, that record makes your explanation in interviews precise rather than anecdotal. And if you stay, it gives you a reference point for the follow-up conversation in three months.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Utrecht who left after seven months

A data scientist joins a retailer to build demand forecasting. Two months in, the analytics manager leaves and the team is folded into IT. His work becomes maintaining reports; the forecasting project is postponed indefinitely.

He writes down what would make the job worth keeping: the forecasting project restarted with a named sponsor, or a move to the supply chain team. He proposes both to the new manager and sets himself a three-month deadline. After ten weeks, nothing has moved beyond sympathetic conversations.

He starts searching quietly, finds a role at a logistics company thirty minutes from home, and only resigns once the contract is signed. In interviews he explains the change in two sentences without criticising anyone, and describes the one thing he did deliver: a documented data quality process the reporting team still uses.

Nobody treats the seven months as a problem. Two interviewers say they would have done the same.

## Key Takeaways

- Identify which of the five common causes applies; misrepresentation and missing foundations rarely improve on their own.
- Run a three-month structured attempt to fix it, with a written list of what would need to change and one clear conversation.
- Dutch probation periods are short and capped, the statutory employee notice period is one month, and resigning without a new role can affect benefits and residence permits.
- One short tenure is unremarkable when explained calmly; a pattern of them is what damages a CV.
- Before your next role, ask about review, data, sponsor, first project and team stability — the questions whose absence usually causes early exits.
