The offer includes something extra on arrival: a sign-on payment, relocation costs covered, a certification programme funded, or a retention amount payable after a year. Attached to it, usually in a separate paragraph near the end of the contract, is the condition — if you leave within a defined period, you pay it back. Candidates tend to read the first part carefully and the second part quickly. The second part is the one that determines whether this money is a benefit or a rope, because it decides what happens if the role turns out to be different from what you were told.

This article gives general information rather than legal advice; for your own contract, an hour with a lawyer, your union or your legal expenses insurer is money well spent, and the free **Juridisch Loket** can point you in the right direction.

## What Gets Attached to Money on Arrival

**A sign-on bonus.** Paid on starting or after a probation period, repayable in whole or in part if you leave within a set time.

**Relocation costs.** Moving expenses, temporary housing, sometimes a settling-in amount, frequently with a repayment condition.

**Training and certification.** A course, a programme or a qualification funded by the employer, with a study costs clause attached.

**Retention payments.** Amounts payable at a future date if you are still employed, common around acquisitions, migrations and exits.

**Equipment or allowances** with conditions, which is rarer and does exist.

## The Dutch Rules Worth Knowing

The area that has changed most is training costs. Following the implementation of the EU directive on transparent and predictable working conditions, training that an employer is obliged to provide — because law or an applicable collective agreement requires it for the job — must in principle be provided free of charge to the employee, count as working time where possible, and cannot be made subject to a repayment clause.

Training outside that category may still carry a study costs clause, but Dutch case law has long set conditions on such clauses: the employee should be informed in advance about the costs and the arrangement, the period during which the employer benefits should be defined, and the repayment obligation should reduce over that period rather than remaining at full value until the last day.

Other general points matter too. What may be deducted from your final payment is constrained, an unclear clause is interpreted against the party that wrote it, and an applicable collective agreement may contain its own rules that override what the individual contract says.

The detail changes over time, so verify the current position for your situation rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

## Why Employers Use These Clauses

Mostly for legitimate reasons. Recruitment is expensive, onboarding takes months before someone is productive, and a data professional who leaves after eight months has cost the organisation more than they contributed. Repayment clauses are an attempt to share that risk.

The less legitimate use is as a retention device in roles that are hard to fill or unpleasant to do: money offered at the start, structured so that leaving is expensive, in place of making the job better.

You can usually tell them apart by the length of the lock-in and the willingness to discuss it.

## What to Check in the Clause

- **What triggers repayment?** Resignation only, or any ending of the contract? A clause that bites when the employer terminates, or when a fixed-term contract simply expires, is unreasonable and worth challenging.
- **Is there a sliding scale?** Full repayment on day 364 of a twelve-month clause is a very different obligation from a monthly reduction.
- **Gross or net?** Repaying a gross amount you received net is a significant difference, and the treatment should be specified.
- **How long is the period?** One year is common for sign-on payments; anything beyond two deserves scrutiny.
- **What exactly is covered?** For training, whether it includes course fees only or also travel, materials and working time.
- **How does it interact with notice?** Some clauses are written so that the obligation depends on the date employment ends rather than the date you resign.
- **Is it in the contract or a policy?** Only the contract binds you.
- **What happens on redundancy, illness or a transfer of the business?**

## The Hidden Cost

The real price of these arrangements is not the money. It is that they change your decisions.

A repayment obligation makes leaving expensive precisely when leaving might be right: when the role turns out to be different from what was described, when the manager who hired you leaves, when the project is cancelled. People stay months longer than they should because of amounts that, in retrospect, were not worth the time.

Before accepting, ask yourself what you would do if the job were not what you expected after five months. If the honest answer is "stay because of the clause", the clause is doing something you should be aware of.

## How to Value a Sign-On Payment

**It is one-off.** Your base salary compounds through future percentage increases; a sign-on payment does not.

**It usually does not count for pension or holiday allowance,** which are calculated on fixed salary.

**Lenders generally discount it** when assessing borrowing capacity.

**It is frequently offered in place of a higher base,** because it costs the employer once rather than every year.

The practical rule: a sign-on payment is worth a fraction of the equivalent amount added to your base salary. If you can convert it, convert it. If you cannot, do not let it decide between two offers.

## How to Negotiate

Ask, plainly, whether the payment can be added to the base salary instead. Employers often prefer the one-off cost, and some will say yes, particularly where the alternative is losing the candidate.

If the clause stays, ask for three amendments, each of which is commonly granted:

**A sliding scale,** reducing monthly over the period.

**Trigger limited to your own resignation,** excluding dismissal, redundancy, expiry of a fixed-term contract and termination during any probationary period.

**A cap and a clear calculation,** stated in net terms if you received it net.

And if the employer refuses every amendment while insisting the role is attractive, that combination is itself informative.

## When It Is Fine

- The period is a year or less, with a sliding scale.
- Repayment applies only if you resign.
- The amount is genuinely additional rather than a substitute for base salary.
- You have strong reasons to expect to stay — the work, the team, the location.
- The employer will discuss the terms openly.
- Relocation costs are covered generously and the condition is proportionate.

## When to Refuse or Walk Away

- The clause applies however the employment ends.
- The period is two years or more with no reduction.
- The payment replaces a competitive base salary.
- Mandatory training is being made repayable, which the rules generally do not permit.
- The clause is vague about amounts, triggers or calculation.
- The employer resists any discussion of it at all.

## Retention Payments Are a Different Animal

Sign-on money is paid to get you through the door. Retention money is paid to keep you somewhere you might otherwise leave, and it appears in specific circumstances: an acquisition, a platform migration, a regulatory programme, or the run-up to a company sale.

The structure is usually simple — an amount payable on a future date if you are still employed — and the details decide whether it is worth anything.

**What counts as "still employed".** Employed on the date, or employed and not under notice? Many arrangements exclude anyone who has resigned, which means handing in your notice a week early can cost the entire amount.

**What happens if the employer ends the contract.** A well-drafted arrangement pays out on redundancy or termination by the employer; a poorly drafted one does not, which turns the payment into a one-sided bet.

**Whether it is contractual.** A retention amount described in a conversation is not a retention arrangement. Ask for the document.

**Timing and tax.** These amounts are taxed as income when paid, which can land awkwardly, and the treatment of a payment spanning a transaction is worth checking with an adviser.

**What you are being asked to give up.** A retention payment is compensation for staying through a period you might not otherwise choose — a reorganisation, an integration, a stressful migration. Judge the amount against that, not against your salary.

The negotiation points are ordinary: payment on employer-initiated termination, a clear definition of the qualifying date, and where possible, staged payments rather than a single cliff. Employers who are serious about keeping people through a transition usually agree to all three, because the alternative is losing the person before the date.

## Real example

### An engineer in Eindhoven who asked for a sliding scale

A machine learning engineer receives an offer including a sign-on payment, repayable in full if he leaves within two years, plus a funded certification with its own repayment condition.

He asks first whether the sign-on amount can go into his base salary instead. The answer is no — it comes from a recruitment budget rather than the salary budget, which is a common and honest reason.

So he asks for three changes. The repayment reduces monthly over the period. It applies only if he resigns, not if the company terminates or restructures. And the period drops from two years to one.

The employer agrees to the sliding scale and the trigger limitation, and holds at eighteen months rather than one year. He accepts.

Fourteen months later his manager leaves and the team is reorganised. He considers moving, calculates the remaining obligation under the sliding scale, and finds it small enough not to influence the decision.

He stays, in the end, for reasons that have nothing to do with the clause — which is exactly the position he had negotiated for.

## Key Takeaways

- Sign-on payments, relocation cover, funded training and retention amounts frequently carry repayment conditions that determine how free you are to leave.
- Training the employer is obliged to provide must in principle be free and cannot generally be made repayable; other study costs clauses must meet conditions including a reducing obligation over a defined period.
- Check the trigger, the sliding scale, gross or net, the length of the period, what is covered and what happens on redundancy or illness.
- A one-off payment is worth far less than the same amount on your base salary, which compounds and counts for pension, allowances and borrowing.
- Negotiate a sliding scale, a trigger limited to your own resignation and a clear calculation — all three are commonly granted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a sign-on payment) Is a repayment clause normal in the Netherlands?
For sign-on payments, relocation costs and some training, yes. What varies is the length, the trigger and whether the obligation reduces over time — and those are negotiable.

### (Scenario: candidate offered funded training) Can my employer make me repay training costs?
Training the employer is obliged to provide must in principle be free and not repayable. Other training may carry a clause, subject to conditions including advance information and a reducing obligation. Verify the current rules for your case.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing offers) How much is a sign-on bonus really worth?
Less than the same amount on your base salary. It does not compound, usually does not count for pension or holiday allowance, and is typically discounted by mortgage lenders.

### (Scenario: candidate reviewing a contract) What should I try to change?
A sliding scale that reduces monthly, a trigger limited to your own resignation, and a clear calculation stated in the same terms as the payment you received.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about being locked in) What is the real risk?
That leaving becomes expensive exactly when leaving becomes right — after a reorganisation, a cancelled project or a manager's departure. Decide in advance what you would do in that situation.
