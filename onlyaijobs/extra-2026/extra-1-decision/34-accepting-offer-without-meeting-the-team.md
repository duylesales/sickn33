Three video calls: a recruiter, a hiring manager, a technical conversation with someone from another department. Then an offer, with a start date, and a friendly note saying the team is looking forward to meeting you. Except you have not met them. Fast, remote-first processes have made this common, and for employers competing for scarce candidates the speed is a feature. For you it removes the single most reliable predictor of whether you will still be happy in a year — not the salary, not the technology stack, but the people you will sit with and the person who decides what you work on. Accepting without that information is sometimes fine. It should at least be a decision.

## Why It Matters More in Data Roles Than Most

**Your work depends on other people's cooperation.** Data access, domain knowledge and the willingness of a business unit to use your output are what determine whether you succeed. Those are relationships, not technologies.

**The team reveals the data reality.** A manager describes ambitions; a colleague describes how long it actually takes to get a new data source, and whether the last model reached production.

**Review culture is invisible from outside.** Whether anyone reads your code, whether disagreement is welcome, whether mistakes are examined or hidden — none of this appears in a job description.

**Data teams are small.** In a team of four, one difficult relationship is a quarter of your working life. In a department of forty, it is an annoyance.

## Why Employers Skip It

Not always carelessness. Common reasons include a genuinely fast process designed to compete for scarce candidates, distributed teams where assembling people costs real coordination, a manager who considers it their decision alone, a role being filled urgently, or an intermediary who compresses the process to close quickly.

Occasionally the reason is worse: a team in conflict, a role created over the objections of the people who would work with you, or a departure the employer would rather you did not ask about. You cannot distinguish these from outside — which is precisely the argument for asking.

## The Request Is Normal and Usually Granted

Asking to speak with a future colleague before accepting is a standard, professional request in the Netherlands. Most employers agree readily; many are pleased, because a candidate who wants to check the team is a candidate taking the decision seriously.

Phrase it simply and without suspicion: *"Before I decide, could I have a short conversation with one or two people I would work with day to day? Half an hour is plenty."* Offer to do it in the evening if scheduling is the obstacle.

Ask for a peer rather than another manager, and ideally someone who joined in the last year or two — recent joiners remember what surprised them, which is exactly the information you want.

## What to Ask in That Conversation

Thirty minutes is enough for five questions, if they are the right ones.

**What surprised you in your first three months?** The single most productive question; nobody has a prepared answer.

**How long does it take to get access to a new data source here?** A concrete proxy for data maturity and organisational friction.

**What happened to the last project that did not work?** Tells you whether failure is examined or buried.

**How are decisions about priorities actually made?** Reveals whether the roadmap in your interview is real.

**What would you change if you could?** Everyone has an answer; the interesting part is whether they feel safe giving it.

Listen for hesitation as much as content. A colleague who answers cheerfully and specifically is telling you something different from one who pauses and chooses careful words.

## When Accepting Without It Is Reasonable

- **You have strong independent signals:** people you trust who work there, a well-known engineering culture, former colleagues who joined and stayed.
- **The team is genuinely distributed** and everyone would have been a video call anyway.
- **The role is short-term or interim,** where the commitment is smaller in both directions.
- **You met the manager properly,** at length and more than once, and they were candid about problems as well as plans.
- **The alternative is losing the offer** and your current situation is worse than the risk.

## When to Insist

- **You would be the first or only data person.** Then the surrounding colleagues are your working environment, and you need to meet at least one.
- **The process was unusually short.** Two conversations and an offer is fast even by 2026 standards.
- **Something felt evasive.** A question about team stability that produced a vague answer deserves follow-up with someone else.
- **You are leaving a stable role.** The bigger the thing you are giving up, the more diligence is warranted.
- **The team has recently changed.** A departed predecessor, a reorganisation or a new manager are all reasons to hear a second voice.

## What a Refusal Tells You

Employers who refuse politely and explain — a team abroad on a deadline, a colleague on leave, a genuinely compressed timeline — are usually being honest, and an alternative such as a short call after acceptance but before your start date is a reasonable compromise.

Employers who refuse without explanation, or who treat the request as a lack of trust, have given you information that no interview would have. In a process where both sides are choosing, a candidate asking to meet the team is doing exactly what the employer did when they asked for references.

## If You Accept Without Meeting Them Anyway

Reduce the risk in three ways.

**Use the probation period consciously.** Dutch probation periods are short and legally capped, which makes them a mutual safety valve. Spend the first weeks deliberately: talk to people, ask the five questions above, and notice whether what you were told matches what you find.

**Get the scope in writing.** A short annex describing the first project and who you work with converts vague promises into something identifiable if it changes.

**Arrange a conversation before your start date.** Even a half-hour informal call two weeks before you begin changes your first day, and employers almost never refuse once you have accepted.

**Keep your network warm for six months.** Not out of pessimism — simply because the cost of discovering a mismatch is lower when you have not gone quiet on everyone.

## The Reverse Signal: Employers Who Insist You Meet Everyone

The opposite pattern exists and is worth recognising as positive. Some employers build a team conversation into every process, occasionally with a veto for existing team members. This slows hiring and consistently produces better matches, because the people who will work with you have said yes.

If you encounter it, treat the extra round as a benefit rather than an obstacle. A team that was consulted about your hire behaves differently in your first month than one that was informed of it.

## Finding the Team Yourself

If the employer cannot or will not arrange a conversation, you are not out of options. The information exists outside their process.

**Your own network first.** Someone you know probably knows someone there, particularly in a market this size. A two-line message — "you worked with X, I have an offer from them, would you tell me honestly what the team is like?" — is normal and usually answered.

**People who left.** Former employees are often the most candid source, and they have no stake in the outcome. Ask what they would want to know if they were in your position.

**Public traces.** Conference talks, engineering blog posts, open-source contributions and community activity tell you whether the team engages with the field and how they describe their own work.

**Regional communities.** Meetups and sector events in your area are where people speak freely about employers, and a single conversation at one can be worth more than an interview round.

Two cautions. Any individual account is one perspective shaped by their team and manager, so weigh several rather than acting on one. And do not contact current employees through channels that would expose your application to their management if discretion matters — a public message to someone's work account can travel further than you intend.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Breda who asked after the offer

A data scientist receives an offer from a logistics company after three video conversations: recruiter, hiring manager, and a technical interview with an architect from another team. The package is good and the commute is twenty minutes.

She asks for thirty minutes with someone she would work with. The recruiter arranges a call with an analyst who joined eight months earlier.

Two things emerge. The team has had three managers in two years, and the current one — her future manager — is widely liked but new. And getting access to a new data source takes about six weeks because of an external IT provider, something nobody had mentioned.

Neither is disqualifying, and both change her decision-making. She accepts, but she negotiates one addition: the first project scoped in writing, with the data access dependency named explicitly. In her first month, when access takes seven weeks, nobody is surprised and nobody blames her.

## What to Watch in Your First Weeks Instead

If you accept without meeting the team, the probation period becomes your evaluation window. Use it deliberately rather than hoping for the best.

**Watch how disagreement happens.** In the first meetings, notice whether someone junior contradicts someone senior and what follows. That single observation tells you more about the culture than any onboarding document.

**Ask each colleague the same question.** "What surprised you when you joined?" Asked to five people in week one, it produces a map of the organisation's blind spots.

**Track how long your first request takes.** Access to a system, a dataset, a meeting with a stakeholder. The elapsed time is the organisation's real velocity, independent of what anyone promised.

**Notice whether your manager's description matches reality.** Not as an accusation — descriptions drift honestly — but as a calibration of how much weight future statements deserve.

**Check whether the sponsor exists.** Someone should want your output. If you cannot identify that person by week three, raise it while you still have the standing of a new joiner.

Write these observations down. Four weeks in, memory has already begun to normalise things that would have struck you on day one, and the decision about whether this is the right place is easier with notes than with impressions.

## Key Takeaways

- Team and manager are the strongest predictors of whether you stay, and short remote processes routinely leave them untested.
- Asking to speak with a future colleague before deciding is normal in the Netherlands and usually granted.
- Ask a recent joiner what surprised them, how long data access takes, what happened to the last failed project and how priorities are set.
- Insist when you would be the only data person, when the process was unusually short, when something felt evasive or when you are leaving a stable role.
- If you accept without meeting them, arrange a call before your start date, get the scope in writing and use the probation period deliberately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate holding an offer) Is it acceptable to ask to meet the team before accepting?
Yes — it is a normal, professional request in the Netherlands and most employers arrange it quickly, often within a day or two.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure who to ask for) Who should I ask to speak with?
A peer rather than another manager, ideally someone who joined in the last year or two and still remembers what surprised them.

### (Scenario: candidate refused a conversation) What if the employer says no?
A clear reason and an alternative is usually honest. A refusal without explanation, or one treated as distrust, is itself a signal worth weighing.

### (Scenario: candidate who already accepted) I accepted without meeting anyone. What now?
Arrange an informal call before your start date, get the first project scoped in writing and use the probation period to check what you were told.

### (Scenario: candidate in a fast process) Should a very short hiring process worry me?
Not automatically, but two conversations and an offer is fast. Use the time you saved to ask the questions the process skipped.
