At the end of a search you are rarely choosing between a good option and a bad one. You are choosing between two or three reasonable roles, each better on something, with incomplete information about all of them. That is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is what makes people default to the highest salary or the most familiar-sounding employer. There is a better way to close this out, and it takes an evening rather than a fortnight. What follows is a way of deciding that survives contact with the actual situation: several factors, weighted honestly, a few things verified rather than assumed, and a clear moment of commitment.

## Start by Naming What You Are Solving For

No role is best on everything, so decide what this particular move is for before you compare anything. Most moves are primarily about one or two of these:

**Learning.** You want to become better at something specific and identifiable.

**Scope.** You want ownership, a bigger surface, or the chance to lead.

**Conditions.** Distance, hours, predictability, the shape of your week.

**Security.** A stable employer, a permanent contract, protection during a demanding period of life.

**Money.** A correction, a step up, or a specific financial goal.

**Direction.** A deliberate change of field, sector or specialism.

Rank your top three. A role that scores well on your top two is almost always the right choice, even when another scores higher overall on a spreadsheet.

## The Factors People Underrate

**Commute.** The most consistently underestimated factor in job satisfaction. An extra thirty-five minutes each way is roughly a working day every two weeks, taken from your life, in all weather, for years. People adapt to salary differences quickly and never adapt to a long daily journey.

**Your direct manager.** More predictive of your experience than the company, the technology or the domain. You are choosing a manager as much as an employer.

**Whether anything reaches production.** An organisation that ships, in any technology, is a fundamentally different environment from one that does not.

**Who reviews your work.** Growth depends on it, and its absence is invisible until a later interview exposes the gap.

**Terms beyond salary.** Pension accrual, training budget and time, leave, allowances and working-time arrangements. Over a few years these frequently outweigh a modest salary difference.

## The Factors People Overrate

**The technology stack.** Tools change, and every employer's stack will look different in three years. The engineering culture around the stack matters; the names do not.

**Brand.** A recognisable employer helps on a CV and does nothing for your daily work. Small Dutch employers regularly offer more interesting data problems than famous ones.

**Title.** Titles are wildly inconsistent between organisations. Scope is what you will be asked about later.

**The interview atmosphere.** Everyone is on their best behaviour. A pleasant process is weak evidence about a workplace.

**Marginal salary differences.** Once the offers are within a modest range of each other, the difference is rarely what determines whether you are pleased with the decision a year later.

## Verify Three Things Before You Decide

Assumptions are what produce regret. Three checks are worth the small awkwardness of asking.

**Speak to a future colleague without a manager present.** Ask what they would change, what surprised them and what the last difficult period was like. Employers who arrange this readily are telling you something; those who refuse are telling you more.

**Ask what happened to the person who held the role before.** Promoted internally, left the organisation, still there in a different position — each answer means something.

**Confirm the terms in writing.** Salary, contract type and duration, hours, holiday, pension, training budget, home working and travel arrangements, on-call expectations, and any specific commitment you negotiated. With pay transparency rules increasingly shaping how employers communicate ranges, written clarity is easier to obtain than it used to be — and a term that exists only in a conversation does not exist.

## A Simple Way to Compare

Take a sheet of paper and list your top three priorities with a weight for each. Score each offer on those, plus commute, manager, terms and the production question. Do not agonise over the numbers; the point is not arithmetic precision but forcing yourself to be explicit.

Then look at the winner and notice your reaction. If you feel relief, you have your answer. If you immediately start looking for reasons the other one should have won, that reaction is information the scoring missed — usually about something you did not want to write down, such as the manager or the domain.

Use the sheet to find your true preference, not to overrule it.

## The Questions That Cut Through

When the comparison is close, four questions usually resolve it.

**Which role would I regret not taking?** Regret asymmetry is a better guide than expected value at this level of uncertainty.

**Where will I be more employable in three years?** Which role produces a story with a system, a result and a decision behind it.

**Which manager would I rather have on a bad week?** Not a good week.

**If both offers were identical in pay, which would I take?** This one settles a surprising number of decisions, because it removes the factor people use to justify choices they are unsure about.

## Commit Properly

Once you decide, decide. Tell the employer promptly and warmly. Decline the other offer with genuine thanks and no false reasons, because the Dutch AI market is small and hiring managers move; the person you decline this year may be interviewing you in four.

Then stop comparing. The alternative you did not take becomes an idealised version of itself the moment you turn it down, and continuing to weigh it against a real job with real bad weeks is a reliable way to be dissatisfied with a good decision.

Give the new role a fair run — a year, unless something is genuinely wrong. The first months of any job are the worst months of that job.

It is worth being specific about what "genuinely wrong" means, because the distinction saves people from both leaving too early and staying too long. Discovering that the work is different from what was described, that a promised commitment is not being honoured, or that the environment is one you cannot professionally accept are real problems, and they are worth raising immediately rather than enduring. Finding the first months hard, the systems confusing, the colleagues unfamiliar and your own contribution unclear is not a problem; it is what starting a job feels like, for everyone, including the people around you who look settled. Almost nobody feels competent in a new role before month four, and the feeling arrives later for people joining a complicated domain.

## When to Take Neither

Sometimes the right answer is to stay, or to keep looking. That is worth saying explicitly, because a search builds momentum of its own.

Decline both when neither offer is better than your current situation on the things you ranked; when the only appeal is escape rather than direction; when the terms would not be honoured — evasiveness during negotiation predicts the rest of the relationship; or when you have discovered during the process that what you actually want is something neither employer offers.

A search that ends in staying, with a clearer picture of the market and of what you want, is not a failed search.

## Deciding With the People Affected

A job decision is rarely only yours, and the parts that involve other people are the ones most likely to be skipped under time pressure.

**Map the week honestly with your household.** Not the salary — the schedule. Which days you would be away and for how long, who does the school run, what a departure at seven and a return at nineteen hundred does to the evenings, and what happens when one of you has an early meeting. Draw the actual week rather than describing it in general terms; the disagreements surface immediately and are much cheaper to resolve now.

**Be explicit about what changes for the other person.** A role with occasional travel, evening calls or on-call duty transfers work to whoever is at home. That is a legitimate trade to make and a poor one to make silently.

**If relocation is involved, treat it as two decisions.** The job and the move are separate, and a partner's work, a school year and a housing market each have their own timelines. Sequencing them deliberately avoids the common outcome where a good job is accepted and everything else is arranged in a rush around it.

**Get one outside perspective.** A former colleague or a mentor who knows your work, not only friends who know your mood. Ask them what they would worry about in each option. People close to the situation often name the thing you have been avoiding.

**Notice whose voice is loudest for the wrong reason.** Enthusiasm from people impressed by a brand name, or caution from people who dislike change generally, is not advice about your situation.

**Then decide yourself.** Consultation is not a vote. You are the one who will do the work, and a decision made to satisfy several people at once tends to satisfy nobody, including you.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Zwolle who chose the smaller offer

A data scientist reaches the end of a search with two offers. One from a well-known employer in Amsterdam, higher pay, an hour and twenty minutes each way, a large team and a role focused on one part of the pipeline. One from a regional manufacturer twenty minutes from home, lower pay, a team of four, end-to-end ownership of quality prediction.

Her ranked priorities are learning, scope and conditions. On paper the offers are close, because the salary difference pulls the Amsterdam role up.

Two checks decide it. She speaks to a future colleague at the manufacturer, who describes two models running in production and a manager who protects the team's time. She asks the Amsterdam employer what happened to her predecessor and learns that the role has been filled three times in four years.

She takes the smaller offer. Three years later she has built two production systems, she has an hour and a half of her life back every working day, and the pay difference has largely closed.

Her summary is unglamorous and accurate: she chose the manager and the commute, and everything she hoped the bigger employer would give her came from the work she was actually allowed to do.

## Key Takeaways

- Decide what this move is for and rank your top three priorities before comparing anything.
- Commute, your direct manager, whether anything reaches production, who reviews your work and the terms beyond salary are consistently underrated.
- Technology stack, brand, title, interview atmosphere and marginal salary differences are consistently overrated.
- Verify three things: a conversation with a future colleague, what happened to your predecessor, and every term in writing.
- Commit properly, decline the other offer gracefully, stop comparing, and give the new role a fair year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two offers) How should I weigh salary against everything else?
Rank what the move is for first. Once offers are within a modest range, salary rarely determines satisfaction a year later, while commute, manager and development consistently do.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure about a company) What should I verify before accepting?
Speak to a future colleague without a manager present, ask what happened to your predecessor, and get every agreed term in writing.

### (Scenario: candidate overthinking) What if the two options seem equal?
Ask which you would regret not taking, which makes you more employable in three years, which manager you would want on a bad week, and which you would choose if pay were identical.

### (Scenario: candidate with doubts after deciding) How do I stop second-guessing myself?
Decline the other offer cleanly and stop comparing. The road not taken becomes idealised, while the job you took has real bad weeks. Give it a fair year.

### (Scenario: candidate unconvinced by both) Is it acceptable to turn both down?
Yes. If neither beats your current situation on what you ranked, or if the appeal is only escape, declining both and continuing deliberately is a legitimate outcome.
