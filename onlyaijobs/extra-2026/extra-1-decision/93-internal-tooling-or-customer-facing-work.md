Inside the same organisation, two data roles can look almost identical on paper and produce completely different careers. One builds what other teams use: the platform, the pipelines, the feature store, the deployment tooling, the internal applications that let analysts work without asking an engineer. The other builds what customers touch: the recommendation, the pricing model, the risk score, the assistant in the app. Candidates usually drift towards whichever is offered rather than choosing, and the choice deserves more attention than it gets, because the two differ in budget security, visibility and what they make you good at.

## What Internal Tooling Actually Covers

**Data platform.** Ingestion, storage, transformation, access, orchestration. The foundation everything else stands on.

**MLOps and deployment tooling.** Training pipelines, model registries, serving infrastructure, monitoring, rollback.

**Self-service capability.** Making it possible for analysts and business users to answer their own questions without a ticket.

**Internal applications.** Tools that let a planner, an underwriter or a technician use a model's output in their actual workflow.

**Governance infrastructure.** Catalogues, lineage, access control, retention — increasingly demanded by regulation rather than by preference.

## Why Candidates Undervalue It

Internal work has no story. Customer-facing models can be described as improving conversion or reducing churn; a platform is described as "we rebuilt the ingestion layer", which sounds like housekeeping even when it unblocked every other team in the company.

There is also a status assumption that internal means less demanding. In most organisations the opposite is true: platform work has more users, more failure modes, more compatibility constraints and far less tolerance for downtime than a model that runs weekly.

## What Internal Work Builds

**Serious engineering.** Systems with many consumers force testing, versioning, backwards compatibility and operational discipline.

**Scale.** You touch every data source in the organisation and understand the whole estate within a year.

**Leverage.** Improving a pipeline improves the work of everyone downstream, permanently.

**In-demand skills.** Data platform and MLOps capability is among the most portable and most sought-after profiles in the Dutch market, across every sector.

**Breadth of stakeholders,** since your users are colleagues with opinions and deadlines.

## What Customer-Facing Work Builds

**Revenue proximity.** You learn what the organisation actually sells and how your work moves a number the board watches.

**Commercial judgement.** Trade-offs between accuracy, latency, cost and user experience become concrete.

**Visible impact,** which shortens the distance between your work and recognition.

**Product thinking,** including the discipline of measuring whether anyone uses what you built.

**Experimentation practice,** where A/B testing and measurement are part of the job rather than an aspiration.

## The Visibility Problem

This is the real asymmetry. Customer-facing work is measured in metrics executives already track. Internal work is measured in the absence of problems, which nobody tracks.

The consequence appears at review time and at reorganisation time. Teams that cannot express their value in business terms are the ones asked to justify themselves first.

If you take internal work, make measurement part of it from the start: time from idea to production before and after, incidents avoided, hours returned to analysts, cost reduced, onboarding time for a new data source. Numbers turn a platform from a cost into an investment in the only language budget holders use.

## The Budget Problem

Internal tooling is usually funded as a cost, and customer-facing work as a contribution to revenue. In a cost programme, that distinction decides what gets cut.

This is not an argument against internal roles — platform teams in organisations that depend on them are extremely secure — but it is an argument for asking where the funding comes from, who sponsors it and what happened to the team during the last cost exercise.

## Questions to Ask

- **Who are the users, and do they want this?** Internal platforms built without consulting their users are the most common failure in this area.
- **How is the team's value measured?**
- **Where does the budget come from, and who defends it?**
- **What happened in the last cost programme?**
- **Is there a product owner for the platform,** or does it serve whoever shouts loudest?
- **What proportion of the work is support and firefighting?**
- **For customer-facing roles: how is impact attributed,** and has a model been credited for a business result here before?

## Which Suits Which Stage

**Early career.** Internal platform work teaches engineering discipline quickly and in a forgiving setting, and the skills transfer everywhere.

**Mid career.** Customer-facing work adds the commercial dimension that turns a competent engineer into someone who chooses the right problems.

**Senior.** Either, and the strongest profiles have done both: platform experience makes you credible with engineering, product experience makes you credible with the business.

**When you want security.** A platform that everyone depends on is a safer place to be than a model whose contribution is disputed — provided the dependency is recognised.

## When to Choose Internal

- You want deep engineering and platform skills that transfer across sectors.
- The organisation genuinely depends on the platform and knows it.
- There is a sponsor and a defined set of internal users.
- You prefer building systems to negotiating about metrics.
- The team measures and communicates its value.

## When to Choose Customer-Facing

- You want to understand how the business makes money.
- You need visible achievements for the next step.
- The organisation can attribute results to models credibly.
- You enjoy experimentation and measurement.
- You have platform experience already and want the other half.

## How Internal Platforms Actually Fail

If you take internal work, it helps to know the failure modes, because they are consistent across organisations and largely avoidable.

**Built without users.** A team designs the platform it would want, launches it, and discovers that the analysts continue exporting to spreadsheets because the new tool does not fit how they actually work. The remedy is unglamorous: sit with three users for a day before designing anything.

**Adoption assumed rather than earned.** Internal tools have captive users in theory and competitors in practice — the old process, the spreadsheet, the direct database connection someone still has. A platform nobody switches to is a cost with no benefit.

**Too general, too early.** Building for every future case produces something that serves no present one. Platforms that succeed usually start by solving one team's problem completely and then generalising.

**No product ownership.** Without someone deciding what the platform is for, it becomes a request queue, and request queues have no architecture.

**Migration never finishes.** The new platform runs alongside the old one indefinitely, doubling maintenance and halving the benefit. Decommissioning is the deliverable, not the launch.

**Invisible success.** Nothing breaks, so nobody notices, and the team is reduced in the next cost exercise.

## What to Build in Your First Year

If you join an internal team, three things produce disproportionate returns.

**Make something faster that people do daily.** Not the grand platform: the twenty-minute wait that six analysts hit every morning. It buys the goodwill that funds everything else.

**Instrument your own platform.** Usage, failures, time-to-value for a new data source. You cannot argue for a platform whose use you cannot describe.

**Retire something.** Every legacy pipeline or report you remove is permanent cost reduction, and it is the easiest thing to explain to a budget holder.

Do those three and the visibility problem largely solves itself, because you will have numbers, users who advocate for you and a line in the cost report that moved in the right direction.

## The Hybrid That Often Beats Both

There is a third arrangement worth looking for, and it is increasingly common in Dutch organisations: platform work embedded in a product or business team rather than in a central function.

You build the pipelines, tooling and deployment capability for one domain — claims, planning, energy trading, patient flow — sitting with the people who use the output. In practice this combines most of the advantages of each option.

**You keep the engineering depth,** because you are still building infrastructure rather than dashboards.

**You gain the visibility,** because your work is attached to a business outcome that somebody reports on.

**Adoption is not a separate problem,** since the users are in the room.

**Your budget usually sits with the business unit,** which makes it easier to defend than a central cost line.

The trade-off is reach: you solve one domain's problems well rather than the organisation's problems partially, and you may duplicate what another unit built. Some organisations manage that with a small central team setting standards and embedded engineers doing the work, which is the arrangement most people find satisfying.

When comparing offers, ask where the role sits and who the users are. An embedded platform role is frequently advertised with the same wording as a central one and is a different job.

## Real example

### An engineer in Groningen who made the platform measurable

An engineer joins an insurer's data platform team, building ingestion and deployment tooling for six analytics teams. The work is technically strong and, after a year, invisible: nobody outside the team can say what it does.

During a cost review, the platform team is asked to justify its size. Nobody has numbers.

He spends two weeks producing them: time from a new data source being requested to being available, before and after their work; the number of pipeline failures per month over two years; hours the analytics teams previously spent on manual extracts; the cost of the infrastructure retired.

The figures are unspectacular individually and overwhelming together. The team is not cut, and the following year it receives budget for two additional engineers.

He also changes how the team works: every quarter, one page of the same numbers goes to the director.

Three years later he is approached for a lead platform role at a larger employer, and the thing they ask about most is not the architecture — it is how he made an invisible team's contribution legible.

## Key Takeaways

- Internal tooling and customer-facing work look similar on paper and differ in visibility, budget security and the skills they build.
- Internal work builds platform and MLOps capability, engineering discipline and leverage; customer-facing work builds commercial judgement, experimentation practice and visible results.
- The asymmetry is measurement: customer-facing work is counted in metrics the board watches, internal work is counted in problems that did not happen.
- If you take internal work, measure it from the start — time to production, incidents avoided, hours returned, costs retired — and report it quarterly.
- Ask who the users are, who sponsors the budget and what happened to the team in the last cost programme.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two internal offers) Is platform work worse for my career than customer-facing work?
No. Platform and MLOps skills are among the most portable in the market. The disadvantage is visibility, which you can fix by measuring and reporting the team's impact.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about cuts) Which is more secure in a cost programme?
Whichever can demonstrate value. Internal teams are more exposed by default because their contribution is invisible, so ask what happened during the last cost exercise.

### (Scenario: candidate choosing early in a career) Which should I do first?
Internal platform work teaches engineering discipline quickly and transfers everywhere. Customer-facing work adds commercial judgement, which matters more from mid-career.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing an internal role) What is the main failure mode?
A platform built without its users. Ask who the users are, whether they asked for it and whether anyone owns the platform as a product.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting both) Is it worth doing each at some point?
Yes. Platform experience makes you credible with engineering and product experience makes you credible with the business, and senior roles increasingly expect both.
