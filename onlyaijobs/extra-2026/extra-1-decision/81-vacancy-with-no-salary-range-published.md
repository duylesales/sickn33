The role looks interesting, the description is detailed, the team sounds capable, and where the salary should be there is a sentence about a competitive package depending on experience. You have a choice: invest three conversations and find out, or ask now and risk seeming to care mainly about money. Candidates overwhelmingly choose the first, spend weeks on processes that were never going to work financially, and then feel obliged to negotiate hard at the end because they have sunk so much into it. There is a better sequence, and the ground underneath this question has shifted in candidates' favour.

## Why Ranges Are Becoming Normal

The **EU Pay Transparency Directive** obliges member states to introduce rules that, among other things, give job applicants information about the initial pay or pay range for the position before the interview stage, and restrict employers from asking applicants about their pay history. It also strengthens rights for employees to ask for pay information and obliges larger employers to report on pay gaps.

Implementation happens through national law, and the detail and timing of the Dutch rules are what determine exactly what an employer must do and when. Verify the current position rather than relying on a summary, including this one. What is already visible in practice is that many Dutch employers have begun publishing ranges in vacancies, and the ones that have not are increasingly the exception rather than the norm.

That matters for your decision, because an absent range is no longer simply "how it is done here".

## What an Absent Range Usually Means

**A collective agreement applies and the employer assumes you know it.** Common in the public sector, healthcare, education and large institutions. The scale is public and the salary question is largely settled before you apply.

**The scale is internal but real.** Larger private employers often grade roles carefully and simply have not updated their vacancy template.

**They want to see your expectation first.** The oldest reason, and the one that costs candidates money. If you name a number below the budget, that becomes the offer.

**The range is genuinely wide,** because the role could be filled at two different levels and they will decide based on who applies.

**There is no structure at all.** Small employers who pay whatever each negotiation produces. This is worth knowing early, because it means pay differences within the team are arbitrary and your negotiation is the only protection you have.

**An agency is in between,** and the range exists but the intermediary would rather discuss it by phone.

Only the third and fifth are concerning, and you can distinguish them with one question.

## Ask Early, Not Late

The efficient sequence is to establish the range before the second conversation — ideally in the first message or the first call. This is not impolite. It respects both sides' time, and employers who have thought about pay answer it easily.

A workable formulation: "Before we go further, could you tell me the salary range and scale for this role? I want to make sure we are in the same territory before either of us invests more time."

Ask in writing where possible, so the answer is recorded. If the recruiter deflects to "it depends on experience", ask for the range for someone with your experience, which converts a non-answer into an answer.

## If They Ask Your Current Salary First

Under the directive's approach, employers are not to ask applicants about their pay history, and the Dutch implementation determines how that works in practice. Whatever the precise legal position at the time you are asked, you are not obliged to volunteer it, and the professional way to decline is to redirect rather than refuse.

"I would rather not anchor on my current salary — it reflects a different role. What is the range for this position?" That sentence is used constantly and is not held against candidates by employers worth working for.

If pressed for an expectation, give a range based on the market rather than on your history, and say what it is based on: the level, the scope, comparable published ranges. An employer who insists on a number before disclosing theirs is telling you how the negotiation will go.

## How to Judge the Answer

**The width of the range.** A narrow band suggests a structured employer. A very wide one usually means the role is undefined, and you should ask what distinguishes the bottom from the top.

**Where you would sit and why.** Ask directly. Being placed at the bottom of a range with vague progression is a slow problem: future increases are percentages of a lower base.

**What moves you up.** A structured answer describes criteria. An unstructured one describes goodwill.

**Whether a collective agreement applies,** in which case the scale is the scale and the negotiation is about placement within it rather than about the number.

**Whether the range includes elements that are not fixed.** A figure that only works with a target bonus attached is not the same offer as fixed salary.

## Red Flags

- Refusal to give any indication before a final interview.
- A range that changes between conversations.
- Pressure to name your expectation first, repeatedly.
- "We pay above market" without a number attached.
- An offer that arrives below the range you were told.
- Discomfort at the question itself, which tells you how internal pay conversations go.

## When to Walk Away Early

- The range is clearly below what the role requires and there is no flexibility.
- Nobody will give an indication after two direct requests.
- The range only works if you count uncertain variable pay.
- The employer's answer suggests placement will depend on how hard you negotiate rather than on the role.

Walking away at this stage costs you one email. Walking away after four rounds and an offer costs you weeks and a relationship.

## When to Continue Without a Number

There are legitimate reasons to keep going.

- A collective agreement applies and you can look the scale up yourself.
- The employer names the scale rather than the amount, which is effectively the same information in structured organisations.
- The role is unusual enough that the level genuinely has not been set, and the employer says so honestly.
- You want the conversation for other reasons — the domain, the team, the learning — and are clear with yourself about what you would accept.

## What This Tells You About the Employer

Pay practice is a reasonable proxy for organisational maturity. An employer with defined scales, published ranges and a clear answer about progression is usually one where other things are also defined: roles, expectations, review processes.

An employer whose pay depends on individual negotiation tends to have unexplained differences between colleagues, which has consequences beyond your own salary. Transparency obligations are gradually making those differences visible and harder to defend, and employers who have prepared for that are typically the ones already publishing ranges.

## What to Do When the Range Comes Back Too Low

Getting an answer is progress even when the answer disappoints, and there are several moves left before walking away.

**Ask what would justify the top of the range.** The reply tells you whether placement is criteria-based or arbitrary, and occasionally reveals that you already meet the criteria.

**Ask whether the role could be graded differently.** In organisations with job families, a role can sometimes be scoped at a higher level — more ownership, a lead component, responsibility for a system — which moves it into another scale. This is a conversation about the job rather than about your salary, and employers engage with it more readily.

**Ask what else is available.** A training budget, a compressed week, travel arrangements, an earlier review date, additional leave. Where the scale is fixed by a collective agreement, these are the only levers that exist, and they are real.

**Ask when the next review is and on what basis.** An employer who will commit in writing to a review at six months with defined criteria is offering something concrete; one who says "we will look at it" is not.

**Say what you need, once, and mean it.** A clear, specific figure with a short reason — the level, the scope, what comparable roles advertise — is more effective than a negotiation stance, and it lets the employer either solve it or say no.

**Then decline warmly if it does not move.** Say that the work interests you and the level does not work, and leave the door open. Budgets change, and employers frequently return months later when a role is regraded — which happens more often than candidates expect, particularly now that pay ranges are becoming visible and internal inequities harder to sustain.

## Real example

### An engineer in Zwolle who asked in the first message

An ML engineer sees a role at a manufacturer with no salary indication. Instead of applying through the form, he replies to the recruiter with two sentences: he is interested, and could they share the range and scale before scheduling anything.

The recruiter comes back within a day with a range and an explanation: the role is graded in the company's own structure, the range covers two levels, and where a candidate lands depends on whether they have led projects.

It is slightly below what he hoped, and the top of the range is workable. He goes ahead, knowing exactly what he is negotiating for.

In parallel he had applied to a second employer that declined to give any indication until the final stage. He withdrew after the second interview, when the recruiter again deflected. He later heard from another candidate that the offer at that employer came in well below the level advertised.

His conclusion: one question at the start saved him a month, and asking it did not harm the process that mattered.

## Key Takeaways

- Pay transparency rules oblige member states to give applicants pay information before the interview stage and restrict questions about pay history; the Dutch implementation determines the detail, so verify the current position.
- An absent range usually means a collective agreement, an outdated template, a wide undefined role, no pay structure at all, or a desire to hear your number first.
- Ask before the second conversation, in writing, and convert "it depends on experience" into a range for someone with your experience.
- Judge the width of the range, where you would sit, what moves you up, and whether the figure depends on variable pay.
- Walk away early when two direct requests produce nothing — it costs an email now and weeks later.
