The vacancy is enthusiastic: build assistants, explore language models, prototype new applications, work with the latest tooling. The interview confirms it — the organisation wants to "do something with generative AI" and has budget. What is less clear, and what determines whether this role advances your career or stalls it, is whether anything you build will ever be used by anyone. A significant number of generative AI roles created in the past two years have consisted of impressive demonstrations that never reached production, in organisations that had not decided what they actually wanted. The work is genuinely interesting. The question is whether it accumulates into anything.

## Why So Many of These Roles Exist

Three forces created them at once. Foundation models made capable prototypes achievable in days rather than months, which lowered the barrier for any organisation to try. Management teams felt competitive pressure to demonstrate activity. And the AI Act's AI literacy obligation pushed organisations to engage with the subject, which for many meant starting a project.

The result is a lot of budget for exploration and considerably less thinking about what happens after a successful demonstration — which is where the difficult work begins: evaluation, security, cost, data governance, integration, and convincing people to change how they work.

## The Two Versions of This Role

**The serious version.** The organisation has a defined problem — document processing, customer service triage, internal knowledge search — and wants it solved. Prototypes are a step towards production. There is a user group, a plan for evaluation, someone accountable for adoption, and a budget beyond the pilot.

**The pilot factory.** Exploration is the purpose. You build demonstrations for management, each one lands well, and then attention moves to the next idea. After eighteen months you have a portfolio of things nobody uses and a CV that says you prototyped extensively.

Both are described identically in vacancies. The difference appears in the answers to a few questions.

## Questions That Separate Them

- **What has this organisation put into production in the last year?** Not built — put into production, with users. If nothing, ask why.
- **Who would use what I build, and have they asked for it?** A named user group that requested something is entirely different from a management hypothesis.
- **How will we know whether it works?** An organisation with no answer about evaluation is not ready to go beyond demonstrations.
- **What is the budget after the pilot?** Prototypes are cheap; running systems with usage costs, monitoring and support are not.
- **Who handles security, privacy and data governance for this?** With internal documents and customer data in scope, there must be someone.
- **What happened to the last prototype?** The most revealing question available.

## What Is Genuinely Valuable to Learn Here

It would be wrong to dismiss this work. The skills that transfer are real, provided the role includes them.

**Evaluation of generative outputs** is a scarce and durable skill — building test sets, defining what correct means, measuring hallucination and regression, and doing it in Dutch where the general benchmarks do not help.

**Retrieval design.** Most of the quality in a document assistant comes from search, chunking, permissions and data preparation rather than from the model.

**Cost and latency engineering.** Making something affordable at scale is a real constraint that prototypes never encounter.

**Security and privacy design.** Which documents may be indexed, whose access rights apply at retrieval time, what is logged, where data is processed.

**Regulatory literacy.** Under the AI Act, systems interacting with people must make that clear, and synthetic content carries marking and disclosure obligations. Organisations need people who know this.

A role that includes these is building transferable expertise. A role that stops at the demonstration is not.

## The Career Risk, Stated Plainly

Two years of prototypes with no production experience creates a specific weakness in interviews. Interviewers ask what happened to the systems you built, how they were evaluated, what broke and how you fixed it. "It was a proof of concept" answers all three questions the same way, and the pattern is visible after the second example.

There is a second risk, subtler. Prototype work tends to skip the engineering practice — testing, monitoring, deployment, documentation — that employers rely on when assessing whether you can be trusted with production. Skills you never had to use do not appear later when you need them.

This is not an argument against generative AI work. It is an argument for insisting that at least one thing you build reaches real users.

## How to Protect Yourself If You Accept

**Negotiate one production commitment.** Ask that at least one project in your first year is intended for real users, with a named owner and a budget for running it. Put it in writing.

**Insist on evaluation from the start.** Build the test set before the demonstration. It makes the work defensible and gives you the most portable part of the experience.

**Keep the engineering standards up.** Version control, tests, documentation and deployment practice, even for prototypes. It costs little and preserves your professional habits.

**Document what happened to each project.** Including the ones that were abandoned and why. This becomes your interview material.

**Maintain neighbouring skills.** Classical modelling, data engineering or optimisation, depending on your direction. Generative AI tooling changes quickly; fundamentals do not.

## When the Role Is Worth Taking Anyway

- The organisation has put something into production before, in any technology.
- A specific user group is waiting for something.
- You would own evaluation, which is the scarcest skill in this area.
- The alternative is no AI work at all, and you need current experience.
- The employer is honest that they are exploring, and has agreed how long exploration lasts before a decision.

## When to Decline

- Nothing has reached users in two years and nobody can explain why.
- The purpose is visibly to demonstrate activity to a board.
- Security, privacy and data governance have no owner.
- There is no budget beyond the pilot phase.
- The role reports to marketing and is measured in demonstrations delivered.
- You are early in your career and would learn no production practice at all.

## Where This Work Does Reach Production

It is worth knowing which applications actually make it past the demonstration stage in Dutch organisations, because those are the roles where the experience accumulates.

**Document-heavy back offices.** Insurers, legal and tax practices, notaries, housing corporations and public bodies process enormous volumes of text. Classification, extraction, summarisation and search over internal documents reach production regularly, because the work is repetitive, measurable and already someone's full-time job.

**Customer contact triage.** Routing incoming messages, drafting replies for an agent to review and summarising call notes. These succeed because a human stays in the loop and the baseline is easy to beat.

**Internal knowledge search.** Retrieval over policies, procedures and technical documentation, where the users are employees rather than customers and the risk profile is manageable.

**Subtitling, transcription and archive enrichment** in media and public organisations, which have a long history of language technology and clear quality standards.

**Developer and analyst productivity tools** inside technical teams, where the users can evaluate the output themselves.

What these share is a defined user group, an existing manual process to compare against and tolerable consequences when the output is wrong. Applications that fail to launch tend to lack at least one of those. When assessing a role, ask which of these patterns the work resembles — and if it resembles none of them, ask what makes this case different.

## How the Market Values This Experience Now

Two years ago, any generative AI experience was a differentiator because almost nobody had it. That has changed, and it is worth understanding how Dutch employers currently read this line on a CV.

**Prototype experience alone has become common.** A large number of candidates can describe a retrieval assistant they built. It no longer distinguishes you, and interviewers have learned to ask the follow-up questions: what did it score, how did you measure that, who used it, what did it cost to run.

**Production experience remains scarce.** Far fewer candidates have taken a generative system through security review, data governance, permission-aware retrieval, monitoring and a real user population that complained when it was wrong. That combination is what employers are short of.

**Evaluation is the most transferable part.** An organisation that has no way of knowing whether its system improved or regressed cannot responsibly deploy it, and people who can build that capability are valued well beyond generative work itself.

**Domain-specific language work is differentiating.** Dutch-language evaluation, regulated document processing, medical or legal terminology — general benchmarks do not cover these, and employers know it.

**Adjacent engineering still decides many hires.** Data pipelines, retrieval infrastructure, cost control and deployment practice are what make a candidate hireable for the production version of this work.

If you take an exploratory role, aim your first year at the second, third and fourth of those. A year spent on demonstrations makes you look like everyone else; a year spent on evaluation, governance and one deployed system makes you look like the person employers cannot find.

## Real example

### An engineer in Utrecht who asked for one production project

An engineer is offered a role at a services organisation to "explore generative AI applications". The budget is generous, the manager is enthusiastic, and the first project is an internal assistant for policy documents.

He asks what reached production last year. The honest answer is nothing: two prototypes were demonstrated and shelved. He asks who would use the assistant, and the answer is more encouraging — a support team of thirty people who currently search a document library manually and have complained about it for years.

He accepts, with one condition in writing: the assistant is intended for that support team in production within nine months, with a named owner in that department, a budget for running costs and evaluation built from the start.

The first version disappoints, because retrieval over inconsistently structured policy documents is harder than the demonstration suggested. The second, six months later, is used daily. The evaluation set he built becomes the organisation's standard for every subsequent project — and the thing every interviewer asks him about afterwards.

## Key Takeaways

- Generative AI roles proliferated because prototypes became cheap, competitive pressure grew and AI literacy obligations pushed organisations to act.
- The serious version solves a defined problem for named users; the pilot factory produces demonstrations that never reach anyone.
- Ask what has reached production, who would use it, how success is measured, what the post-pilot budget is and what happened to the last prototype.
- Durable skills in this area are evaluation, retrieval design, cost and latency engineering, security and privacy design, and AI Act literacy.
- If you accept, negotiate one production commitment in writing, build evaluation from the start and maintain engineering practice and neighbouring skills.
