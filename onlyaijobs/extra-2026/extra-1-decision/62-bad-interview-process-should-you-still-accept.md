Four weeks of silence, then an interview moved twice, then a technical round with someone who had clearly not read your CV, then an offer that arrived with a different job title than the one you applied for. And the work still sounds interesting, the team you met seemed capable, and the money is right. Candidates are routinely told that a bad process means a bad employer, which is too simple to be useful. Some recruitment failures genuinely predict how the organisation operates. Others predict nothing except that one person was on holiday during a busy quarter. The skill is telling them apart before you decide.

## What the Process Legitimately Tells You

Recruitment is the one organisational activity you observe from outside before committing, so it is worth reading carefully — for what it actually reveals.

**How decisions get made.** A process where nobody can say who decides, or where an offer waits three weeks for an unnamed approval, is showing you something about internal authority that will also apply to your project budget.

**Whether the role is defined.** Interviewers describing different jobs is the most reliable warning in the entire process. It usually means the role was created without agreement about what it is for, and you would be the one resolving that.

**How people are treated when they have no power.** Candidates have very little leverage. How an organisation behaves towards people in that position is a reasonable proxy for how it behaves towards new joiners, who have only slightly more.

**Whether the team has capacity.** Interviewers who are visibly unprepared and rushed are often genuinely overloaded, which is exactly the environment you would be joining.

## What It Does Not Tell You

**The recruiter is not the team.** External agencies and internal recruiters frequently run a process badly for reasons the hiring team never sees. If the technical conversations were good, weight them more heavily than the administration.

**Slowness is often structural.** Public bodies, large corporates and organisations with works council involvement in appointment procedures move slowly by design. Slow is not the same as disrespectful.

**Holidays are real.** In the Netherlands, decisions genuinely stall in the summer and around the turn of the year, and a three-week gap in August means less than the same gap in March.

**One bad interviewer is one bad interviewer.** Unless they would be your manager, in which case it is not a data point but the decision itself.

## What Is Normal Here

Dutch recruitment practice is guided in part by the **NVP recruitment code**, which sets expectations around transparency about the procedure, timely communication and handling of candidate information. It is a code of conduct rather than law, and many employers follow it loosely, so treat it as a benchmark for what good looks like rather than a rule you can enforce.

A few reasonable expectations follow from it. You should be told what the process involves and roughly how long it takes. You should hear back within a reasonable period, with a reason if you are rejected. Your application data should be handled properly under privacy rules and not retained indefinitely without a basis. And with pay transparency obligations increasingly shaping practice, an indication of the salary range early in the process is becoming standard rather than exceptional.

Travel reimbursement for interviews is common, though not universal; asking is normal and is not held against candidates.

## Failures That Are Ordinary

- One rescheduled interview, with notice and an apology.
- A two- or three-week pause during a holiday period, explained.
- A recruiter who knows little about the technical work but arranges everything else competently.
- A generic first-round conversation before you meet the team.
- A take-home assignment of a few hours, clearly scoped and relevant to the work.
- A slower process in a public organisation with a formal appointment procedure.

None of these predicts anything about the job.

## Failures That Predict the Job

- **Interviewers describe different roles.** The single strongest warning available.
- **Nobody can say who your manager would be,** or the answer changes.
- **The scope of the job grows with each conversation** while the terms do not.
- **An unpaid assignment that is real work,** particularly one resembling a current problem, or one that takes days rather than hours.
- **Pressure tactics:** an exploding deadline, or enthusiasm treated as a test of commitment.
- **Evasiveness about the basics** — salary range, contract type, who you would work with, what happened to your predecessor.
- **Disrespect that is casual rather than accidental:** dismissive comments, an interviewer taking other calls, questions about personal circumstances that have no business being asked.
- **Terms that change between the verbal offer and the document.**

The distinction underneath this list is between disorganisation and disregard. Disorganised employers are often perfectly good places to work. Employers who treat you carelessly when they are trying to impress you will not treat you better afterwards.

## The Most Useful Move: Ask About It

Rather than speculating, raise it. Near the end of the process, in a normal tone: the process has taken longer than expected and I met people with different descriptions of the role, so I would like to understand how it is defined and who decides.

The answer resolves most cases. A candid response — a reorganisation, a departure, a hiring freeze that lifted, an honest apology — is reassuring, and the willingness to give it says more than the original failure did. A defensive or dismissive answer tells you that raising problems is unwelcome, which is exactly what you needed to know.

This also has a side benefit: candidates who ask questions like this calmly are frequently taken more seriously, not less.

## Verify Before You Decide

Three checks are worth more than any amount of interpreting the process.

**Speak to a future colleague without a manager present.** Ask what the last difficult period was like and what they would change. The candour of the answer is itself the signal.

**Ask what happened to the person who held the role before,** and how long they stayed.

**Get everything in writing.** Title, scope, salary and scale position, contract type, hours, holiday, pension, home working, training budget and anything you negotiated. A messy process makes this more important, not less, because verbal agreements in a disorganised organisation evaporate when the person who made them moves on.

## When to Withdraw Mid-Process

- The role's definition keeps changing and nobody will commit to one.
- You were asked to do substantial unpaid work that benefits them directly.
- Someone treated you or another candidate with open disrespect.
- You were asked questions that have no legitimate place in a hiring conversation, and nobody intervened.
- The employer will not confirm basic terms before expecting a decision.

Withdrawing is allowed, and doing it politely costs you nothing. The Dutch AI market is small, and a courteous withdrawal is remembered better than a reluctant acceptance.

## When to Accept Anyway

- The failures were administrative, and the people you would work with were prepared, honest and interesting.
- Someone acknowledged the problems without being prompted.
- The role is clearly defined and consistently described.
- Your future manager was impressive, whatever the process around them was like.
- The written offer matches everything you were told.

A chaotic process with a strong team is a common combination, especially in organisations hiring their first data people, where nobody has done this before and the recruitment machinery is genuinely improvised.

## What a Good Process Looks Like

It helps to have a benchmark, because candidates who have only seen poor processes sometimes normalise them. A well-run recruitment for an AI role in the Netherlands tends to look like this.

**Three to four conversations, not seven.** A first conversation about the role and your background, a technical discussion, a meeting with the team, and a closing conversation about terms. Processes with six or more rounds usually indicate indecision rather than thoroughness.

**A stated shape at the start.** How many steps, who you will meet, roughly how long it takes and when you will hear back.

**A salary indication early.** Increasingly standard as pay transparency obligations shape practice, and a strong signal of an employer that has thought about fairness internally.

**A technical assessment proportionate to the role.** A conversation about your past work, a discussion of a realistic problem, or a scoped exercise of a few hours. Serious employers increasingly prefer a structured technical discussion over a take-home assignment, because it takes less of your time and tells them more.

**Meeting people you would actually work with,** including at least one person who is not your manager.

**Reasons given.** A rejection with a sentence of substance, and a decision communicated when promised.

**Room to ask.** Time set aside for your questions, and honest answers to the difficult ones — what went wrong last year, why the role is open, what the team finds frustrating.

Measured against that, most processes have some rough edges, which is normal. What matters is whether the rough edges were administrative or whether they went to the substance: the definition of the role, the honesty of the answers and the respect shown to people with no leverage.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Amersfoort who asked the awkward question

A data scientist reaches the offer stage after a process that took eleven weeks. Two interviews were rescheduled, four weeks passed with no contact, and the two technical interviewers described the role differently: one as a modelling role, one as a platform role.

She is inclined to decline, and instead asks. The answer is more informative than she expected: the team's lead left during the process, hiring paused for a month, and the two interviewers genuinely disagreed about what the role should be — a disagreement that was resolved two weeks ago by the new lead, who explains the outcome clearly and admits the process was poor.

She then asks to speak to an engineer on the team, without managers. He confirms the version she was given, says the lead's departure was difficult and that things have settled, and describes work she finds interesting.

She accepts, with the role definition and everything she negotiated in the written offer.

A year later, her view is that the process was a fair reflection of a difficult period rather than of the organisation — and that she would have declined a good job on incomplete information if she had not asked.

## Key Takeaways

- Distinguish disorganisation, which often predicts nothing, from disregard, which predicts how you will be treated as an employee.
- The strongest warning is interviewers describing different roles; it usually means the job was never defined.
- Dutch practice, guided by the NVP recruitment code, sets reasonable expectations on transparency, timeliness and handling of your data — use it as a benchmark, not a rule you can enforce.
- Raise the problems directly near the end of the process; the quality of the answer resolves most cases.
- Verify with a colleague conversation, the predecessor's story and a complete written offer before deciding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate after a chaotic process) Does a bad interview process mean a bad employer?
Not necessarily. Administrative chaos frequently predicts nothing, particularly in organisations hiring data people for the first time. Carelessness towards candidates predicts more.

### (Scenario: candidate seeing inconsistencies) What is the most serious warning sign?
Interviewers describing different roles, or nobody being able to say who your manager would be. That usually means the role was created without agreement about its purpose.

### (Scenario: candidate deciding whether to raise it) Should I mention the problems before accepting?
Yes, calmly and near the end. A candid explanation is reassuring; a defensive answer tells you how raising problems will be received once you work there.

### (Scenario: candidate asked to do an assignment) When is a take-home assignment unreasonable?
When it takes days rather than hours, resembles a current business problem, or is unpaid work that benefits the employer directly.

### (Scenario: candidate about to accept) What should I insist on?
Everything in writing — title, scope, salary and scale position, contract type, hours, pension, training budget and any specific commitment made during the process.
