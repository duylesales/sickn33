You spent an evening tailoring an application, sent it, and heard nothing. Two weeks later you are refreshing your inbox and wondering whether a follow-up would look keen or desperate. Most candidates resolve this by doing nothing, which is understandable and usually a small mistake. A short, well-timed follow-up rarely changes a decision that has already been made, but it regularly produces information — and information is what lets you stop waiting for a role that is gone and put your effort somewhere it can still work.

## Why Silence Is So Common

Silence usually has mundane causes rather than hidden meaning.

**Volume.** Specialised roles at attractive employers can attract many applications, and smaller employers often have no recruiter at all — the hiring manager reads applications between meetings.

**Internal candidates.** Some processes are effectively decided before external applications are reviewed.

**Timing inside the organisation.** A decision-maker on holiday, a budget review, a reorganisation or a hiring pause can freeze a process without anyone updating candidates.

**Fragmented systems.** Applications arrive in an applicant tracking system that nobody has configured to send status updates, or in a shared mailbox.

**Process drift.** Specialised roles often take months. Two weeks of silence is normal; two months of silence is a signal.

**Agencies in between.** When an intermediary is involved, a response to them does not always reach you.

## What Dutch Practice Expects

Many Dutch employers follow the **NVP Recruitment Code**, a widely used code of conduct for recruitment. It sets expectations that candidates are informed about the procedure, that applications are handled within reasonable timeframes, and that candidates receive reasons for rejection on request. It is a code rather than law, but it describes the norm most professional employers try to meet.

This matters for your decision: asking politely for a status update is not pushy in the Dutch context. It is asking an employer to do what the prevailing norm already expects.

## When to Follow Up

| Situation | When to follow up |
|---|---|
| The vacancy states a response time | One week after that date |
| No timeline stated | Two to three weeks after applying |
| You applied through an agency | After one week, to the recruiter, not the employer |
| After a first interview | If no timeline was given, one week after the interview |
| After a final interview | On the date they promised, or one week if no date |
| You received another offer | Immediately, whatever stage you are at |
| The vacancy is still live after your rejection | No follow-up needed; it may simply not be removed |

Following up sooner than a week rarely helps and can read as impatience. Waiting longer than a month usually means you are chasing a decision that has already been made.

## How to Follow Up Well

**Use email, to a named person where possible.** Phone calls interrupt; email can be answered when convenient. If the vacancy names a contact, use that address rather than a generic inbox.

**Keep it to three sentences.** Confirm you applied and when, ask about the timeline or status, and state briefly that you remain interested and why.

**Add one small piece of value if you have it.** A relevant project you finished, a question about the team's tooling, or a short note on something in their vacancy that connects to your experience.

**Do not repeat your whole application.** They have it. If they did not read it the first time, length will not fix that.

**Write in the language of the vacancy.** A Dutch vacancy usually deserves a Dutch follow-up, even a short one.

A workable shape: *"I applied on 3 November for the data engineer role and wanted to check how the process stands. I remain very interested, particularly because of the pipeline work described in the vacancy. Happy to provide anything else that would help."*

## What Not to Do

- **Following up on multiple channels at once.** An email plus a message on a professional network plus a phone call in one week reads as pressure.
- **Following up more than twice.** Two attempts is the maximum before silence becomes the answer.
- **Asking for feedback before a decision.** Feedback comes after a rejection, not during a process.
- **Implying entitlement.** Employers are not obliged to respond quickly, only to behave reasonably.
- **Sending a follow-up that is longer than the original application.**

## Following Up After an Interview

The dynamic changes after you have met people. A brief message within a day or two thanking the interviewers and adding one substantive point — a clarification you did not make well, a link to something discussed — is well received in most Dutch organisations, provided it is genuinely short.

If the interviewer promised an answer by a date and that date passes, following up is straightforward and expected. If no date was given, ask at the end of the interview: "When can I expect to hear from you?" That single question removes most of the uncertainty that later makes follow-ups awkward.

## When You Have Another Offer

This is the one situation where a follow-up is not just acceptable but expected. Tell the employer plainly: you have received an offer with a decision deadline, you are genuinely interested in their role, and you wanted to ask whether their process can be accelerated.

Employers respond to this professionally, because it is a normal part of hiring. It also produces a clear answer quickly — either they move, or they tell you they cannot, which is itself useful information.

Do not invent a competing offer that does not exist. In a small market, the bluff is unnecessary and the reputational risk is real.

## What the Response Tells You

The answer you get is often more informative than the answer you wanted.

**A prompt, specific reply** with a stage and a date indicates an organised employer. Worth your continued attention.

**A vague reply** about "still reviewing" without any timeline, repeated twice, suggests a process without momentum.

**No reply at all after two attempts** answers the question. Mark it closed in your tracker and move your energy elsewhere.

**A reply from a different person than expected** can indicate a change inside the organisation, which sometimes explains the delay.

## Your Rights Around Your Application Data

Separately from the outcome, you have rights over the data you handed over. Under the **GDPR** you can ask what an employer stores about you, request deletion and object to further processing. Dutch guidance from the **Dutch Data Protection Authority** indicates that application data should generally be deleted within a short period after the procedure ends, unless you consent to longer retention for future vacancies.

If you would like to be considered later, say so explicitly — that consent is what allows an employer to keep your details legitimately.

## Keep a Follow-Up Log, Not Just an Application List

Most candidates track where they applied. Far fewer track what they were told, which is where the useful information sits.

For each application, note the date you applied, the response deadline stated in the vacancy, the date of any follow-up, and the substance of the answer you received. Three columns beyond the basics: promised date, actual date, outcome.

Over a month this turns vague frustration into a pattern you can act on. If several employers promised answers "next week" and none arrived, you learn that your pipeline is slower than it feels, which changes how many applications you should have running. If one recruiter consistently answers within a day, that relationship is worth maintaining. If the only silent applications are those sent through one particular channel, that tells you where your effort is being wasted.

The log also prevents a specific embarrassment: following up twice on the same application because you forgot you already did, or contacting an employer who told you they would come back in January.

Saved jobs on a board covers the first half of this. A spreadsheet with six columns covers the rest, and takes about a minute per application to maintain.

## When to Stop Waiting

Two follow-ups without a substantive answer is the end of the line. The practical response is not to feel wronged but to update your own process: mark the application closed, note the employer and date in your tracker, and allocate the attention to applications that are moving.

It is also worth checking your funnel. If most applications end in silence rather than rejection, the issue is usually targeting or a CV that does not show fit quickly — not the follow-up etiquette.

## Following Up After a Take-Home Assignment

Technical assessments deserve their own approach, because you have invested hours rather than minutes.

Confirm delivery. A one-line message noting that you submitted the assignment, with the date, protects you against the surprisingly common case of a file that never arrived or landed in a spam folder.

Then wait a week before asking about the outcome. Assessments often need review by several people, which takes longer than a CV screening.

When you do follow up, ask two things: whether a decision has been made, and whether you could receive brief feedback on the submission regardless of the outcome. Framing feedback as separate from the decision makes it easier for the employer to give, and it is the most valuable thing you can extract from the process.

If the answer is a rejection, ask once more, specifically: which part of the approach was weakest relative to what they needed. Many reviewers will answer a precise question even when they decline to write general feedback. That answer is worth considerably more than the two hours you spent, because it applies to every future assessment.

## Real example

### A data analyst in Den Bosch who asked one question at the right moment

A data analyst in Den Bosch applies to four roles in a fortnight and hears nothing from any of them. Rather than waiting, she sends three-sentence follow-ups at the two-and-a-half-week mark.

One employer replies the same day: interviews start next week and her application is in the shortlist. A second replies after three days to say the role was filled internally, apologising for not informing candidates. The third never answers, and she closes it after a second attempt. The fourth replies that the process is paused until budget approval in January, and asks whether she would like to be contacted then — she says yes, in writing, which also gives them a basis to keep her details.

Three follow-ups, roughly fifteen minutes of work, and she goes from four unknowns to one live process, two closed files and one future option. The follow-ups did not change any decision. They let her stop spending attention on processes that were already over.

## Key Takeaways

- Silence usually reflects volume, internal candidates, process delays or unconfigured systems rather than a judgement on you.
- The NVP Recruitment Code, followed by many Dutch employers, expects candidates to be informed within reasonable timeframes and given reasons on request.
- Follow up two to three weeks after applying, or one week after a stated response date, by email to a named person and in three sentences.
- A competing offer justifies an immediate follow-up; never invent one.
- Two attempts without a substantive answer means the answer is no — close it and move your effort to live processes.

## Where to Start

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate waiting for a reply) How long should I wait before following up on an application?
Two to three weeks if no timeline was given, or one week after the response date stated in the vacancy.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about appearing pushy) Is following up seen as unprofessional in the Netherlands?
No — a short, polite email is normal, and the NVP Recruitment Code expects employers to inform candidates within reasonable timeframes.

### (Scenario: candidate who applied through an agency) Should I contact the employer directly if an agency submitted me?
No — follow up with the recruiter who presented you. Contacting the employer directly can create confusion and disputes about who introduced you.

### (Scenario: candidate with a competing offer) Can I ask an employer to speed up their process?
Yes, and it is expected. State the deadline honestly and confirm your interest; never invent an offer you do not have.

### (Scenario: candidate receiving no answer at all) What does it mean if nobody replies after two follow-ups?
Treat it as a no. Close the application in your tracker and spend the attention on processes that are still moving.
