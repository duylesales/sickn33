The process has gone well. Two interviews, a technical conversation that felt genuinely interesting, and then an email: before the final stage, the employer would like you to complete an assessment at an external agency. Half a day, a cognitive ability test, a personality questionnaire and an interview with a psychologist. For candidates in AI and data roles — people who evaluate models for a living — this often produces a specific discomfort, because the instrument being applied to you is harder to interrogate than the ones you build. It is worth knowing what these assessments are, what rights you have over the results, and when declining is a reasonable professional choice rather than a red flag.

## What a Dutch Assessment Usually Contains

**Cognitive ability tests.** Timed exercises on verbal, numerical and abstract reasoning. These have the strongest research support of anything in the selection literature, and also the most debate about fairness across groups and language backgrounds.

**Personality questionnaires.** Self-report instruments mapping traits such as conscientiousness, openness or stress tolerance. Useful as conversation material; weaker as a basis for a hiring decision on their own.

**Work simulations or case exercises.** In-basket tasks, a presentation, a role-play with an actor. For data roles these are usually less relevant than a technical assessment, but they do test structuring and communication.

**An interview with a psychologist.** Typically the part that carries the most weight in the final report.

For AI and data roles specifically, assessments appear most often at larger employers, public organisations, consultancies and traineeship programmes. Smaller employers rarely use them, relying on technical conversations instead.

## Your Rights Around the Results

This is the part most candidates do not know, and it changes the decision.

Under the **NVP Recruitment Code**, which many Dutch employers and assessment agencies follow, a psychological assessment is conducted with your consent, you are entitled to see the report, and you have the right to decide whether it is shared with the employer — the blocking right. Reputable agencies will state this explicitly before you start.

Under the **GDPR**, the assessment results are personal data about you. You can request access to them, ask for correction of factual errors and ask how long they are retained. The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** supervises this, and Dutch guidance indicates application data should not be kept longer than necessary.

Professional standards matter too. Psychologists working under the standards of the **Netherlands Institute of Psychologists (NIP)** are bound by a professional code covering consent, confidentiality and careful use of instruments.

Two practical consequences. First, ask before agreeing whether you will see the report first and whether you may block its release. Second, if the agency cannot answer that question clearly, that tells you something about the process you are entering.

## What Assessments Are Reasonably Good At

They are better than unstructured interviews at predicting general job performance, which is a low bar but a real one. They give a structured comparison across candidates, which reduces the influence of who interviewed best on the day. And they often surface a genuinely useful conversation: a well-written report can describe how you work under pressure or how you prefer to receive feedback in a way that helps both sides.

For roles where you would be the first data professional, working largely alone, an employer's interest in stress tolerance and independence is not unreasonable.

## What They Are Poor At

They do not measure whether you can build a reliable data pipeline, evaluate a model honestly or explain uncertainty to a planner. For AI and data roles, the actual predictive content sits in the technical conversation and any practical assignment, not in an abstract reasoning score.

Personality questionnaires in particular are self-report instruments, easily influenced by how you interpret the questions, and weak as a stand-alone selection tool. Reports can also read as more definitive than the underlying evidence supports — a familiar problem to anyone who has watched a confident model output be treated as fact.

And language matters. A verbal reasoning test in Dutch measures something different for a non-native speaker than for a native one, which is a fairness question worth raising if it applies to you.

## Before You Agree: Five Questions

- **Who conducts it, and under which professional standards?**
- **Will I see the report before the employer does, and can I block its release?**
- **What weight does this carry in the decision?** An assessment used as a tiebreaker is different from one used as a gate.
- **How long are the results retained, and by whom?**
- **Is any part of it automated?** Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems used to evaluate candidates are high-risk, with obligations including human oversight and informing candidates; under the GDPR you have rights concerning decisions based solely on automated processing.

Asking these is normal and professional. An employer who reacts badly to them has answered a different question.

## When Declining Is Reasonable

**When the timing is disproportionate.** A half-day assessment before any substantive conversation about the role is a lot to ask.

**When the content is irrelevant.** For a senior specialist role, a generic management simulation may add nothing that the technical stages did not.

**When your rights are unclear.** No answer about seeing the report or blocking it is a legitimate reason to pause.

**When it appears to be automated scoring without human review.** That raises both AI Act and GDPR questions.

**When it feels like a substitute for judgement.** Occasionally an employer uses an assessment because nobody internally can evaluate the technical work. That is a warning about the role itself.

Declining is best done by proposing an alternative: a technical conversation with a senior engineer, a short practical exercise, or a reference from someone who has managed you. Many employers accept, particularly for experienced candidates.

## When Agreeing Is the Right Call

If the role matters to you, the assessment is proportionate, your rights are clear and it is one input among several, agreeing costs half a day and removes an obstacle. Public sector and large corporate processes often cannot easily make exceptions, and refusing on principle may simply end an otherwise good process.

There is also a selfish argument: you get a professionally written description of how you work, which many people find genuinely useful regardless of the outcome.

## Preparing Without Gaming It

**Practise the format, not the answers.** Timed reasoning tests reward familiarity with the question types. A few practice sets remove the surprise factor.

**Sleep, and do it in the morning if you can.** Cognitive tests are sensitive to fatigue in a way that is entirely unromantic.

**Answer personality questionnaires consistently and honestly.** Most contain consistency checks, and a profile engineered to look ideal often reads as exactly that.

**Prepare two examples** of a project that went wrong and what you changed afterwards. Psychologist interviews return to this territory repeatedly.

**Ask for adjustments if you need them.** Dutch equal treatment legislation includes an obligation to make reasonable accommodations for people with disabilities or chronic conditions, including in selection; you do not need to disclose more than necessary to arrange it.

## What Is Not Allowed

Health questions and medical examinations in recruitment are restricted under the **Medical Examinations Act**; a pre-employment medical examination is only permitted where the role has specific medical requirements, which is essentially never the case for data work.

Selection decisions may not discriminate on protected grounds under the **General Equal Treatment Act**, and an assessment that produces systematically different outcomes for a protected group without justification is a problem for the employer, not for you.

## Where Assessments Are Hardest to Avoid

Some processes treat an assessment as a fixed step, and knowing which ones saves you a pointless negotiation.

**Traineeship programmes** select cohorts rather than individuals, and comparability between candidates is the whole point of their process. Assessments are almost always part of it.

**Public sector and executive agencies** frequently use standardised instruments to demonstrate that selection was objective and defensible — which is also why their processes are slower and more formal.

**Large corporates with structured grading systems** apply the same steps to everyone at a given level, and exceptions require someone senior to authorise them.

**Roles with formal integrity requirements** may combine an assessment with screening, particularly where access to sensitive data or financial systems is involved.

By contrast, smaller employers, scale-ups and most regional companies rarely use them at all, relying on technical conversations and a practical exercise. If assessments are something you would rather avoid entirely, that is a reason to weight your search towards those employers rather than a reason to argue with a process that cannot bend.

When the step is genuinely fixed, the useful questions are not whether but how: who conducts it, what you will see, and how much weight it carries alongside the technical stages.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Groningen who asked about the report first

A data scientist reaches the final stage with a large public organisation and is invited to a half-day assessment. Before agreeing, she asks three questions: who conducts it, will she see the report first, and how heavily does it weigh.

The answers are reassuring: an agency working under professional psychological standards, results shared with her before the employer, an explicit blocking right, and the assessment described as one input alongside the interviews and a technical case.

She agrees, spends an evening practising reasoning tests and completes the assessment. The report describes her as strongly analytical, direct in communication and inclined to take on too much — which she recognises and which prompts a useful conversation in the final interview about how the team handles workload.

She gets the job. Two years later she uses the same report's observation about over-commitment when negotiating the scope of a new project. The assessment turned out to be the least decisive and most personally useful part of the process.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch assessments usually combine cognitive tests, a personality questionnaire, sometimes simulations, and an interview with a psychologist.
- Under the NVP Recruitment Code you are generally entitled to see the report and to decide whether it is released; under the GDPR you can request access and ask about retention.
- Assessments predict general performance modestly but say little about the technical work AI and data roles actually require.
- Ask five questions before agreeing: who, rights over the report, weight in the decision, retention, and whether any part is automated.
- Declining is reasonable when the request is disproportionate, irrelevant or unclear — propose a technical alternative instead of refusing flatly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate invited to an assessment) Am I obliged to do a psychological assessment?
No. Participation is based on consent, and you can decline — ideally by proposing an alternative such as a technical conversation or a practical exercise.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about results) Do I get to see the assessment report?
Under the NVP Recruitment Code, followed by many Dutch employers and agencies, you are entitled to see the report and to decide whether it is shared with the employer.

### (Scenario: candidate in a technical role) Do assessments predict performance in data roles?
Only partly. Cognitive tests predict general performance modestly; the technical conversation and a practical assignment say far more about data work specifically.

### (Scenario: candidate facing automated screening) What if part of the assessment is automated?
Ask. Under the AI Act, AI used to evaluate candidates is high-risk with human oversight requirements, and GDPR rules apply to decisions based solely on automated processing.

### (Scenario: candidate needing adjustments) Can I ask for adjustments during an assessment?
Yes. Dutch equal treatment legislation includes an obligation to make reasonable accommodations, and you need only share what is necessary to arrange them.
