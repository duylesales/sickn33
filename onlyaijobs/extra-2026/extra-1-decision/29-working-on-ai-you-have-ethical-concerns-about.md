The role is technically interesting and the team is good. The system is a risk model that decides which people get additional checks, or a monitoring tool that scores employee behaviour, or a recommendation engine whose optimisation target you quietly suspect is engagement at any cost. Nothing about it is illegal. Something about it makes you hesitate. In the Netherlands, this hesitation carries particular weight: the childcare benefits scandal showed what happens when capable people build a risk system without enough people asking uncomfortable questions, and the SyRI judgment showed that legality is not the same as acceptability. Deciding whether to take such a role is a professional judgement, not a purity test — but it is one worth making deliberately rather than by default.

## First: Separate Three Different Discomforts

**"This is probably unlawful."** Processing without a proper basis, discrimination on protected grounds, an automated decision with no human involvement where one is required. This is a legal problem, not only an ethical one, and the answer is usually clear.

**"This is lawful but likely to harm people."** A model that is technically compliant but whose errors fall disproportionately on those least able to contest them. This is the category that requires actual judgement.

**"This conflicts with my personal values."** Advertising optimisation, defence applications, gambling, fossil fuel operations. Legitimate to decline, and different from a claim that the work is wrong for everyone.

Confusing the three makes conversations harder. Being precise about which one you are in makes both your decision and your explanation clearer.

## The Questions to Ask in the Interview

An employer's answers tell you more than any policy document.

- **Who is affected by this system, and what happens to them when it is wrong?** The single most revealing question. Listen for whether anyone has thought about the error cases from the subject's perspective.
- **What assessment has been done?** Under the EU AI Act, AI used to evaluate eligibility for public benefits, to assess creditworthiness of individuals, or in recruitment and employment decisions falls into high-risk categories, with obligations including risk management, data governance, human oversight and documentation. Public bodies deploying high-risk AI must carry out a fundamental rights impact assessment, and the Dutch government has developed instruments such as FRAIA for exactly this.
- **How is human oversight actually implemented?** Not "a person reviews it", but what that person sees, how long they have and how often they deviate.
- **Who can say no here?** Is there a privacy officer, an ethics board, a works council involved, and has anything ever been stopped?
- **What happened the last time someone raised a concern?** The answer, or the discomfort around it, is the culture.

An employer who welcomes these questions is showing you something important. So is one who treats them as naive.

## The Dutch Context Is Not Abstract

Two events changed how this conversation is had in the Netherlands.

The **childcare benefits scandal** led to a parliamentary inquiry concluding that fundamental principles of the rule of law had been violated, and the Dutch Data Protection Authority found processing that was unlawful and discriminatory. Thousands of families were treated as fraudsters.

The **SyRI judgment** in 2020 saw a court rule that legislation behind a system linking government data to detect fraud violated the right to private life, partly because of insufficient transparency and safeguards.

Both involved technically competent people building systems that worked as specified. That is precisely why "I only built the model" is a weaker position here than elsewhere, and why Dutch public organisations now register algorithms, conduct impact assessments and expect practitioners to raise concerns.

## Where You Have Actual Leverage

Employees are not powerless in this area, and several mechanisms exist.

**Inside the project.** The person building the system has more influence over its design than anyone downstream. Error analysis by subgroup, threshold choices, what is logged, what the interface shows a human reviewer, what is documented as a limitation — these are technical decisions with ethical consequences, and they are yours.

**Through governance functions.** Data protection officers, privacy counsel, compliance and internal audit exist partly to be consulted. Involving them early is normal practice, not escalation.

**Through the works council.** Under Dutch law, works councils have consent rights on arrangements concerning personal data of employees and systems that can monitor attendance, behaviour or performance. For workplace monitoring systems specifically, this is a real constraint.

**Through regulators.** The Dutch Data Protection Authority supervises the GDPR and has a coordinating role in algorithm and AI supervision.

**Through protected reporting.** EU and Dutch whistleblower rules protect reporting breaches of EU law, including data protection, through internal and external channels. This is the last resort, not the first move, and worth understanding before you need it.

## When to Decline the Role

- **The illegality is structural**, not a detail someone forgot.
- **Nobody can explain who is affected when the system is wrong**, and nobody seems to consider it their question.
- **The oversight is decorative** — a human clicks approve on a list they cannot realistically evaluate.
- **The team's response to your questions is defensive or dismissive** during the interview, when they are trying to impress you.
- **The purpose itself conflicts with your values** and no amount of good implementation changes that.

Declining a role for these reasons is a professional judgement, not an act of protest. You can say so plainly and without accusation: the work is not something you want to build.

## When to Take It Anyway

The opposite case deserves equal weight. Systems that affect people get built either way. Whether they are built carefully depends on who is in the room.

If the organisation takes assessment seriously, if governance functions have real authority, if the team is genuinely wrestling with the trade-offs, then being the person who insists on subgroup analysis and honest documentation is more useful than refusing on principle from outside. Many of the people who improved public sector algorithm practice in the Netherlands did so from inside the organisations that needed improving.

The distinction is whether your concerns can influence the outcome. If they can, staying is doing the work. If they cannot, you are providing competence to something you do not believe in.

## Before You Sign

Ask for the assessment documentation if it exists. Ask whether you would be named as a contributor on technical documentation for a high-risk system, and what that means for your responsibility. Check whether the contract contains anything that would prevent you raising concerns internally or with a regulator. And consider asking for one thing in writing: that you will be involved in decisions about evaluation criteria for the system you build.

None of these requests is unusual. How they are received is information.

## Documenting Concerns Properly

If you take the role and a concern arises, how you record it matters — for the organisation, for the people affected and, if things go badly, for you.

**Write it into the artefact, not only into an email.** Model documentation, evaluation reports and design decisions are the right place for known limitations, subgroup performance differences and conditions under which the system should not be used. Under the AI Act, technical documentation and record-keeping are obligations for high-risk systems, so this is normal practice rather than an act of dissent.

**Be specific and falsifiable.** "This model performs worse for applicants with non-Dutch names in our test set, by this margin" is actionable. "This feels discriminatory" invites a debate about feelings.

**Propose something.** A threshold change, an additional monitoring metric, a human review step for a subgroup, a limitation on scope. Concerns accompanied by a proposal are far more likely to be adopted.

**Keep your own record.** A dated note of what you raised, to whom and what was decided. Not to build a case, but because memories differ a year later and you may need to explain your role.

**Escalate in order.** Team, then privacy or compliance colleagues, then the works council where employee data is involved. External routes exist under whistleblower protection rules, but they are the last step, not the first.

## Real example

### A data scientist in The Hague who asked one question twice

A data scientist interviews for a role building a risk model that prioritises inspections at an executive organisation. The work is interesting and the salary is good.

In the first interview she asks what happens to someone wrongly selected for additional checks. The hiring manager answers in terms of process efficiency. In the second interview she asks again, more directly: what is the burden on a person who is selected incorrectly, and how would you know if that burden fell unevenly?

This time the answer is different. The team lead explains that a human rights impact assessment was conducted, that a previous version of the model was rejected because its selection correlated with neighbourhood, and that the current design monitors subgroup outcomes quarterly with a named owner.

She takes the job. Two years later she is the person who runs that quarterly analysis — and once, successfully, argues for a threshold change that reduced selection of a group the model had been treating unfairly.

## When the Concern Arises After You Joined

Most of this article assumes you are deciding before accepting. The harder version is discovering the problem six months in, when you have colleagues you like, a mortgage and a project you have invested in.

The sequence that works is deliberately unglamorous. Establish the facts first: what exactly does the system do, who is affected, what does the evaluation actually show? A concern based on an assumption collapses in the first conversation, and you only get a limited number of those.

Then raise it once, in the right place, with a proposal attached. Most concerns are resolved here, because the person responsible had not seen the problem from that angle.

If nothing happens, decide what your line is and say it plainly: which part of the work you are not willing to do, and what alternative you propose. Refusing a specific task with a stated reason is a professional position; general unease expressed repeatedly is not, and it wears out your credibility.

If the line is crossed anyway, the options narrow to accepting it, moving internally or leaving. Knowing in advance which of those you would choose makes the moment far less overwhelming — and it is worth knowing that the Dutch AI market is large enough that leaving over a principle rarely ends a career.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate the three discomforts: probable illegality, lawful but harmful, and personal values. Each leads to a different conversation.
- Ask in the interview who is affected when the system is wrong, what assessment was done, how human oversight really works and who has ever said no.
- Under the AI Act, systems assessing benefits eligibility, creditworthiness or employment decisions are high-risk, with public deployers required to assess fundamental rights impacts.
- Dutch history — the benefits scandal and the SyRI judgment — makes "I only built the model" a weaker position here than elsewhere.
- Take the role if your concerns can influence design; decline if oversight is decorative or the purpose itself conflicts with your values.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate uneasy about a role) Should I refuse to work on an AI system I have doubts about?
It depends on whether your concerns can influence the design. If assessment, oversight and governance are real, working on it carefully is often more useful than declining.

### (Scenario: candidate interviewing) What should I ask about ethics in an interview?
Ask who is affected when the system is wrong, what impact assessment was done, how human oversight works in practice and whether anything has ever been stopped.

### (Scenario: candidate in a Dutch public organisation) What does the AI Act require for systems affecting people?
Systems assessing eligibility for benefits, creditworthiness or employment decisions are high-risk, with obligations on risk management, data governance, oversight and documentation.

### (Scenario: employee with a concern) What can I do if I have concerns once I am inside?
Raise them with the project team, involve privacy or compliance colleagues and, for employee monitoring, the works council; protected reporting channels exist as a last resort.

### (Scenario: candidate declining an offer) How do I decline a role on ethical grounds without burning bridges?
State plainly and without accusation that the work is not something you want to build, thank them for the process and leave the relationship intact.
