You want different work — more engineering, a different domain, a lead role, a team that ships — and there are two routes to it. One is inside your current employer: a different team, a different function, possibly a different site, with everything else unchanged. The other is a new employer, a new contract and a fresh start. Candidates often treat the internal option as a consolation prize and the external one as the real move. That ranking is frequently wrong, and it costs people good opportunities that were available two floors away. It is also sometimes right, particularly about money.

## Why the Internal Move Is Underrated

**Your context is worth more than you think.** Knowing the systems, the data, the people and how decisions actually get made takes new joiners a year to acquire. Internally, you keep all of it and apply it to different work from week one.

**The risk is far lower.** You know the organisation, the terms, the culture and, usually, the manager. Most of what goes wrong with a job change is the part you cannot see in interviews, and internally you can see it.

**Your reputation transfers.** If you are trusted, you arrive trusted, which shortens the period before you are given interesting work.

**Continuity of terms.** Accrued entitlements, pension arrangement, contract status and notice position all continue, which matters if a mortgage or a permit is in the picture.

**It is often faster to arrange.** Where an internal vacancy exists and both managers agree, a move can happen in weeks rather than months.

## Why It Often Does Not Happen

**Nobody offers it.** Internal mobility rarely happens by itself. The receiving manager does not know you are interested, and your own manager has no incentive to raise it.

**Managers hold on to good people.** Not always deliberately, and the effect is the same: a release delayed by one quarter and then another.

**There is no vacancy today.** The team you want has no opening this month, and you conclude it is impossible rather than positioning for the next one.

**People overestimate the awkwardness.** In most Dutch organisations, expressing interest in a different team is entirely normal and is read as ambition rather than disloyalty.

## The Money Question

Here the external route usually wins, and it is worth being clear-eyed about it.

Internal moves typically follow the organisation's pay structure: a step within a scale, or a move to an adjacent one. External moves reset your salary to whatever the market will pay, which after several years in one place is frequently a larger jump than any internal progression would produce.

Two things moderate that. First, pay transparency obligations are pushing employers to be more explicit about ranges and criteria, which makes internal inequities harder to sustain quietly and gives you better grounds to ask. Second, where a collective agreement applies, the scale is the scale, and no amount of negotiation changes it — so the internal ceiling is knowable in advance.

The practical approach: find out the scale attached to the internal role before you decide. If it is a genuine step, the internal move's lower risk may well be worth more than an external increase. If the internal role pays the same for materially more responsibility, you have learned something about your employer.

## How to Run an Internal Move Properly

**Talk to the receiving manager first, informally.** A coffee, not an application. Ask what the team needs and whether someone with your background would be useful. This conversation is what creates the opportunity, and it is entirely normal.

**Then tell your own manager, before it becomes gossip.** Frame it as development rather than as escape. Most managers respond reasonably, and the ones who do not have told you something useful.

**Agree a timeline and a handover.** A named date, a documented handover and, ideally, a role in finding or training your replacement. Nothing protects your reputation better than leaving a team properly.

**Get the terms in writing.** Scale, salary, hours, reporting line and any commitment about scope. Internal moves are frequently agreed verbally and remembered differently later.

**Expect to prove yourself again, a little.** Internal credibility transfers, but not entirely — you are still new to that team's work.

## What Can Go Wrong

**The blocked release.** Your manager agrees in principle and delays in practice. If two quarters pass without movement, that is an answer, and it is often the moment people start looking externally.

**The half-move.** You take the new role and keep the old responsibilities because nobody replaced you. This is common and worth refusing explicitly at the start.

**The reputation shift.** In a small number of organisations, expressing interest elsewhere marks you as disloyal. Where that is the culture, you have learned something important about staying.

**The same problem, different team.** If what you dislike is the organisation — its pace, its politics, its appetite for data work — an internal move changes the wallpaper. Be honest about whether the problem is the team or the place.

## When Internal Is Clearly Better

- The work you want genuinely exists inside your employer.
- Your context and relationships would transfer into the new role usefully.
- The organisation is a good place to work and the current team is the problem.
- You need stability — a mortgage, a permit, a demanding period at home.
- The internal role is a real step in scale and scope.
- You would keep learning from people you respect.

## When External Is Clearly Better

- The work you want does not exist where you are.
- Your salary has drifted well below market and internal structures cannot correct it.
- You have already tried internally and been blocked.
- You have stopped learning from the organisation as a whole.
- Your reputation there is fixed at a level you have outgrown — a real phenomenon, and a common reason capable people leave.
- The employer's direction on data work is one you do not believe in.

## A Note on Using an External Offer

Some people obtain an external offer to force an internal move or a pay correction. It works often enough that the tactic persists, and it carries two costs: you may be treated afterwards as someone who negotiates under threat, and you must be genuinely willing to leave, because the offer may be accepted rather than matched.

The cleaner sequence is to ask internally first, on the merits, and to go externally when the answer is no. You keep the relationships either way, and in a market as small as Dutch data work, that matters more than a single increase.

There is also a middle path that costs nothing: interview externally to find out what the market pays, decide honestly whether you would accept, and only then open the internal conversation. Knowing your market value changes how you ask, even when you never mention the other process — and if the internal answer is disappointing, you already know what your alternatives look like rather than guessing.

## How Employers Read This Later

Both routes leave a trace on your record, and it is worth knowing how Dutch hiring managers interpret them.

**Long tenure with internal moves reads well.** Eight years at one employer, with three distinct roles, signals that an organisation kept investing in you and that you can operate in different contexts. It is read very differently from eight years in the same job.

**Long tenure without internal moves raises a question.** Not about loyalty, but about breadth: interviewers wonder whether your practice has been shaped by one organisation's habits, and whether you would adapt. The answer is to describe what changed within the role — new systems, new domains, new responsibilities — rather than the years.

**Frequent external moves raise a different question.** Three employers in four years invites a question about whether you stay long enough to live with your own decisions, which matters in a field where models fail eighteen months after deployment.

**Describe internal moves as moves.** On a CV, list them as separate roles with their own dates and scope, rather than as one long entry with a title change at the end. Candidates routinely under-sell internal progression by presenting it as a single job.

**Benchmark yourself externally every couple of years,** even when you intend to stay. An interview or two tells you what the market pays and what it expects, which is the information internal structures are least likely to give you honestly.

The most common regret is not choosing wrongly between the two routes. It is staying in one place long enough that the choice stopped being available, because your skills had narrowed to one organisation's way of working.

## Real example

### An analyst in Utrecht who moved two floors instead of two cities

An analyst in a finance department wants to do machine learning rather than reporting. The obvious route is a new employer, and the vacancies she likes are all forty minutes further away.

Before applying, she asks for a coffee with the lead of the risk modelling team, whom she has met twice. She asks what the team needs. The answer is someone who understands the finance data, which almost nobody in that team does.

There is no vacancy. He says he expects one within six months and asks her to tell him when she is ready.

She tells her own manager, who is briefly unenthusiastic and then constructive. They agree that she will document her reporting work over the coming months either way, which turns out to be useful for the department regardless.

The vacancy appears four months later. She moves, one scale step up, keeping her pension arrangement and her notice position, and arrives already knowing the data the team struggles with most.

Two years on, she is doing the work she wanted, at an employer she never had to gamble on. Her only regret is having assumed for a year that the move was impossible without asking.

## Key Takeaways

- Internal moves preserve context, relationships, reputation and terms, and carry far less of the risk that makes job changes go wrong.
- They usually happen only when you create them: talk to the receiving manager informally first, then your own manager.
- External moves generally pay better, because they reset your salary to the market rather than following an internal structure — check the scale of the internal role before deciding.
- Refuse the half-move where you keep old responsibilities, agree a handover and get the new terms in writing.
- Go externally when the work does not exist internally, when you have been blocked, or when your reputation there is fixed at a level you have outgrown.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: professional wanting different work) Is an internal move a lesser option?
Usually not. It preserves context, relationships and terms while carrying far less risk. The common disadvantage is pay, since internal moves follow the organisation's structure.

### (Scenario: professional considering the approach) How do I start an internal move?
Speak informally to the receiving manager first about what their team needs, then tell your own manager, framing it as development rather than escape.

### (Scenario: professional comparing pay) Will I earn less by moving internally?
Often, because external moves reset your salary to the market. Check the scale attached to the internal role first; where a collective agreement applies, the ceiling is knowable in advance.

### (Scenario: professional being delayed) What if my manager blocks the move?
Agreement in principle with no movement after two quarters is an answer. That is usually the point at which an external search becomes the reasonable next step.

### (Scenario: professional considering leverage) Should I use an external offer to force the move?
It works, and it costs you: you may be seen as negotiating under threat, and you must be genuinely willing to leave. Asking internally on the merits first is cleaner.
