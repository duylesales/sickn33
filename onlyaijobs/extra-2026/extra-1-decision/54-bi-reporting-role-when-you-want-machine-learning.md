You studied machine learning, or retrained into it, and the offer in front of you is a reporting role: building dashboards, maintaining definitions, answering questions from managers who want a number by Thursday. The employer says there is "definitely scope to move into predictive work". You have heard that before, or you have read that others have. The honest position is that a large share of Dutch data careers began exactly here, and another large share got stuck exactly here. What separates the two is knowable in advance, and it has little to do with how enthusiastically the manager describes the future.

## Why This Offer Keeps Appearing

Most organisations need reporting before they need prediction, and many need it badly. If a company cannot agree what "active customer" means, or reconcile two systems that count orders differently, a forecasting model is not the binding constraint.

That is also why these roles are numerous. The Dutch market has far more reporting and analysis vacancies than machine learning ones, and they are spread across every sector and region rather than concentrated in a few employers in the Randstad. For a career changer or a recent graduate, this is frequently the realistic first door.

## What a Reporting Role Genuinely Builds

It is a mistake to treat this work as beneath you, because a substantial part of what makes a data scientist effective is developed here.

**SQL and data modelling.** Fluency with joins, grain, slowly changing dimensions and the shape of transactional data is the foundation of everything afterwards, and it is tested in nearly every interview.

**Knowing where the data lies.** Which system is authoritative, which field was repurposed in 2021, which records are duplicated. Model quality depends on this more than on algorithm choice.

**Domain understanding.** You learn how the business actually works, because you answer questions about it every week.

**Stakeholder skill.** Turning a vague request into a defined question is the single most common failure point in data projects, and reporting work drills it constantly.

**Delivery habits.** Definitions, documentation, version control of logic, communicating change. Unglamorous and exactly what distinguishes a hireable data scientist from a capable student.

## What It Does Not Build

Experimentation. Validation design. Production engineering. Model monitoring. The judgement that comes from having a model behave differently in production than in your notebook.

Left alone, a reporting role develops none of these, and after three years the gap is visible. Interviewers ask about validation and drift, and the honest answer becomes "I have not done that in a work setting", which is survivable once and not twice.

## The Honest Risk: The Ladder That Is Not There

The failure mode is not that the employer lies. It is that the predictive work never becomes anyone's priority, because reporting demand is infinite and immediate while modelling is speculative and can always start next quarter.

Meanwhile you become the person who knows the reporting layer, and that knowledge makes you harder to release. The better you are at the role, the more expensive it becomes for the organisation to let you move. Three years pass in a way that feels reasonable each quarter.

## Signals the Move Is Real

- **Something predictive is already in production somewhere in the organisation,** in any team.
- **Someone has made this move internally before,** and you can be told their name and what they work on now.
- **There is a named problem waiting** — churn, demand, maintenance, fraud — with a business owner who wants it.
- **The data platform can support it:** history retained, pipelines that run, an environment where models can actually be deployed.
- **The training budget and time are contractual,** not aspirational.
- **The team structure has somewhere to go,** rather than a single reporting job with a hopeful title.

## Signals It Is Not

- Nothing predictive exists anywhere and nobody can name a candidate problem.
- "We are still getting the basics right" — often true, and often true indefinitely.
- The reporting backlog is enormous and you would be the only person on it.
- The manager is enthusiastic about your ambition but cannot describe what would need to happen.
- Analytics reports into a function with no appetite for uncertainty in outputs.
- The last three people in the role are all still doing reporting.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **What predictive work exists in this organisation today?**
- **Has anyone moved from this role into modelling work? What are they doing now?**
- **What problem would be first, and who owns it on the business side?**
- **How much of my week would be reporting in year one, and in year two?**
- **Is historical data retained in a form that would support modelling?**
- **What is the training budget and the time allowance, and is it in the contract?**
- **Who would review technical work if I built a model?**

## How to Convert a Reporting Role Into a Modelling One

If you accept, treat the transition as a project you run rather than a promise you wait on.

**Do the reporting work exceptionally well first.** Credibility is the currency, and nobody grants modelling latitude to someone whose dashboards are late.

**Choose prediction-shaped questions.** Many reporting requests are forecasting questions in disguise: how many will we need next month, which of these is likely to fail, where will demand be. Answer one properly — with a baseline, a holdout period and an honest error measure — and present it as an extension of the reporting you already do.

**Make it small and useful.** A modest, well-validated forecast that a planner actually uses is worth more than an ambitious model nobody adopts.

**Ask for a defined allocation.** A day a week, or a quarter of your time for one project, agreed with your manager and written down.

**Build the evidence.** Document what you did, what it achieved and what you learned. This is what convinces the next employer, and often this one.

**Find review.** A colleague in engineering, a peer group, an external reviewer. Unreviewed modelling produces confident mistakes.

**Set a horizon.** Eighteen to twenty-four months. If no modelling work has materialised by then despite your having created the opportunity, the organisation has answered.

## How Employers Read This Experience Later

Recruiters and hiring managers do not dismiss reporting backgrounds — a great many senior Dutch data scientists have one. What they look for is evidence that you moved beyond it: a project with a validation design, a metric you can defend, a system that ran on a schedule, a decision that changed because of your work.

Two candidates with three years in a reporting team can look completely different on paper. One lists dashboards and tools. The other lists a demand forecast that reduced manual planning effort, with the method and the evaluation described in two sentences. The second is not more talented; they were more deliberate.

## Reading the Vacancy Before You Apply

Job titles in this area are close to meaningless in the Dutch market. "Data scientist" is used for reporting roles, "BI specialist" occasionally covers production modelling, and "analytics engineer" means different things at different employers. The description, not the title, tells you what the work is.

**Look at the verbs.** Build, deploy, monitor, retrain and validate describe modelling work. Report, visualise, maintain, gather requirements and support describe reporting work. A vacancy full of the second set with one sentence about machine learning at the end is a reporting role.

**Look at the tools listed.** A stack centred on a reporting platform and a data warehouse, with no mention of how models run, tells you what exists today. A vacancy mentioning orchestration, model serving or experiment tracking implies someone is already doing this work.

**Look at who you would work with.** "Part of the BI team, reporting to the finance manager" places the role in reporting. "Working with data engineers and the platform team" places it closer to production.

**Look at what already runs.** Vacancies that describe existing models — what they predict, for whom — are describing reality. Vacancies that describe ambitions in the future tense are describing a hope.

**Notice the word "also".** "You will build dashboards and also explore predictive possibilities" almost always means the first part is the job and the second is the aspiration, because the first has deadlines and the second does not.

None of this means you should skip such vacancies. It means you should apply knowing which job you are applying for, and ask the questions in the previous section at the first interview rather than discovering the answer in month four.

## Real example

### A career changer in Breda who turned one question into a model

A teacher retraining into data work takes an analyst role at a wholesaler, twenty-five minutes from home. The job is dashboards, and the interview included the familiar sentence about scope for predictive work. She asks the questions first: nothing predictive exists, but the commercial director has complained for years about stock in seasonal categories, and historical data goes back eight years.

She spends nine months doing reporting properly and building a reputation for definitions people trust. Then she takes the stock complaint and treats it as a question: a simple seasonal forecast for one category group, validated on the previous two years against what the planners actually did.

It is slightly better than the manual approach, and much faster. The planners adopt it for that category, then for two more.

By her second year, a third of her time is forecasting work, and the company has hired an analyst to take on some of the reporting. When she interviews elsewhere at four years, the conversation is entirely about the forecasting, the validation and the adoption problem she solved — not about the dashboards she built first.

## Key Takeaways

- Reporting roles are numerous across the Netherlands and build genuinely valuable foundations: SQL, data knowledge, domain understanding, stakeholder skill and delivery habits.
- They do not build experimentation, validation design, production engineering or monitoring, and that gap becomes visible in interviews after a few years.
- The move stalls not because employers lie but because reporting demand is immediate and modelling is always postponable.
- Check for existing predictive work, a named first problem, someone who made the move before, retained history and a contractual training allowance.
- If you accept, deliver reporting excellently, convert one request into a properly validated prediction, secure a defined time allocation and set an eighteen-to-twenty-four-month horizon.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: career changer weighing a first role) Is a dashboard job a good way into machine learning?
It can be an excellent first step, because it builds SQL, data knowledge and stakeholder skill. It becomes a dead end when no predictive work exists and nobody can name a first problem.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing promises) How do I tell whether the promised move is real?
Ask what predictive work exists today, whether anyone has made the move internally, which problem would be first and who owns it on the business side.

### (Scenario: candidate already in reporting) How do I move into modelling from inside the role?
Deliver reporting well, convert one recurring request into a properly validated prediction, secure a written time allocation and find someone to review your technical work.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about time) How long should I give it?
Eighteen to twenty-four months. If you have created opportunities and none were taken up, the organisation has given you its answer.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking about their CV) How do employers view a reporting background?
Positively, provided you can show one project with a validation design, a defensible metric and real adoption. Tools and dashboards alone do not distinguish you.
