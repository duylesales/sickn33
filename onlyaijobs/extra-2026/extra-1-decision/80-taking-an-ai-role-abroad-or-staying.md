The offer is somewhere else: a research group in Switzerland, a product company in Berlin, a team in London or Dublin, occasionally something further. The work is more specialised than anything available here, or the salary is larger by a margin that seems to settle the argument, or you have simply wanted to live somewhere else for a while. Moving abroad for AI work is a reasonable thing to do and a decision people routinely make on the wrong information — usually a gross salary comparison, which is the least reliable number in the entire calculation.

## The Money Illusion

A gross salary in another country tells you almost nothing. What matters is what remains after tax and social contributions, after housing in the city you would actually live in, after healthcare arrangements that differ from the Dutch system, after childcare that may cost several times what it does here or a fraction of it, and after the pension you may no longer be accruing.

Do the calculation properly before anything else. Net monthly income, minus housing for a comparable place, minus healthcare, minus childcare, minus transport, and then look at what is left beside your current figure. Salaries that appear to be half again as large frequently end up roughly level; some end up lower. Occasionally they really are substantially better, and then you know it rather than hoping it.

Add one line most people omit: the cost of moving back. Shipping, deposits, a gap between jobs, and re-establishing yourself in a housing market that may have moved.

## What Happens to Your Dutch Position

**Occupational pension accrual stops** when you stop working for a Dutch employer. Your existing entitlement remains, and the years abroad are simply years you did not build up here.

**State pension build-up is tied to residence and, in some cases, to insured employment.** Living outside the Netherlands generally means you stop accruing for that period, which reduces the eventual entitlement. Voluntary arrangements may exist; the **Sociale Verzekeringsbank** is the body to ask, and it is worth asking before you go rather than after.

**Social security coordination.** Within the EU, Regulation 883/2004 determines where you are insured, generally the country where you work. Outside the EU, treaties or their absence decide, and the differences can be substantial.

**Health insurance.** The Dutch obligation ends when you are no longer insured here, and the replacement arrangement varies enormously by country.

**Housing.** Renting out a Dutch home has tax and mortgage implications, and selling in order to leave means re-entering the market later on its terms.

**Registration and administration.** Deregistering, and later registering again, affects tax position, benefits and entitlements in ways worth checking in advance.

None of this is an argument against going. It is an argument for spending an hour with a qualified adviser before deciding, which is a small cost against a decision of this size.

## The Career Argument For Going

**Work that does not exist here.** Certain research areas, certain scales of system, certain industries have few or no Dutch employers. If your specialism is one of them, the honest answer may be that the interesting version of your career is abroad.

**Depth of team.** Large concentrations of senior people in one place mean more to learn from than most Dutch employers can offer.

**A genuinely different environment.** Working in another professional culture teaches you things about your own assumptions that are difficult to learn otherwise.

**Compensation, in a minority of cases.** For some specialisms and some locations, the difference survives the full calculation comfortably.

## The Career Argument For Staying

**The Dutch market rewards local depth.** Domain knowledge of Dutch sectors, familiarity with local regulation, and enough language to work with stakeholders are worth a great deal here, and they depreciate while you are away.

**Returning gets harder over time.** After two or three years abroad, returning is straightforward. After seven or eight, you are re-entering a market where your network has thinned, your domain knowledge is dated and employers assess you as an unknown.

**Conditions here are good and easy to undervalue.** Working hours, holiday, parental arrangements, a functioning public system and the general expectation that a job is part of a life rather than the whole of it. People who move to higher-paying markets often discover they traded these away without noticing.

**Proximity to everything.** Family, friends, the support network that matters when something goes wrong. This is the factor people most often underweight at the point of decision and most often cite when returning.

## EU and Non-EU Moves Are Different Decisions

Within the EU and the wider free movement area, the move is administratively light: you can take the job, your partner can work, and social security coordination rules cover much of the complexity. Returning is equally simple.

Outside it, everything is heavier. A visa tied to an employer, sponsorship that constrains your ability to change jobs, a partner who may not be permitted to work, dependants' status, healthcare arrangements and a return that is a full relocation rather than a move. These roles can still be worth taking, and the questions to ask are more numerous and the answers more consequential.

## Questions to Ask the Employer

- **What exactly does the relocation package cover** — moving costs, temporary housing, flights, a return trip if it does not work out?
- **Is tax and social security advice provided?** Serious employers hiring internationally arrange this.
- **What is the notice period, and what happens to my residence status if the role ends?** Outside the EU, this question is critical.
- **Can my partner work, and does the employer help?**
- **What is the salary relative to local levels, and what does housing cost near the office?**
- **How many international hires are in the team, and how long do they stay?**
- **Is there a route back?** Some international employers have Dutch or European offices, which makes a later return much simpler.

## When Going Is the Right Call

- Your specialism genuinely has few employers in the Netherlands.
- The role offers depth, scale or a team you cannot find here.
- The full financial calculation, not the gross comparison, is clearly favourable.
- You are early enough in your career that a few years abroad costs little in local standing.
- Your household situation supports it, including your partner's work.
- You want to live somewhere else, which is a perfectly good reason on its own.

## When Staying Is the Right Call

- The comparable work exists here, within a distance you would travel.
- The calculation is roughly neutral once housing and pension are included.
- Your value is built on Dutch domain knowledge and relationships.
- Your household has commitments — a partner's career, schooling, care responsibilities — that the move would damage.
- You would be going mainly to escape a specific job rather than towards something.

## If You Go, Do These Things

Keep your Dutch network alive deliberately: former colleagues, a professional community, occasional visits. Returning is a matter of who remembers you.

Take advice before leaving on pension, tax and social security, and again before returning.

Set a horizon, even a loose one. People who move with a rough plan tend to make deliberate decisions at the three- or five-year mark; people who move open-endedly often find the decision made for them by inertia.

And do work that is legible from here. International experience impresses Dutch employers most when it is a system, a scale or a domain they recognise — not a role that requires three paragraphs to explain.

## The Third Option, and Its Limits

Before choosing between moving and staying, most people ask whether they can have both: take the foreign role and work remotely from the Netherlands. Sometimes this is possible, and it is worth understanding what it does and does not solve.

**What it solves.** You keep your home, your network, your partner's career and your family proximity, while working on problems that have no local equivalent.

**What it does not solve.** The foreign employer must be able to employ you here, which usually means an employer of record, a local entity or a contractor arrangement, each with consequences for your social security, pension and protection. Your salary will often be set against Dutch levels rather than the foreign market, which removes much of the financial argument. And you carry the time zone: an American or Asian team means your evenings or your early mornings belong to work.

**What it costs professionally.** The depth-of-team argument — the reason many people go abroad — depends substantially on being in the room. Remote participation in a team centred elsewhere gives you the work and much less of the learning, and your visibility for promotion sits with the people in the office.

**When it genuinely works.** Where the employer is remote-first rather than centred in one place, where the overlap is workable, where the employment arrangement is properly set up, and where you would travel to the team regularly.

If a remote arrangement is your preference, negotiate it as a deliberate structure rather than as a concession, and settle the employment, social security and travel questions in writing before starting. Treated as an afterthought, it tends to deliver the disadvantages of both options.

## Real example

### An engineer in Delft who did the full calculation

An engineer working on computer vision receives an offer from a company in Zurich. The gross salary is considerably higher than his Dutch one, and the work is on a scale of deployment he cannot find here.

He does the arithmetic properly. Higher taxes than he expected in his canton, housing that costs roughly double what he pays now, health insurance arranged differently, and no Dutch pension accrual for the duration. The net advantage is real but far smaller than the gross comparison suggested.

Then he weighs the parts that are not money. His partner works in Dutch healthcare and her qualification does not transfer easily. His specialism, however, has perhaps five serious employers in the Netherlands, and he has worked for two of them.

They agree on a compromise: he asks the Swiss employer whether the role can be reviewed after two years, with the possibility of moving to their European office. The answer is yes, informally, and he treats it as no more than that.

He goes for two years, works on a deployment larger than anything available here, and returns to a Dutch employer who hires him specifically because of it. His partner took unpaid leave for the period, which they had planned for.

His view: the decision was correct and the gross salary had nothing to do with it.

## Key Takeaways

- Gross salary comparisons across countries are meaningless; calculate net income after housing, healthcare, childcare and transport, and add the cost of moving back.
- Occupational pension accrual stops, state pension build-up is affected by residence, and social security follows where you work — take advice before deciding.
- Go when your specialism has few Dutch employers, when the depth or scale is unavailable here, or when you simply want to live elsewhere.
- Stay when comparable work exists within reach, when your value rests on Dutch domain knowledge and relationships, or when your household would carry the cost.
- EU moves are administratively light and reversible; non-EU moves involve sponsorship, partner work rights and a status that may depend on the job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate with a foreign offer) How should I compare a salary abroad with my Dutch one?
Never on gross figures. Calculate net income after tax and contributions, housing in the city you would live in, healthcare, childcare and transport, then add the cost of eventually moving back.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about pension) What happens to my pension if I work abroad?
Occupational accrual stops while you are not employed here, and state pension build-up is affected by residence and insured employment. Ask the SVB about your situation and any voluntary arrangements before leaving.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking about returning) How hard is it to come back?
Straightforward after two or three years, considerably harder after seven or eight, when your network has thinned and your Dutch domain knowledge has dated. Keep the network alive deliberately.

### (Scenario: candidate considering a non-EU move) What is different outside the EU?
Sponsorship ties your status to the employer, your partner may not be permitted to work, healthcare arrangements differ and the return is a full relocation. Ask what happens to your status if the role ends.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure of the reason) Is wanting to live elsewhere a good enough reason?
Yes, provided the financial and household calculations work. The poor reason is going mainly to escape a specific job, which travels with you more often than people expect.
