Somewhere in the second interview it slips out: the person who held this role left three weeks ago, rather suddenly, and the team is keen to fill the gap quickly. Sometimes that is exactly what it sounds like — someone received a better offer, or moved city, or went on parental leave and decided not to return. Sometimes it is the visible end of a situation that will be waiting for you on your first day: an impossible mandate, a manager nobody lasts under, a system held together by one person's unpaid evenings. The information you need to tell these apart is usually available, and asking for it is entirely normal. The mistake is treating a quick vacancy as a lucky break and not asking at all.

## Why People Actually Leave Data Roles

Most departures are unremarkable. In the Dutch market, the common reasons are a better offer, a shorter commute, a partner's job, parental leave, a return to study, or simply wanting different work after three or four years.

The reasons that should concern you are narrower and recognisable: nothing they built ever reached production, promises about budget or scope that never materialised, an unmanageable workload sustained by goodwill, a manager who could not be worked with, or an organisation that wanted "AI" and resented being told what that required.

The important distinction is not whether the departure was abrupt but whether the cause is structural. A person leaving for a better offer takes their reasons with them. A role that cannot be done stays exactly where it is.

## How to Ask Without Sounding Suspicious

The question is legitimate and expected. Ask it plainly, early, and more than once to different people.

To the hiring manager: *"What happened with the previous person in this role?"* Then, more usefully: *"What would they say was hardest about the job?"*

To a future colleague: *"How long was the last person here, and what did they find frustrating?"* Colleagues answer this honestly more often than managers do, which is one of several reasons to insist on a team conversation before accepting.

To the recruiter: *"How many people have held this role in the last three years?"* Recruiters usually know, and the number is far more informative than any explanation.

An employer with nothing to hide answers all three without discomfort. Evasiveness, irritation or a rehearsed answer repeated word for word by two people is itself the finding.

## The Numbers That Matter More Than the Story

**Tenure of the last two holders.** One short tenure is noise. Two in a row is a pattern that will probably include you.

**Time the role has existed.** A newly created role that already had one occupant leave quickly suggests the role was never properly defined.

**Size of the team and its turnover.** In a team of four, two departures in a year is a different signal from two in a department of forty.

**Whether the manager is new.** A manager who arrived after the departure changes the calculation substantially; a manager who has seen three people leave does not.

**How long the vacancy has been open.** A gap of months alongside an urgent tone suggests candidates have looked and declined.

## What a Sudden Departure Does to Your First Months

Even when the reason is benign, an abrupt exit has practical consequences you will inherit.

**No handover.** Nobody is available to explain what runs where, what the temporary fix in the pipeline was for, or why that model has a hard-coded threshold.

**Undocumented systems.** Documentation is the first thing to go when someone leaves quickly, and you will spend weeks reconstructing knowledge that existed in one person's head.

**Stakeholders mid-project.** People were waiting for something. You arrive owing them a deliverable you did not agree to, on a timeline you did not set.

**Inflated expectations of speed.** The organisation has been without the function for weeks and wants momentum immediately, which is precisely when you need time to understand things.

**Emotional residue.** If the departure was unhappy, colleagues may be wary, protective of their own areas, or quick to tell you their version.

## What to Ask For Before Accepting

**A handover conversation with the predecessor, even one hour.** Employers rarely offer this and frequently arrange it when asked. If the relationship ended badly and it is impossible, that is informative too.

**A written scope for the first three months,** so that the expectations you inherit are the ones you agreed rather than the ones the organisation accumulated while waiting.

**An explicit statement of what is currently broken or unfinished.** Ask directly: what will I find on day one that is not working?

**Access to whatever documentation exists,** before you sign if possible. Its quality tells you what your first month will look like.

**Confirmation of who the sponsor is now.** Sponsors sometimes leave with the person they sponsored.

## When to Walk Away

- Two or more short tenures in the same role, whatever the explanations.
- Nobody will discuss the departure at all.
- The team conversation you requested is refused, and this is the role where it matters most.
- The reason given by the manager and the reason given by a colleague are incompatible.
- The role clearly requires more than one person and the employer insists otherwise.
- The predecessor left because promises were not kept, and the same promises are being made to you.

## When It Is Genuinely Fine

- A single departure with a mundane, verifiable explanation.
- A predecessor who stayed several years first.
- A new manager who has already changed something concrete.
- An employer who volunteers the difficult parts without being asked.
- Colleagues who speak about the departed person warmly and without tension.
- A role that was recently rescoped in response to what went wrong.

That last point is worth emphasising. An organisation that lost someone, thought about why, and changed the role before advertising it again is often a better employer than one that has never had to.

## The Advantage Nobody Mentions

An urgent vacancy gives you leverage you would not otherwise have. The employer is under time pressure, the cost of the gap is visible to them, and alternatives are scarce.

Use it for structural things rather than only for salary: the scope in writing, a named sponsor, budget for tooling, a handover conversation, agreement that the first month is for understanding rather than delivering. These requests are easy to grant under pressure and almost impossible to obtain once you have started.

## Your First Ninety Days in a Vacated Role

Even when the departure was benign, stepping into an empty seat has a predictable shape, and planning for it prevents the most common mistakes.

**Weeks one to three: inventory, not delivery.** Find out what runs, where, and who depends on it. Resist the pressure to produce something immediately; a wrong change to an inherited system costs more than a slow start.

**Weeks three to six: meet the stakeholders individually.** Each will tell you what they were promised. Collect those promises before deciding which ones you will keep, and say explicitly that you are collecting rather than committing.

**Weeks six to ten: reset expectations once, in writing.** A short note listing what you found, what you will do in the coming quarter and what you are not doing yet. This is the single most valuable document you will write, because it replaces the accumulated assumptions of the gap period with something explicit.

**Weeks ten to thirteen: deliver something small and visible.** Not the big project — a fixed report, a documented pipeline, an automated step. It establishes that the function works again, which is what the organisation is anxious about.

Above all, avoid inheriting your predecessor's reputation. If they were seen as slow, your early caution will be read the same way unless you explain what you are doing and why.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Utrecht who asked three people the same question

An ML engineer interviews for a role at a logistics company where the previous data scientist left after seven months. The manager's explanation is that the person "wanted to go back to research".

He asks a future colleague the same question. The answer is different: the previous person had been promised a data engineer who was never hired, and had spent most of the role maintaining reports alone.

Rather than withdrawing, he raises it directly with the manager. The conversation is uncomfortable and honest: the engineer had indeed not been hired, the budget now exists, and recruitment for that role is underway.

He asks for three things in writing — the data engineer hire as a condition, the first three months scoped as stabilisation rather than new models, and a named sponsor in operations. The employer agrees to all three.

The data engineer starts a month after him. Two years later both are still there. Had he not asked the second person, he would have accepted the same role that had just broken someone else.

## Look Beyond Your Own Role

The departure you were told about is one data point. The pattern around it is more informative, and it is usually visible if you ask.

Ask how long the team as a whole has been together. A data team where everyone arrived in the last eighteen months has either grown quickly or lost people quickly, and those look identical on an organisation chart.

Ask about the manager's own tenure, and their manager's. Stability two levels up matters, because reorganisations and strategy reversals arrive from there.

Ask what has changed in the team's mandate over the past two years. Repeated redefinition — from analytics to AI to reporting and back — wears people out more reliably than any single bad manager.

And notice who is not in your interview process. If you never meet anyone who has been there more than a year, that is a finding rather than a scheduling accident.

## If You Discover the Truth After Joining

Sometimes the honest picture only appears in week three, when a colleague explains what really happened. If the cause turns out to be structural, act early rather than waiting to see.

Raise it with your manager as a question about the role rather than as an accusation: the previous person struggled with X; what has changed? Ask for the scope you agreed to be confirmed in writing if it was not already. Use your probation period, which is short and legally capped in the Netherlands, as a genuine decision point rather than a formality.

Most importantly, avoid the trap of trying to prove that you can succeed where someone else failed under the same conditions. If the conditions have not changed, the outcome usually does not either.

## Key Takeaways

- Most departures are mundane; the ones that matter are structural — impossible mandates, broken promises, unmanageable workload or a difficult manager.
- Ask the hiring manager, a future colleague and the recruiter the same question, and compare the answers.
- Tenure of the last two holders, team turnover and how long the vacancy has been open are more informative than any explanation.
- Expect no handover, undocumented systems, waiting stakeholders and pressure for immediate momentum.
- Use the employer's urgency to secure scope in writing, a named sponsor, a handover conversation and time to understand before delivering.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate hearing about a sudden departure) Is it a red flag if the previous person left abruptly?
Not on its own. Most departures are mundane. The concern is a pattern, or a structural cause such as broken promises or an impossible workload.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure how to raise it) How do I ask why the last person left?
Ask plainly and ask more than one person: the hiring manager, a future colleague and the recruiter. Compare the answers rather than relying on one.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about turnover) What number of departures should concern me?
Two short tenures in the same role in a row, whatever the explanations, is the clearest signal that the problem is the role rather than the people.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting context) Can I speak to the person who left?
You can ask the employer to arrange a handover conversation, which is often granted when requested at the offer stage and rarely offered unprompted.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating) What should I ask for in an urgent vacancy?
Scope for the first three months in writing, a named sponsor, a handover, an honest list of what is currently broken, and time to understand before delivering.
