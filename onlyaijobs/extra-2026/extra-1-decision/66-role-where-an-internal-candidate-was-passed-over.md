It usually comes out sideways. A hiring manager mentions that "we also looked internally", or a future colleague says something slightly too carefully during the team interview, or you find out on your first day that the person sitting opposite you applied for the role you just accepted. This is common, particularly for lead and senior positions, and it is one of the few situations where the quality of your first year depends less on the work than on one relationship. It is rarely a reason to decline. It is always a reason to ask more questions before you do.

## How You Usually Find Out

**You are told during the process.** The best case by a distance. It means the organisation is handling the situation deliberately and has probably told the internal candidate too.

**You infer it from the team interview.** A colleague who asks pointed questions about your experience, or who describes the role as though they had thought about doing it themselves.

**You discover it afterwards.** The worst case, because it means nobody planned for the aftermath — and you spend your first month working out why a particular colleague is cool with you.

If it has not been mentioned by the final stage, ask. "Were there internal candidates for this role?" is a normal question, and the way it is answered is as useful as the answer.

## Why Organisations Hire Externally Anyway

There are legitimate reasons, and knowing which one applies helps you understand the position you are walking into.

**A capability gap.** The internal person is strong but has not done the specific thing the organisation now needs — production systems, a regulated domain, leading a team.

**Scale of change.** Where the mandate is to change how things are done, someone with no history in the existing arrangements can do it more easily.

**Seniority.** The internal candidate is a year or two away from the level, and the organisation cannot wait.

**Politics.** The internal candidate is disliked, or has a history with someone senior. This is the version nobody states, and it should make you curious about the surrounding dynamics.

**Ambition management.** Occasionally the internal candidate applied knowing they would not get it, to signal interest for next time. These situations resolve themselves quickly.

The first three are ordinary and manageable. The fourth deserves careful attention, because a team where someone is being quietly sidelined is a team with a problem that will eventually involve you.

## What Is Normal in the Netherlands

Many Dutch employers advertise vacancies internally first, sometimes as a matter of policy and sometimes under provisions in a collective agreement, and in some organisations the works council has a role in appointment procedures. Recruitment practice is also guided by the **NVP recruitment code**, which sets expectations about transparency and timely, reasoned communication to applicants — internal ones included.

What this means practically: in a well-run organisation, the internal candidate has had a proper conversation, a reason and ideally a development plan. In a badly run one, they received a short email while you were being welcomed. You are not responsible for which of those happened, and you will live with the consequences either way, so it is worth knowing which it was.

## The Three Situations You Might Be Joining

**The gracious colleague.** Disappointed, professional, sometimes relieved. They will help you, and the relationship recovers within months. This is the most common outcome, and candidates worry about it more than it deserves.

**The wounded colleague.** Cooperative in public, unhelpful in the specifics: information withheld, context not shared, a slight delay in every answer. Not malicious, and genuinely costly, because they hold the knowledge you need.

**The departing colleague.** They have already decided to leave and are working out their notice period internally. You inherit their systems without their knowledge, which is a practical problem rather than an emotional one — and a reason to accelerate handover immediately.

Ask the hiring manager, directly, which they think it is. A manager who has thought about it will have an answer and a plan; one who says "it will be fine" has not.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **Were there internal candidates, and has the decision been communicated to them?**
- **Would that person report to me, be my peer, or sit elsewhere?** Reporting to you is a much bigger consideration than being a colleague.
- **What was the reason they were not selected, in general terms?** You do not need details, but "not ready for the level" and "we needed someone from outside" mean different things.
- **What was offered to them instead?** A development plan, a different role, a conversation about next time — or nothing.
- **How does the manager plan to support both of us in the first months?**
- **Do they hold critical knowledge that I will depend on?**
- **Is there any expectation that I will change how they work?**

## The First Ninety Days

The relationship is decided early, and mostly by small things.

**Acknowledge it once, briefly, if they raise it.** Not an apology — you did nothing wrong — but a plain acknowledgement that the situation is awkward and that you would like to work well together. Then let it go.

**Do not pretend you do not know.** Nothing damages trust faster than obvious pretence about something everyone is aware of.

**Ask for their expertise, genuinely and often.** They know things you do not, and treating that knowledge as valuable is both true and the fastest route to a working relationship.

**Do not change everything in month one.** A new external hire rearranging the team's practices immediately confirms every fear anyone had about the appointment.

**Give credit in public, precisely.** Specific, attributed credit for their work, in front of the people whose opinion matters to them.

**If they report to you, have the career conversation early.** What they want, what the gap is, and what you can do about it. Managers who avoid this conversation inherit a slow-burning problem; managers who have it in the first month often gain their most committed team member.

## What Not to Do

Do not raise it repeatedly, which makes it larger. Do not use it as an explanation for every difficulty in the team. Do not go to the manager about their attitude in the first weeks, before you have built any relationship yourself. And do not treat their knowledge as an obstacle to be routed around — it is the asset you were hired to work with.

## When to Accept

- You were told during the process, openly.
- The manager has a plan and can describe it.
- The internal candidate was offered something concrete.
- They would be a peer rather than a direct report, or you are experienced enough to manage the latter.
- The reason for the external hire is a capability or seniority gap you can explain without insulting anyone.
- The rest of the team seemed open in the interview.

## When to Be Careful

- You discovered it by accident, late.
- The internal candidate is well-liked, long-serving, and was given no explanation.
- They hold all the operational knowledge and are already disengaged.
- The manager is avoiding the subject.
- The unstated reason appears to be a personal conflict with someone senior.
- You would be managing them, you have not managed before, and no support is on offer.

## If They Leave Anyway

Some do, and it is not a failure on your part. Plan for the possibility: prioritise knowledge transfer in your first weeks, document what only they know, and make sure critical systems are not dependent on a single person's memory.

If they leave well, keep the relationship. The Dutch data community is small, and a colleague who left gracefully after being passed over frequently becomes a useful contact — occasionally the person hiring at your next employer.

## What to Watch During the Team Interview

Most candidates meet the team before the offer, and that conversation contains the evidence if you know what to look for.

**Someone who interviews you rather than meeting you.** Pointed questions about your experience, comparisons with how things are done now, or a tone of assessment from a peer rather than curiosity.

**A colleague who describes the role in the first person.** Phrases like "the way I would run this team" are a strong indication that they thought seriously about doing it.

**Careful language about the previous structure.** If people describe how the team currently works in guarded terms, something recently changed or was decided.

**The manager's framing.** Listen for whether they describe what the team needs that it does not currently have. A manager who can articulate the gap clearly has usually explained it internally too.

**Who is not in the room.** If an obvious senior team member is absent from the interview process, ask why. The answer is occasionally a holiday and occasionally the story.

None of these is disqualifying. They are prompts to ask the direct question before you decide rather than after, when the answer would arrive as a surprise in your first week.

## Real example

### A lead in Arnhem who asked the question in the final interview

A senior engineer is offered a team lead role at a utility company. In the final conversation she asks whether there were internal candidates. There was one: the longest-serving engineer on the team, passed over because he had never led people and the organisation wanted the team structured differently within a year.

She asks what he was offered. A senior technical track position with a pay adjustment, agreed before the external search was completed, and a development plan if he still wants to lead later.

She asks the manager how he plans to support both of them. He describes a joint conversation in week one and a handover of the platform ownership that the engineer will keep rather than transfer.

She accepts. The first weeks are cool but civil. She asks him to walk her through the platform he built, listens for two hours without suggesting anything, and leaves the ownership with him publicly.

Eighteen months later he is her deputy and the person she relies on most. Her summary is that she did almost nothing clever — she asked the question before accepting, and then treated his knowledge as an asset rather than a threat.

## Key Takeaways

- Ask directly whether there were internal candidates; if it has not come up by the final stage, the way the question is answered is itself informative.
- The reason matters: a capability or seniority gap is ordinary, while an unstated personal conflict signals a team problem you would inherit.
- Establish whether they would be a peer or a direct report, whether they were offered anything concrete, and whether they hold knowledge you will depend on.
- In the first ninety days, acknowledge it once, ask for their expertise, change nothing quickly and give precise public credit.
- Be careful when you discovered it late, when nothing was offered to them, or when you would manage them without support.

## Where to Start

If a role comes with this dynamic, weigh it against comparable roles near you where it does not — and ask the question early enough that it informs your decision rather than your first month.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate suspecting an internal applicant) Should I ask whether there were internal candidates?
Yes, at the final stage. It is a normal question, and how it is answered tells you whether the organisation has handled the situation deliberately.

### (Scenario: candidate offered a lead role) Does it matter whether they would report to me?
Considerably. A peer relationship usually settles within months; managing the person who wanted your job requires an early career conversation and support from your own manager.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about the team) Is this a reason to decline?
Rarely on its own. Be careful when the person was given no explanation, holds all the operational knowledge and is disengaged, or when the real reason appears to be a personal conflict.

### (Scenario: new joiner in the first weeks) How should I handle the first month?
Acknowledge the situation once if it is raised, ask for their expertise genuinely, avoid changing things immediately and give specific public credit for their work.

### (Scenario: candidate whose colleague resigns) What if they leave anyway?
Plan for it: prioritise knowledge transfer early, document what only they know, and part on good terms — the Dutch data community is small.
