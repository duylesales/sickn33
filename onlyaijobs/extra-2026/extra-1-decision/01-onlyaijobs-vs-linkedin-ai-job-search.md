Most AI and data professionals in the Netherlands run their job search on LinkedIn, for an understandable reason: that is where their network, their profile and their recruiter messages already live. The question worth asking is not whether LinkedIn is useful — it clearly is — but what it was built to optimise, and whether that matches what you are trying to do. LinkedIn is a professional network with a jobs product attached. OnlyAIJobs is a job board that does one thing: list AI, machine learning and data vacancies, shown at exact address level with the distance from where you live. Those are different products solving different parts of the same problem, and the practical answer for most candidates is not "either" but "both, for different purposes".

## What Each Platform Optimises For

LinkedIn optimises for engagement and reach. Its jobs product benefits from an enormous catalogue across every profession, a matching system that ranks roles based on your profile and behaviour, and tools that let employers promote listings to more candidates. That combination is genuinely powerful when you want volume, when you are open to many types of roles, or when you want recruiters to find you.

OnlyAIJobs optimises for something narrower: making relevant local vacancies findable. Every listing is an AI, machine learning or data role. Every listing shows where the employer actually is, not just the city name, with the distance from your home. There is no mechanism for an employer to buy a higher position — the platform's stated aim is a level playing field where a five-person ML team is not pushed down the page by a multinational's advertising budget.

Neither model is inherently better. They fail in different ways, which is exactly why comparing them is useful.

## Where LinkedIn Genuinely Wins

**Passive discovery.** If you are not actively searching, LinkedIn is where opportunities find you. That is a real advantage no niche board replicates.

**Breadth.** For adjacent roles — product management, consulting, engineering leadership — a general platform covers ground a specialist board does not.

**People, not just postings.** Seeing who works at a company, who moved where and who you know in common is genuinely useful before an application.

**Employer research.** Company pages, employee posts and mutual connections help you judge an organisation before you apply.

**Reach beyond the Netherlands.** For international searches, the scale is difficult to match.

## Where a Specialist Board Wins

**Precision instead of keyword noise.** "AI" is a keyword that broadens reach, which is why general searches return roles where machine learning appears in the fifth bullet of a generic software engineering posting, alongside sales roles at AI companies and marketing roles mentioning AI tools. When every listing on a platform is already an AI, ML or data role, the filtering problem largely disappears.

**Location at address level.** This is the difference most candidates underestimate. Two vacancies both labelled "Amsterdam" can mean a twelve-minute cycle or a seventy-minute door-to-door commute. A platform that shows the actual address and the distance from your home lets you filter on the thing that will shape your daily life, rather than on a label.

**No pay-to-win ranking.** On platforms where promotion is available, visibility partly reflects recruitment budget. A smaller employer with one interesting vacancy can be effectively invisible. Removing paid prominence changes which employers you actually see.

**Fewer distractions.** A job board does not show you posts, notifications or messages. For focused searching, that is a feature.

## The Job Title Problem Neither Platform Solves Completely

AI job titles in the Netherlands are inconsistent. The same work appears as data scientist, machine learning engineer, algorithm developer, research engineer, quality specialist or systems engineer depending on the sector. In manufacturing, pharmaceutical and public organisations especially, the most interesting technical work often carries a title that never mentions AI.

On a general platform, this means title-based searches miss relevant roles and return irrelevant ones. On a specialist board, it means you should search by category rather than by exact title — the category filter exists precisely because titles cannot be trusted. Either way, the practical advice is the same: search the work, not the word.

## What OnlyAIJobs Does Not Do

Being honest about limitations is part of a useful comparison.

**It is not a network.** There are no connections, posts or messages. If you want to be approached passively, that happens elsewhere.

**The catalogue is specialised and therefore smaller.** A platform covering one field in one country will never match the raw volume of a global network. The trade-off is that a much higher share of what you see is relevant.

**Applications happen at the employer.** Listings link to the employer's own vacancy page rather than offering an application flow on the platform. That means one extra step — and it also means your application goes directly into the employer's process rather than into a queue of hundreds of quick applications.

**No profile is required.** Browsing is free and open, which is convenient, but it also means the platform is not doing anything on your behalf while you are not looking.

## Quick Applications Versus Considered Applications

One structural difference deserves attention. Platforms with one-click application flows make applying fast for candidates and, predictably, generate large application volumes for employers. Hiring managers for AI roles frequently describe reviewing long lists in which a small minority of applicants have read the posting carefully.

That has two consequences for you. First, your application competes against volume, so the marginal value of a tailored application rises. Second, employers increasingly value candidates who arrived through a channel that suggests genuine interest in that specific type of work. Applying directly on the employer's site, with a CV adapted to the role, is slower per application and usually more effective per application.

## What Pay Transparency Will Change on Both

The EU Pay Transparency Directive requires employers to give candidates information about the initial pay or its range before the interview and prohibits asking about pay history. As this is implemented, salary information should appear earlier in the process across all channels.

For your search, this is a practical filter: vacancies that state a range let you decide before investing hours. Whichever platform you use, treat a published range as a signal of an employer that has organised its pay structure, and a complete absence of salary information late in a process as a reason to ask early.

## Recruiter Messages: The Feature Only One Side Has

The steady flow of recruiter messages is the clearest thing a network platform offers that a job board does not. For experienced AI professionals it can be substantial, and it is worth handling deliberately rather than ignoring it or answering everything.

A message worth a reply names the company or at least the sector and team, describes the problem the role solves, states a salary range and location, and shows that someone read your profile rather than matching a keyword. A message worth ignoring is generic, withholds the company name after you ask, or pressures you to send a CV immediately.

Two habits protect you. First, never give blanket permission for your CV to be forwarded; agree per company, in writing. Second, keep your own list of where you have applied, because being presented twice to the same employer — once by yourself and once by an agency — can end a candidacy that was going well.

This is also where the two channels interact usefully. A recruiter message about an interesting company is a reason to check that employer's own listings and location yourself, rather than letting the intermediary frame the role for you.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Utrecht comparing two searches

An ML engineer living in Utrecht spends two weeks searching mainly on a general platform. Her saved searches return a steady stream of roles, most of them in Amsterdam, several from recruitment agencies, and a few where "AI" appears only in the company description. She applies to six roles through quick-apply flows and hears back from one.

In the third week she changes approach. She keeps the general platform for following companies and for the recruiter messages that occasionally lead somewhere, but she does her actual searching on a specialist board, sorted by distance from her home in Utrecht. Three things surprise her: a grid operator twenty minutes away with a forecasting role she had never seen, a medical device company in a town she had not considered searching, and a logistics firm whose vacancy is titled "planning analyst" but describes optimisation work.

She applies to three roles, each directly on the employer's site, with a CV adapted to the specific problem described in the posting. Two lead to interviews. The change was not effort — it was searching the market by the things that determine her daily life, distance and type of work, rather than by keyword.

## How to Combine Both Without Doubling the Work

A practical division of labour:

- **Use the network platform for people and passive discovery:** follow employers you like, keep your profile current, respond selectively to recruiters and research companies before applying.
- **Use the specialist board for actual searching:** browse by category rather than title, sort by distance, and save the roles worth a closer look.
- **Apply at the employer:** whichever channel surfaced the role, applying directly and deliberately beats applying quickly.
- **Track everything in one place:** saved jobs on a board plus a simple list of applications and dates prevents duplicate applications and forgotten follow-ups.
- **Review weekly:** which channel produced interviews, not just applications? Shift your time accordingly after a few weeks of evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate who already uses LinkedIn daily) Should I stop using LinkedIn for my AI job search?
No — it remains valuable for passive discovery, employer research and your network. Use a specialist board for the searching itself, where precision and location detail matter more.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about catalogue size) Isn't a smaller job board worse because it has fewer vacancies?
Fewer listings, but a much higher share that are genuinely AI, ML or data roles near you. The relevant measure is how many suitable roles you find, not how many results appear.

### (Scenario: candidate who wants to be found by recruiters) Can employers approach me through OnlyAIJobs?
No — it is a job board, not a network. Browsing is free and requires no profile, so passive discovery remains something a network platform does.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing application flows) Why does OnlyAIJobs link to the employer instead of offering quick apply?
Applications go directly into the employer's own process. It is one extra step, but it favours considered applications over volume.

### (Scenario: candidate who searches by job title) Why should I search by category instead of job title?
Because AI work in the Netherlands appears under titles such as algorithm developer, planning analyst or systems engineer. Category filters catch roles that title searches miss.
