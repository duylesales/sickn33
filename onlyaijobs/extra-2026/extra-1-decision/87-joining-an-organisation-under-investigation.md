You find it while preparing for the interview: a fine, an inquiry by a supervisory authority, a data breach that made the news, a report criticising an automated system, or a parliamentary question about how the organisation treated people. The role is still open and still interesting, and now it comes with context. These situations produce some of the best-funded and most consequential data work available in the Netherlands, and they also produce roles that exist so that somebody can point at them. Telling the two apart is a matter of a few specific questions.

## What Kind of Trouble Is It?

**A data breach.** Usually followed by a period of scrutiny, remediation and investment in security and data hygiene.

**A privacy investigation or enforcement action.** The **Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens** has been active on lawful basis, retention, transparency and automated decision-making, and an organisation under its attention has concrete obligations rather than vague intentions.

**Sector supervision.** Financial, healthcare, energy and public bodies each have their own supervisors, and the resulting programmes are typically well resourced and tightly deadlined.

**An algorithm controversy.** A system judged to have treated people unfairly. Dutch public administration has lived through this, and the consequences have been serious and lasting.

**Internal wrongdoing.** Fraud, integrity investigations, procurement issues — often with forensic data work attached.

**Employment or works council disputes,** sometimes about monitoring or reorganisation.

Each produces a different job. The first three usually mean building things properly. The fourth means rebuilding trust as much as systems.

## Why They Are Hiring

Remediation is the most common reason, and it is a good one. After enforcement or a public failure, organisations receive something they previously lacked: a mandate and a budget. Work that had been declined for years — a data register, retention cleanup, documentation, monitoring, oversight design, decommissioning of systems nobody could justify — suddenly becomes the priority.

For a data professional, this is often the most interesting version of governance work, because it is real, funded and urgent rather than theoretical.

The less good reason is optics: hiring a specialist so the organisation can say it has one, without changing the decisions that produced the problem.

## The Good Case

- There is a defined remediation programme with a sponsor at board level.
- Deadlines come from a regulator or an agreement, which means they do not slip quietly.
- Budget exists for tooling, external expertise and people.
- Legal, privacy and compliance colleagues are involved and technically literate.
- The organisation can describe what it did wrong, in its own words, without blaming a supplier or a junior employee.
- The remediation has already produced visible changes — systems switched off, processes stopped, practices changed.

In that environment, two or three years of work will teach you more about governance, documentation and oversight than a decade in an untroubled organisation, and it is directly marketable afterwards.

## The Bad Case

- Nobody will discuss what happened.
- The account you are given differs from what was published.
- The remediation has no named owner or no budget.
- The same people who made the decisions still make them, unchanged.
- You would be the governance function, alone, without authority.
- The organisation's energy is going into communication rather than into fixing anything.

A role in that setting exposes you to responsibility you cannot discharge, and the second failure will need an explanation from somebody.

## What the Work Actually Involves

Expect less modelling than the title suggests, at least initially.

**Inventory and evidence.** What systems exist, what data they hold, on what basis, for how long, and who decided. Most organisations cannot answer this at the start.

**Documentation.** Producing records that did not exist, to a standard that will be read by auditors and regulators rather than colleagues.

**Retention and minimisation.** Deleting what should not be kept, which is more difficult technically and politically than it sounds.

**Impact assessments and oversight design.** Working out who reviews what, when a human can override, and what happens then.

**Decommissioning.** Switching things off is frequently the most valuable remediation available.

**Working with external parties.** Auditors, external counsel, sometimes the supervisor. Precise writing matters here more than elegant code.

## Questions to Ask

- **What happened, in your own account?** Compare it with the public record.
- **What has changed since, concretely?**
- **Is there a remediation programme, who sponsors it and what is the deadline?**
- **What is the budget, and who controls it?**
- **Who would I report to, and do they have authority to stop a system?**
- **Are legal, privacy and compliance colleagues involved day to day?**
- **What was the hardest thing to fix, and is it fixed?**
- **Has anyone left over this?** The answer tells you whether accountability was real.
- **What would happen if I found something else?** The single most revealing question available.

## Your Own Position

Obligations under privacy and AI rules rest on the organisation rather than on individual employees, and the practical protections are the ones that apply in any sensitive work: raise concerns in writing, address them to the people whose role it is — the data protection officer, legal, internal audit — and keep your own record of what you reported and what was decided.

The Netherlands also has a framework for reporting suspected wrongdoing, under the **Whistleblower Protection Act**, which implements the EU directive and includes protection against detrimental treatment for people who report through the prescribed channels; larger employers are required to have an internal reporting procedure. It is worth knowing that this framework exists before you need it, and worth taking advice if you ever do — it is not a substitute for ordinary escalation, but it is not nothing either.

## The Reputation Question

Candidates worry that joining a troubled organisation looks bad. In practice, hiring managers read it the opposite way, provided you can describe what you did: arriving after a failure and helping to fix it is a credible, senior-sounding story, and organisations in difficulty attract people who are good at exactly the things everyone else neglects.

The version that reads badly is the one where you were there while it continued and cannot say what you did about it. That is an argument for choosing the well-mandated version of the role, not for avoiding the situation entirely.

## When to Accept

- The remediation is funded, sponsored and deadlined.
- The organisation describes its own failure plainly.
- You would have access to legal and privacy expertise.
- Your role has authority, or a clear escalation route to someone who has.
- The work would build documentation, oversight and governance experience you want.
- Visible changes have already happened.

## When to Decline

- The account you are given contradicts the public record.
- There is no programme, no budget and no owner.
- You would be the only person responsible for compliance, without authority.
- Decision makers are unchanged and unreflective.
- The answer to "what if I find something else" is uncomfortable.
- The role appears designed to be pointed at rather than to fix anything.

## What the First Hundred Days Look Like

Remediation roles have a recognisable shape, and knowing it makes the early months far less disorienting.

**Weeks one to four: inventory.** What systems exist, what data they process, on what basis, for how long, and who decided. In most organisations this has never been written down in one place, and producing it is the foundation for everything else — including your own understanding of what you have walked into.

**Weeks four to eight: the quick corrections.** Almost every inventory surfaces things that can be stopped immediately: a dataset nobody uses that contains personal data, an export that still runs to a system decommissioned last year, retention that nobody configured. Switching these off produces real risk reduction within weeks and buys credibility for the slower work.

**Weeks eight to sixteen: evidence and documentation.** Producing records to a standard that an auditor or supervisor will read rather than a standard that satisfies colleagues. Expect to write far more than you code, and expect the writing to be reviewed closely.

**Throughout: precision in communication.** In an organisation under scrutiny, an imprecise internal message can become an external problem. Write carefully, distinguish what you know from what you suspect, and route findings through the agreed channel rather than mentioning them in passing.

**Also throughout: resist the urge to fix everything.** A remediation programme has a scope agreed with someone, and work outside it competes with work that has a deadline attached. Log what you find, raise it properly, and let the programme decide.

By day one hundred the useful markers are: an inventory that did not exist, a handful of things switched off, and a documented understanding of where the remaining gaps are. That is a strong first quarter in this kind of role, even though none of it looks like machine learning.

## Real example

### An engineer in The Hague who asked what happened next time

A data engineer is approached by a public body that had been criticised in an external review of an automated system used to prioritise cases. The role is described as data governance and platform work.

He reads the published report before the interview, which turns out to be the most useful preparation he could have done, because it lets him ask specific questions.

The organisation's own account matches the report. Two systems were switched off during the review and one has not returned. There is a programme with a director accountable to the board, a deadline agreed with the supervisor, and an external auditor who reviews progress quarterly.

He asks what would happen if he found something else. The director's answer is procedural and unhesitating: it goes into the register, it is assessed within a fixed period, and the programme board decides — and two such findings have already come from employees.

He accepts. His first year is an inventory, a retention programme and documentation for systems that had none. It is not the machine learning role he imagined, and it is the work he now gets approached about by other organisations facing the same obligations.

## Key Takeaways

- Establish what kind of trouble it is: a breach, a privacy enforcement action, sector supervision, an algorithm controversy or internal wrongdoing each produce a different job.
- The good version comes with a mandate, a budget, a board-level sponsor and regulator-driven deadlines — conditions that rarely exist otherwise.
- The bad version is optics: a specialist hired so the organisation can say it has one, with the same decision makers unchanged.
- Expect inventory, documentation, retention, impact assessments, oversight design and decommissioning rather than modelling, at least at first.
- Ask what happened in their own words, what has changed concretely, and what would happen if you found something else — and keep written records of anything you raise.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate researching an employer) Is it a mistake to join an organisation under investigation?
Often not. Enforcement creates mandate and budget for work that is otherwise declined for years, and the experience is directly marketable. It is a mistake when nothing has actually changed.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the role) How do I tell remediation from decoration?
Ask whether there is a programme with a sponsor, a budget and a deadline, whether visible changes have already happened, and what would occur if you found a new problem.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about exposure) Am I personally at risk?
Obligations rest on the organisation. Protect your position by raising concerns in writing to the data protection officer, legal or internal audit, and keeping your own record of what was decided.

### (Scenario: candidate considering escalation) What if serious wrongdoing is not addressed?
The Netherlands has a statutory framework for reporting suspected wrongdoing with protection against detrimental treatment, and larger employers must have an internal procedure. Take advice before using it.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking about their CV) How does this look to future employers?
Good, if you can say what you did. Arriving after a failure and helping fix it reads as senior. Having been present while it continued, with nothing to show, does not.
