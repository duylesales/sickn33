You have spent four years doing one thing — language models, or computer vision, or forecasting, or building pipelines — and the interesting offer in front of you is for something adjacent but different. Or the roles near where you live are mostly in a specialism you have never worked in. Candidates tend to overestimate how much of their expertise is specific and underestimate how long it takes to be genuinely good at something new. The truth sits in between, and where exactly depends far more on which direction you are moving than on how capable you are.

## What Counts as a Switch

**Between modelling domains.** Language to vision, vision to tabular, tabular to time series. The methods differ; the discipline does not.

**Between problem types.** Prediction to optimisation, supervised learning to causal inference, classification to ranking. This is a bigger shift than people expect, because the way you frame a problem changes.

**Between layers of the stack.** Modelling to data engineering, or engineering to modelling. Different daily work, largely different tooling, overlapping fundamentals.

**Between research and applied work.** The largest gap in either direction, and the one employers scrutinise most.

## What Transfers More Than You Think

**Evaluation discipline.** Knowing how to build an honest test setup, what leakage looks like, why a metric is misleading and how to detect degradation. This is the single most valuable thing you own, and it is domain-independent.

**Data intuition.** The instinct that a distribution looks wrong, that a field changed meaning, that a result is too good. Built from experience with any data.

**Production practice.** Deployment, monitoring, versioning, rollback, incident handling. Identical across specialisms and in short supply everywhere.

**Stakeholder skill.** Turning a vague request into a defined problem, and explaining what a model can and cannot do.

**Statistical foundations.** Uncertainty, bias, sample size, confounding. Underneath every specialism.

If you have these, you are not starting again. You are starting two thirds of the way in.

## What Does Not Transfer

**Modelling intuition for the new domain.** Knowing which approach usually works, what the failure modes are, how much data you need and which shortcuts are safe. This takes real projects, not courses.

**Tooling and ecosystem.** Every specialism has its libraries, conventions and standard pipelines.

**Literature and benchmarks.** What counts as a good result, and what the field has already settled.

**Domain-specific pitfalls.** The seasonality traps in forecasting, the annotation problems in language work, the distribution shift issues in vision, the identification problems in causal work.

Expect six months to be functional and eighteen to be genuinely good. That is the honest cost, and it is smaller than changing careers entirely.

## Which Directions Are Easier

**Towards engineering: easiest.** Demand is high, the skills are verifiable, and a modeller who can build reliable pipelines is immediately useful. Many people make this move and few regret it.

**Between applied modelling domains: moderate.** A forecasting specialist moving to demand-side optimisation, or a vision engineer moving to tabular risk modelling, can usually do it in one job change, particularly with a domain they already understand.

**Towards research: hardest.** Research roles assess depth, publications and literature familiarity, and a switch generally requires a deliberate route — an industrial research group, a collaboration, or a doctorate.

**From research to applied: easier than feared,** provided you can show something shipped. The doubt employers have is about delivery, not ability, and one production system answers it.

**Towards optimisation and operations research: underestimated.** Fewer people have this background than the market needs, and the mathematics is approachable for anyone comfortable with modelling.

## The Dutch Market Makes This Easier Than It Looks

Outside a handful of research-heavy employers, most Dutch organisations hire applied generalists rather than specialists. A vacancy asking for a machine learning engineer at an insurer, a manufacturer or a public body is asking for someone who can take a problem to production, not someone who has spent five years exclusively in one subfield.

That has two consequences. First, the number of roles available to you is larger than a specialism-focused search suggests. Second, the way you describe your experience matters more than its exact contents: employers respond to production, evaluation and delivery, and those are the parts of your record that travel.

The exception is the small set of employers where the specialism is the product — language technology companies, vision-heavy industrial firms, research institutes. There, deep specific experience is the requirement, and a switch into those roles takes longer.

## What It Costs

Usually about one level, temporarily. You may enter a new specialism at a slightly lower position than your years suggest, or at the same title with the understanding that you are learning. Within eighteen months that generally corrects, because your accumulated discipline makes you faster than someone with equivalent domain experience and less production background.

There is also an opportunity cost worth naming: the deep specialism you were building stops compounding. If you are two years from being genuinely rare in a field with real demand, finishing that is often worth more than switching.

## How Employers Assess a Switcher

They ask three things, directly or indirectly.

**Can you demonstrate the fundamentals?** Evaluation, validation, production. These are tested regardless of domain.

**Have you done anything in the new area at all?** One project, however modest, moves you from "interested" to "started".

**Why are you switching?** A coherent reason — this is where the problems I want to work on are, this is what my domain needs, I want to work closer to production — reads as direction. "I was bored" reads as drift.

## How to Make the Switch Credible Before You Apply

Do one real thing in the new specialism inside your current job. Almost every organisation has an adjacent problem nobody is working on: a forecasting question in a language-focused team, a data pipeline nobody owns, an optimisation problem being solved by spreadsheet. Ask for it.

Learn the evaluation standards of the new field first, not the models. Knowing what counts as a good result is what separates someone who has read about a domain from someone who can work in it.

Read the failure modes. Every specialism has a list of characteristic mistakes, and knowing them is disproportionately convincing in interviews.

Describe your existing work in transferable terms: what you validated, what you deployed, what broke and what you changed. Then connect it explicitly to the new area.

## When to Switch

- The roles near where you live are concentrated in a specialism you do not have.
- Your current specialism is narrowing, or the work has become repetitive.
- You want to move towards production and engineering, where demand is broad.
- The new area genuinely interests you, and you can say why in one sentence.
- You have strong fundamentals to carry across.

## When to Stay

- You are close to genuine depth in a field with real demand.
- The switch is motivated by a single frustrating project rather than by direction.
- You would be moving into a specialism with very few employers in the Netherlands.
- You have not yet built solid production and evaluation practice; acquire that first, in the domain you know.

## How to Talk About the Switch in an Interview

The switch is usually decided by how you describe it rather than by the gap itself, and most candidates make the same avoidable mistakes.

**Lead with the fundamentals, not the gap.** Open with what you have done that is directly relevant — production systems, validation design, monitoring, working with messy data — and let the domain difference arrive as a detail rather than as a confession.

**Give one concrete reason for the move.** "The problems I want to work on are here", "this is where the work near my home is", "I want to be closer to production". One sentence, said without apology. Interviewers are testing whether the switch is directional or reactive.

**Show that you know what you do not know.** Naming the characteristic pitfalls of the new domain — leakage across time in forecasting, annotation quality in language work, distribution shift in vision — is more convincing than claiming familiarity you lack. It signals that you have taken the field seriously.

**Bring one piece of evidence.** A project, even a modest one, moves the conversation from intention to track record.

**Answer the specialist question directly.** When asked why they should hire you over someone with five years in the domain, the honest answer usually works: they will be faster in the first months, you will be better at taking things to production and evaluating them honestly, and the domain gap closes within a year while engineering discipline takes much longer to build.

**Do not oversell.** Claiming the domains are basically the same irritates people who know better, and it invites exactly the technical questions you cannot answer.

## Real example

### An NLP engineer in Groningen who moved to forecasting without moving house

An engineer spends four years on Dutch-language text processing at a service organisation. The work is interesting and the local market for it is small: nearly every relevant vacancy within an hour of home is in forecasting, planning or industrial data.

Rather than relocate, he changes specialism. Inside his current job he takes on a demand forecasting question the operations team had been solving manually, builds it properly with a backtesting setup and a clear baseline, and spends four months on it alongside his normal work.

He then applies for forecasting roles with a straightforward story: four years of production machine learning, evaluation practice he can describe in detail, and one forecasting system in use.

He is hired at the same level by a logistics company twenty minutes away. The first six months are humbling — seasonality and hierarchy problems are unfamiliar and he makes beginner mistakes — but his validation discipline is better than that of colleagues who have only ever done forecasting.

Two years on, he leads the forecasting work. His language processing background turns up unexpectedly when the company starts extracting data from supplier documents, and he is the only person who knows how.

## Key Takeaways

- Evaluation discipline, data intuition, production practice, stakeholder skill and statistical foundations transfer across every AI specialism.
- Modelling intuition, tooling, literature and domain-specific pitfalls do not; expect six months to be functional and eighteen to be good.
- Moving towards engineering is easiest, moving between applied modelling domains is moderate, and moving into research is hardest without a deliberate route.
- Most Dutch employers hire applied generalists, so the available market is wider than a specialism-focused search suggests.
- Make the switch credible by doing one real project in the new area inside your current job, and by learning the new field's evaluation standards first.
