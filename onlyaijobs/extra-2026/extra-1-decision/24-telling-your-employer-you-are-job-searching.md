You have decided to look. Somewhere in the first week comes a smaller question that people handle badly in both directions: does your current employer find out from you, or not at all? Some candidates tell their manager on principle, and occasionally get a better role without changing employer. Others say nothing, and occasionally get caught in a way that damages a relationship they still needed. In the Dutch AI market — small, interconnected, where your manager probably knows the hiring manager at the company you are interviewing with — this decision deserves more thought than it usually gets.

## The Default Is Discretion, For Good Reasons

You are not obliged to tell your employer that you are exploring other roles. Looking for work is not a breach of any duty, and your search is your own business.

The risks of telling run in one direction. Once a manager knows you are considering leaving, you may be left out of long-term plans, passed over for a project that spans the next year, or quietly reclassified as temporary. This is rarely malicious; it is a rational response to uncertainty. But it is difficult to reverse if you end up staying.

There is also a practical asymmetry. If you tell and then stay, you carry the consequences for months. If you stay silent and then leave, the conversation happens once, at the end, in a form everyone expects.

## When Telling Is Genuinely the Better Move

**When what you want exists internally.** If the real problem is your team, your project or your scope rather than your employer, an internal move may solve it faster than an external search. That conversation cannot happen in secret.

**When your manager has explicitly invited it.** Some organisations have a culture where development conversations include "what would make you leave". Where that is genuine, using it is reasonable.

**When you need their cooperation.** Interviews during working hours, a reference before an offer, or a flexible notice period are easier to arrange with an employer who knows.

**When leaving is certain and the timing is known.** If you are moving city, finishing a contract or have already decided to go, transparency lets you plan a proper handover, which protects your reputation.

**When staying silent is becoming dishonest.** If your manager is making concrete commitments on your behalf for next year, saying nothing starts to cost you more than it saves.

## When to Say Nothing Yet

**Early in the search.** Before you know whether anything better exists, there is nothing to tell.

**When your organisation is unstable.** In a reorganisation or cost-cutting period, being known to be looking can make you an easy candidate for the list.

**When your relationship with your manager is the problem.** Telling the person who is the reason you are leaving rarely improves your position.

**When you are exploring rather than deciding.** "I had a coffee with a recruiter" is not news that needs sharing.

**When your employer has a history of poor reactions.** The clearest evidence is how they treated the last person who resigned.

## The Practical Question: Raise the Problem, Not the Search

There is a middle path that candidates often miss. You can raise what would need to change without announcing that you are interviewing.

"I want to work more on production systems and less on reporting. Can we look at how that could change in the next six months?" is a conversation about your development. It gives your employer the chance to fix the problem, it is honest, and it does not disclose anything about your search.

Do it early, before you have offers. If they act, you may not need to leave. If they do not, you have a clean answer to the inevitable exit question — and to your own doubts about whether you gave them a chance.

## Searching Discreetly Without Being Deceptive

**Keep it off company systems.** Use personal email, a personal phone and your own devices. Employers may monitor systems within legal limits, and works council involvement and GDPR obligations apply, but you should not rely on the boundaries of what is permitted to protect you.

**Be careful with your professional profile.** Sudden activity and a rewritten profile are noticed by colleagues before recruiters. If you update it, do so gradually and switch off notifications about changes.

**Schedule interviews outside working hours where possible,** or use leave. Taking half a day is normal; a pattern of unexplained absences is not.

**Dress consistently.** In a hybrid, casual sector, arriving in a suit is the oldest tell there is.

**Ask recruiters to confirm before submitting you.** A CV presented to a client who knows your manager is how many searches become public unintentionally.

**Do not discuss it with colleagues.** Not because they are untrustworthy, but because information travels and you cannot control where it stops.

**Keep doing your job well.** The most damaging version of a discreet search is visible disengagement, which tells your employer what your words did not.

## References Without Announcing Yourself

The awkward point comes when a prospective employer asks for references. Two normal practices help.

First, offer references from previous employers, former managers who have since left, or clients rather than your current line manager. This is standard and rarely questioned.

Second, if a current reference is required, ask for it to be sought only after a conditional offer. Most Dutch employers accept this, and the request itself signals nothing unusual — many candidates are employed and searching.

## What to Check in Your Own Contract

Before you search seriously, read two clauses. A non-compete clause may restrict where you can go, and in a fixed-term contract it is only valid with written justification based on substantial business interests. A client non-solicitation clause matters particularly if you work at a consultancy or through secondment and are considering joining a client.

Also check your notice period, which for employees is one month by default unless a longer period is agreed. Knowing it in advance lets you answer the "when could you start?" question without hesitation.

## When It Comes Out Anyway

Sometimes your search becomes known before you are ready: a recruiter mentions your name, a former colleague talks, a manager sees you at an event.

Handle it calmly and without elaborate denial. A version of the truth that is both honest and measured works best: you are not actively unhappy but you keep an eye on the market, and if there is something you would want to discuss about your own role, you are open to that conversation.

Then use the opening. If the employer values you, this is the moment they will say so — and if they do not respond at all, that is information you did not have yesterday.

## Interviewing During Working Hours Without Lying

The practical problem in most searches is not principle but scheduling. Interviews happen during office hours, and you are expected to be somewhere else.

The cleanest solution is leave. Taking a morning or an afternoon off requires no explanation in most Dutch organisations, and "I have a personal appointment" is both true and sufficient. Nobody is entitled to the details of how you spend your own leave.

Where possible, ask the prospective employer for early morning, late afternoon or online slots. Hiring managers know that good candidates are usually employed, and most accommodate this without comment — a refusal to be flexible at that stage is itself a signal about the organisation.

Avoid two specific mistakes. Do not invent detailed cover stories: elaborate explanations are remembered and unravel badly, while a vague, honest one does not. And do not take interview calls from a meeting room at your current employer, however convenient it seems; it is the single most common way searches become public.

If a process reaches a stage requiring several hours — an assessment day, a full round of conversations — a day of leave is the honest and simple answer. Spread across a search of a few months, the total leave involved is usually smaller than people fear.

## What Not to Put in Writing

A search becomes visible far more often through written traces than through conversations, and the traces are usually avoidable.

**Company email and chat.** Anything sent through your employer's systems can, within legal limits and with the safeguards that apply to employee monitoring, be seen by the organisation. Never send an application, a CV or a scheduling message from a work account — and be aware that a personal webmail tab open on a shared screen during a presentation has ended more than one discreet search.

**Shared calendars.** "Appointment, 10:00–12:00, external" on a calendar colleagues can read invites curiosity. Mark such blocks private, or take leave.

**Company devices.** Job board accounts, saved CVs and downloaded offer letters on a work laptop are both visible and awkward to retrieve later.

**Messages to colleagues.** A trusted colleague is still a colleague, and a written message can be forwarded long after the conversation would have been forgotten.

**Documents in shared drives.** A CV saved in a synced folder is the most common accidental disclosure of all.

The general rule is simple: keep the entire search on your own accounts and devices. It costs nothing, protects your negotiating position and avoids the situation where your employer learns something from a file rather than from you.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Amersfoort who raised the problem first

An ML engineer has been frustrated for months: too much maintenance, no new model work, a platform project repeatedly postponed. He starts looking quietly and, after two weeks, decides to try one conversation first.

He does not mention the search. He tells his manager which work he wants more of, which he wants less of, and asks whether the balance can change within six months. His manager listens, promises to look at it and changes nothing in the following eight weeks.

He continues searching, keeps everything off company systems, takes leave for interviews and provides references from a previous employer. When he resigns, his manager is surprised but cannot claim to have been unaware of the underlying issue — and the handover goes well because the relationship was never damaged by a bad conversation.

A year later they meet at a conference and the manager refers a former colleague to him. In a market this size, that is the outcome worth protecting.

## Key Takeaways

- You are not obliged to disclose a job search, and discretion is the sensible default early on.
- Telling makes sense when the role you want exists internally, when you need cooperation, or when leaving is already certain.
- The middle path — raising what would need to change, without mentioning the search — gives your employer a fair chance without exposing you.
- Search off company systems, be careful with profile updates, schedule interviews outside working hours and ask recruiters for permission before every submission.
- Offer references from previous employers, and check your non-compete, client non-solicitation and notice period clauses before searching seriously.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employee starting a search) Do I have to tell my employer I am looking for another job?
No. There is no obligation to disclose a job search, and discretion is usually the sensible default until you know whether a better option exists.

### (Scenario: employee who wants change) Should I tell my manager what is wrong instead?
Often yes. Raising what would need to change, without mentioning a search, gives your employer a chance to fix it and costs you nothing.

### (Scenario: candidate asked for references) How do I handle references while still employed?
Offer previous employers, former managers or clients, and ask that any current reference be sought only after a conditional offer.

### (Scenario: employee worried about being found out) How do I search discreetly?
Use personal devices and email, schedule interviews outside working hours, avoid sudden profile changes and require recruiters to ask permission before each submission.

### (Scenario: employee whose search became known) What if my employer finds out before I am ready?
Respond calmly and honestly without over-explaining, and use the moment to discuss what would make your current role better.
