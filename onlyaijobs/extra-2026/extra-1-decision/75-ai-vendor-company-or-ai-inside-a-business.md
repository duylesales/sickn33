Two offers, both described as machine learning engineering. One is at a company whose product is an AI system sold to other organisations: document processing, planning software, inspection tooling, a language platform. The other is inside a business that uses AI to run itself better: a manufacturer, an insurer, a health organisation, a retailer. The technologies overlap almost entirely. Nearly everything else differs — what counts as success, how close you get to the data, who decides what you work on, and what your experience is worth afterwards. Candidates rarely make this comparison explicitly, and it is one of the more consequential ones available.

## What Changes Day to Day

At a **vendor**, your model must work for many customers with different data, different conventions and different definitions of the same concept. Generalisation is the central problem. Releases are versioned, breaking changes matter, and a regression affects everyone at once. You work with product managers, customer success and sales, and the roadmap is shaped by what will sell.

**Inside a business**, your model must work for one organisation, which you can study in depth. Fit matters more than generality. Adoption is the central problem: a model nobody uses has no value regardless of its metrics. You work with the people whose process you are changing, and the roadmap is shaped by what the business needs this quarter.

## The Data Access Difference

This is the practical distinction that shapes everything else, and candidates consistently underestimate it.

**At a vendor you usually do not own the data.** Customer data arrives under contractual and privacy constraints, sometimes in anonymised or restricted form, sometimes not at all. You may be debugging a failure you cannot see, on data you cannot access, through a customer's support ticket. Vendors handle this with synthetic data, careful logging, on-site analysis and data processing arrangements that define exactly what is permitted.

**Inside a business, the data is yours,** along with its history, its documentation and the people who created it. You can walk to the department that generates it and ask why a field changed meaning in 2021.

If the part of the work you enjoy is understanding data deeply, in-house has a structural advantage. If you prefer building systems that work under unknown conditions, the vendor problem is more interesting.

## What a Vendor Role Builds

**Engineering rigour.** Software sold to others is tested, versioned, monitored and supported. Practices are usually stronger than in-house teams, out of necessity.

**Generalisation skills.** Making something work across ten customers is a harder and rarer capability than making it work for one.

**Scale exposure.** Your system may process more volume than any single organisation would generate.

**Commercial literacy.** You learn what people pay for, which reframes how you judge technical work.

**Portability.** Vendor experience reads well almost everywhere, because it implies production discipline.

## What an In-House Role Builds

**Domain depth.** Two years of insurance, logistics or manufacturing makes you valuable to every employer in that sector.

**End-to-end ownership.** Problem definition, data, model, deployment, adoption and the consequences.

**Stakeholder skill.** Convincing people to change how they work is the actual difficulty, and it is learned only by doing it.

**Data governance experience.** Provenance, quality, access and compliance, on data your organisation is accountable for.

**Consequence.** You see what happened after the model went live, for years, which is the most instructive feedback loop in the field.

## Commercial Pressure and What It Does

At a vendor, the roadmap follows revenue. That is not cynical, and it has consequences: a large customer's request may outrank a technically important improvement, features are sometimes promised before they exist, and work can be reprioritised mid-quarter by a deal. Some people find this energising; others find it corrosive.

Inside a business, the pressure is different: cost, internal politics, competing departments and the risk that your project loses its sponsor. Nothing gets cancelled because a sale fell through, and plenty gets cancelled because a director changed.

Ask, in either case, for an example of something that was reprioritised in the past year and why. The answer describes the environment more accurately than any statement about culture.

## The Regulatory Angle

Where AI is the product, your employer is likely the **provider** of the system under the AI Act, which carries the heavier set of obligations; where AI is used internally, the organisation is typically the **deployer**. That difference shapes the work: vendors invest more in documentation, conformity processes and technical standards, while deployers focus on oversight, correct use and monitoring in their own context.

Privacy obligations also land differently. A vendor processing customer data acts under contractual arrangements defining what it may do; an in-house team works with data its own organisation controls, with impact assessments and internal governance.

If you want to build regulatory expertise, both routes offer it in different shapes — provider-side documentation and standards, or deployer-side oversight and monitoring.

## The Dutch Market Reality

The Netherlands has a modest number of substantial AI product companies, concentrated around the Randstad, Eindhoven and a few regional clusters, plus a long tail of smaller software companies with AI features. In-house roles are far more numerous and spread across the entire country, which matters if you are not willing to move or commute long distances.

Practically, this means the vendor path often involves a location constraint, while the in-house path usually offers something within reach of wherever you live.

## Questions to Ask a Vendor

- **What access do engineers have to customer data, and under what arrangements?**
- **How do you evaluate the product across customers?**
- **How often does the roadmap change, and what drives it?**
- **Who handles customer-specific tuning — engineering, implementation consultants or the customer?**
- **What proportion of my week would be support and implementation work?**

## Questions to Ask an In-House Employer

- **What is in production, and who maintains it?**
- **Who owns adoption when a model is delivered?**
- **How long does it take to get access to a new data source?**
- **What happened to the last project that was cancelled?**
- **Would I be building, or configuring purchased systems?**

## When Each Suits You

**Choose the vendor** when you want engineering rigour, generalisation problems, scale and commercial exposure, and when you accept that the data will often be out of reach.

**Choose in-house** when you want domain depth, ownership of the full chain, direct data access and the experience of living with what you built — and when you want more employers within a sensible distance of home.

## The Implementation Trap

The most common disappointment in vendor roles is not the data access problem. It is discovering that the job advertised as machine learning engineering is largely customer implementation: configuring the product for each new client, tuning parameters, investigating support tickets, joining sales conversations to provide technical credibility, and building the small custom extensions a large customer demanded as a condition of signing.

This work is legitimate and someone must do it. It is also a different career from product engineering, and the two are frequently described in the same vacancy.

**How to spot it.** Ask what proportion of the week is customer-facing, how many customers you would be responsible for, and whether there is a separate implementation or customer success team. A vendor without one is distributing that work across its engineers, and you would be among them.

**Ask who does custom work.** If engineering builds bespoke extensions for individual customers, the product fragments and so does your week.

**Ask about the support rota.** Being in the escalation path for a product used by hundreds of organisations is a significant commitment, particularly if customers span time zones.

**Ask what shipped to the core product last quarter,** and who built it. The answer tells you whether product engineering is a real function or an aspiration between customer demands.

There is a version of implementation work that is genuinely good — deep exposure to many organisations' data and processes, which builds pattern recognition quickly — but you should choose it knowingly rather than discover it in month two. If what you want is to build the product itself, confirm that the role sits in the team that does.

## What Happens When a Large Customer Leaves

One more question worth asking a smaller vendor: how concentrated is the revenue? A company where two or three customers represent most of the income behaves differently from one with a broad base. Roadmaps bend towards the large accounts, and a single departure can trigger a reorganisation. It is a reasonable thing to ask, and founders who are comfortable with the question usually have a reassuring answer.

## Real example

### An engineer in Eindhoven who did a year of each

An engineer joins a company selling inspection software to manufacturers. The engineering culture is the best he has worked in: everything tested, versioned and monitored, with release discipline he had never encountered. The frustration is that when a customer's results degrade, he cannot see their images without a support process, and half his debugging is done blind.

After three years he moves in-house, to a manufacturer using similar technology on its own lines. The engineering practice is markedly weaker and the data access is total: he can walk onto the floor, see the camera, watch the process and talk to the operators who label the defects.

He fixes in two months a class of problem he had chased for a year at the vendor, because he could finally see what was happening.

He also imports the vendor's practices — testing, versioning, monitoring — into a team that had none, which is what gets him promoted.

His conclusion is that the vendor taught him how to build software and the manufacturer taught him what the software was for, and that anyone who can do both is unusually employable in either direction.

## Key Takeaways

- At a vendor, generalisation across customers is the central problem and data access is limited by contract and privacy; in-house, fit and adoption are central and the data is yours.
- Vendor roles build engineering rigour, generalisation, scale and commercial literacy; in-house roles build domain depth, end-to-end ownership, stakeholder skill and consequence.
- Commercial pressure shapes vendor roadmaps; sponsorship and internal politics shape in-house ones — ask for a concrete example of something reprioritised last year.
- Vendors are typically providers under the AI Act and in-house teams deployers, which changes where the regulatory work sits.
- In the Netherlands, vendor roles cluster geographically while in-house roles exist almost everywhere, which often decides the matter for people who will not relocate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two offers) Is it better to work for an AI product company or an end user?
Neither is better in general. Vendors build engineering rigour and generalisation skills; in-house roles build domain depth, adoption experience and direct data access.

### (Scenario: candidate who enjoys data work) Which gives better access to data?
In-house, decisively. At a vendor, customer data arrives under contractual and privacy constraints, and debugging is often done without seeing the data that caused the problem.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking about employability) Which experience travels better?
Both travel. Vendor experience signals production discipline; in-house experience signals domain depth and adoption skill. The strongest profiles have some of each.

### (Scenario: candidate considering regulation) Does the AI Act apply differently?
Typically yes: vendors are usually providers, with heavier documentation and conformity obligations, while in-house organisations are usually deployers, focused on oversight, correct use and monitoring.

### (Scenario: candidate who will not relocate) Which is more realistic where I live?
In-house roles are spread across the whole country, while AI product companies cluster in a few regions — often the deciding factor for people unwilling to commute far.
