The offer arrives with flattering framing. You understand the technology and you can talk to the business, which apparently makes you rare, and the organisation would like you to own the data products rather than build them: the roadmap, the priorities, the stakeholders, the decisions about what gets made. It sounds like a step up. Sometimes it is. It is also a move into a different profession, with different skills, a different market and a return journey that gets harder each year. Candidates who take it deliberately rarely regret it; candidates who take it because they were flattered frequently do.

## What the Role Actually Is

Titles vary — data product owner, product manager for data, analytics lead, analytics translator — and the substance is consistent. You decide what gets built and why, and other people build it.

That means a week of deciding between requests that all have advocates, writing down what "done" means for things that are inherently uncertain, negotiating with stakeholders whose priorities conflict, explaining delays, defining success measures, and protecting your team's attention from the organisation's enthusiasm.

It also means far less code. Some product owners keep a small technical corner; most find it disappears within a year, because the calendar fills with other people's needs.

## Why Employers Offer This to Technical People

Because the failure mode they are trying to solve is a technical one. Organisations accumulate models nobody uses, dashboards nobody reads and projects that were solved correctly after being framed incorrectly. A product owner who cannot assess technical feasibility approves impossible things; one who cannot talk to the business builds elegant irrelevance.

You are being offered the role because you can do both. That is a genuine compliment and not, by itself, a reason to accept.

## What You Gain

**Leverage.** Deciding what five people work on for a quarter affects more than what you personally build in that quarter.

**Visibility.** Product roles sit closer to decision makers and are more visible in promotion conversations.

**A broader market later.** Product skills apply across sectors and outlive any particular technology stack.

**Influence over whether the work matters.** Much of what makes data work satisfying — being used, changing a decision — is determined at the framing stage, which is now your job.

**Relief from a particular frustration.** If you have spent years building things that were never adopted, owning the adoption problem is unusually satisfying.

## What You Lose

**Depth, gradually.** After six months away from code you are fine. After two years, the tooling has moved and your instincts are stale. After four, returning to a senior engineering role means a serious rebuilding effort and interviewers who are sceptical.

**The satisfaction of making something.** Product work produces decisions and documents. Some people find this energising; others describe it as a year of meetings with nothing to show.

**A clear standard for your own work.** Engineering has tests. Product work is judged by outcomes that arrive slowly and have many parents.

**Technical credibility with the team,** unless you actively maintain it. Teams forgive a product owner who does not code; they do not forgive one who no longer understands why an estimate is what it is.

## Is It Actually a Promotion?

Frequently not, and it is worth establishing this before deciding.

Compare the salary scale, not the title. In many Dutch organisations a senior engineer and a product owner sit at similar levels, and the move is lateral. Ask where the role sits in the scale, what the progression path looks like from there, and who the role reports to.

Ask also whether the previous product owner was promoted, moved sideways or left. And ask whether the senior technical track exists at all in this organisation — in places where it does not, product or management is the only route upwards, which is a real constraint but also an argument for looking at employers where that is not true.

## The Reversibility Question

This is the part candidates underweight. Moving out of hands-on work is easy; moving back is not, and the difficulty compounds.

Within a year, returning is straightforward. Within two, you will need to demonstrate current skills and accept that some roles will treat you as out of practice. Beyond three or four, you are effectively changing career again, often at a lower level than you left.

What keeps the door open: staying technically involved in reviews and design discussions, doing one hands-on thing regularly even if small, keeping up with the stack your teams use, and being able to describe technical decisions you influenced in real detail.

What closes it: a calendar with no engineering contact, two years of roadmaps and nothing built, and a vocabulary that has drifted entirely into process language.

## Signals It Is a Real Product Role

- You would have authority to say no, and the organisation would support it.
- There is a defined product with users, not a service desk with a backlog.
- You would own success measures, not only delivery dates.
- The team is stable and staffed; a product owner without a team is a coordinator.
- Someone senior sponsors the role and expects you to push back on them occasionally.
- The role exists because the organisation wants better decisions, not because it wants faster ones.

## Signals It Is a Meeting Role

- The job is described mostly as coordination between existing teams.
- Priorities are set elsewhere and you would communicate them.
- The backlog is a list of requests from departments, with no product definition.
- You would have responsibility for delivery and no authority over scope.
- The previous person left quickly, or the role has been vacant for a long time.
- Nobody can name the users.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **What decisions would be mine, and which are not?**
- **Who can overrule me, and how often does that happen?**
- **Where does this sit in the salary scale compared with senior engineering roles?**
- **What is the team I would work with, and is it fully staffed?**
- **How is success measured, and over what period?**
- **What happened to the previous product owner?**
- **Would I keep any technical responsibility, and is that realistic given the calendar?**
- **Does a senior technical track exist here?**

## When to Accept

- You genuinely enjoy the framing, negotiating and adoption problems rather than tolerating them.
- You have enough production experience that your technical judgement is well formed.
- The role has real authority and a staffed team.
- You are clear that this is a change of profession and you want it.
- The organisation has no senior technical track and you have decided to stay there anyway.

## When to Decline

- You would take it mainly for the title or because it was offered.
- You still want to be a better engineer in three years.
- The role has responsibility without authority.
- You are the only person who can do the technical work, so you would end up doing both jobs.
- You are early in your career, with too little production experience to make good feasibility calls.

## How to Do It Without Losing Yourself Technically

If you accept, protect a small technical practice deliberately. Attend design reviews and read the code that matters, not to approve it but to stay fluent. Keep one hands-on responsibility, however modest, and defend it against your own calendar. Write the evaluation criteria for models yourself rather than delegating them.

Then set a review point at a year. Ask whether you are doing product work well and enjoying it, or whether you are doing it competently and missing the making. Both answers are useful, and the second is much easier to act on at twelve months than at thirty-six.

## What the Market for These Roles Looks Like

If you are weighing the move partly on future employability, it helps to know how these roles sit in the Dutch market.

**Demand exists, concentrated in larger organisations.** Banks, insurers, retailers, energy companies, health organisations and scale-ups past their first growth phase all hire for data product roles. Smaller employers rarely do; there, the same work is done by a lead or by the manager.

**The titles are inconsistent.** Data product owner, product manager data, analytics lead, information manager, sometimes business partner. Read the responsibilities, since two roles with the same title can differ completely in authority.

**Your competition is not only technical people.** Many candidates for these roles come from the business side — planners, analysts, process owners — and they usually have more experience of stakeholder work than you do. Your advantage is feasibility judgement, and it is worth making explicit: you can tell when an estimate is wrong, when a request is technically incoherent and when a promised capability does not exist.

**Returning is easier from a technical product role than from a general one.** If your product work stays close to models and data platforms, the return path to engineering remains open longer than if you drift into general delivery management.

**Pay tends to track the organisation's product or management family rather than its technical one.** That can help or hurt depending on the employer, which is exactly why comparing the scale rather than the title matters.

## Real example

### An engineer in Rotterdam who took it for a year and went back

A senior ML engineer at a logistics company is offered the product owner role for the planning platform. Same salary scale, a team of six, a seat in conversations she had previously only heard about second-hand.

She accepts, with two conditions: a review after a year, and one technical responsibility retained — she keeps ownership of the evaluation framework for the forecasting models.

The first six months are the most satisfying of her career. Three projects that had been stuck for a year get unstuck, mostly by being reframed or cancelled. She discovers she is good at saying no.

The second six months are different. The organisation reorganises, her sponsor leaves, and the role becomes coordination between departments rather than product ownership. The authority she was given informally evaporates with the person who gave it.

At the review, she moves back to a lead engineering role. The evaluation framework she kept is what makes the return straightforward — it is current, technical and hers.

Her view now: the year taught her more about why data projects fail than five years of building did, and keeping one technical thing was the decision that made it a detour rather than a change of career.

## Key Takeaways

- A data product role is a different profession, not a senior version of your current one: you decide what gets built and others build it.
- The real gains are leverage, visibility, transferable product skills and ownership of whether the work gets used.
- Technical depth decays gradually — comfortable at six months, noticeable at two years, a career change at four.
- Check the salary scale rather than the title; in many Dutch organisations the move is lateral, and the absence of a senior technical track is itself information.
- If you accept, keep one genuine technical responsibility and set a review point at a year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: engineer offered a product role) Is a data product owner role a promotion?
Often it is lateral. Compare the salary scale rather than the title, and ask what the progression path from the role looks like.

### (Scenario: engineer worried about skills) How quickly do technical skills decay?
Comfortably reversible within a year, noticeable at two, and effectively a career change at three or four. Keeping one hands-on responsibility slows this considerably.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the role) How do I tell a real product role from a coordination job?
Real product roles carry authority to say no, a defined product with users, ownership of success measures and a staffed team. Coordination roles have responsibility without authority.

### (Scenario: engineer who enjoys building) Should I take it if I still like coding?
Probably not yet. Product work produces decisions rather than artefacts, and people who miss making things tend to find that out expensively.

### (Scenario: engineer with no senior technical path) What if product is the only way up here?
That is a legitimate reason to accept, and also a reason to check whether other employers near you maintain a senior technical track.
