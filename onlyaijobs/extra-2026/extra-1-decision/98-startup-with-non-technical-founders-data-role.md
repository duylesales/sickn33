Two founders from the industry they are disrupting — logistics, healthcare, construction, insurance — with customers already interested, a clear view of the problem, funding in the bank, and no technical person in the company. They want you to build the thing. It is flattering, it is genuinely an opportunity, and it is also the configuration in which data professionals most often spend eighteen months building something that was never going to work. Domain founders bring advantages that technical founders rarely have. What they cannot do is tell you whether what they promised their customers is possible, and that judgement is about to become entirely yours.

## What Domain Founders Get Right

It is worth starting here, because the stereotype is unfair.

**They know the problem.** Not a guess at a problem — the actual workflow, the actual frustration, the actual money being lost, from years of living it.

**They can sell.** Their network is the customer base, and credibility in an industry opens doors that no technical demonstration would.

**They understand the buying process,** including who decides, what procurement requires and what the budget cycle looks like.

**They are often better at pricing and positioning** than technically founded companies, which frequently build impressive things nobody buys.

A company with domain founders and a strong technical hire is a well-known successful combination. The risk is not the founders' background; it is what happens in the gap they cannot see.

## The Central Risk: Nobody Can Say "That Is Not Possible"

Everything difficult about this situation follows from one fact: until you arrive, no one in the company can assess technical claims.

That leads to a recognisable pattern. Promises are made to customers based on what feels reasonable. Timelines are set by commercial need rather than by engineering reality. A demonstration built in a fortnight is assumed to be nearly a product. And an estimate you give is heard as a commitment, because in their previous world estimates were commitments.

None of this is bad faith. It is the absence of a corrective, and you would be the corrective — which is a role you should accept knowingly rather than discover.

## The Feasibility Check You Must Do Before Accepting

Do not skip this, and do not do it politely. Serious founders respect it; the ones who do not are telling you something.

**Does the data exist?** Not "will customers have data" — does it exist today, in a usable form, and would customers actually hand it over? Many promising products fail here, because the data is on paper, in a supplier's system, or subject to contracts that forbid sharing.

**Has anyone verified that the core claim is achievable?** If the pitch involves predicting something, ask what the baseline is and whether anyone has tested it on real data.

**What has been promised to customers, in writing?** Ask to see it. This is the single most revealing document in the company.

**Is the difficulty in the modelling or in the integration?** Most business-facing products fail on integration with the customers' systems, not on the model. Founders often underestimate that entirely.

**What would make this fail?** A founder who has a thoughtful answer has thought about the risks; one who says "nothing, if we execute" has not.

## Who Makes Technical Decisions?

Establish this before you accept, because it determines whether you are a technical leader or an implementer of other people's assumptions.

Ask: who decides the architecture, the priorities and what is technically feasible? If the answer is you, ask whether that is written into the role. If the answer is the founders, ask how they would evaluate a recommendation they disagreed with.

Then ask about the plan: is a technical co-founder or senior technical hire intended, and on what timeline? Being the only technical person in a company is a heavy position, and the honest answer matters more than the reassuring one.

Ask also whether there is an advisor with a technical background. Many Dutch startups with domain founders have one, and it is a strong signal — it means the founders already know what they cannot judge.

## What You Would Actually Be Taking On

More than the job title suggests: architecture, build, deployment, security, data protection, infrastructure costs, hiring the next engineers, and explaining technical reality to commercial people repeatedly and patiently.

You would also carry the regulatory questions. If the product processes personal data, or falls into a regulated use, someone has to understand what that requires — and in a company without technical founders, nobody else will raise it until a customer's procurement team does.

## Terms Worth Getting Right

**Title and authority.** If you are the technical lead, say so in the contract. It matters for hiring, for customer conversations and for your next role.

**Equity, treated as a possible bonus rather than compensation.** Ask about the structure, the vesting, what happens if you leave, and what the last valuation was — and then value the offer on salary alone.

**Salary that reflects the risk.** Early-stage companies pay less, and a below-market salary plus uncertain equity plus sole technical responsibility is three risks stacked.

**A budget for infrastructure and tooling,** because working without one is a constant negotiation.

**Clarity on funding.** How much runway, from whom, and what the next milestone is.

## Warning Signs

- Customers have been promised something nobody has tested.
- The founders describe the technical work as "just the implementation".
- There is no plan to hire more technical people.
- Your estimates are received as opening positions in a negotiation.
- Equity is offered in place of a reasonable salary.
- No technical advisor, and no interest in one.
- The data the product depends on does not exist yet.

## When to Accept

- The founders know their industry deeply and have customers, not just interest.
- The feasibility check holds: data exists, the core claim is testable, integration is understood.
- You would have genuine technical authority, in writing.
- A second technical hire is planned and funded.
- The salary is reasonable and the equity is upside rather than substitute.
- The founders demonstrably accept being told that something will not work.

## When to Decline

- The product depends on data nobody has.
- Commitments have been made that you would inherit and cannot meet.
- Technical authority stays with the founders.
- You would be alone with no plan to change that.
- Runway is under a year and the next milestone requires the impossible.
- You are early in your career; this role needs someone who has seen production systems fail.

## The First Six Months as the Only Technical Person

If you accept, the early decisions matter more than in any other setting, because there is nobody to correct them.

**Build the smallest thing a customer will actually use.** Not the platform, not the architecture for a company of fifty. Early-stage companies die from not shipping, and your credibility with the founders comes from something working in front of a customer.

**Establish how estimates are treated, immediately.** Give ranges, explain what drives them, and say plainly when something is uncertain. The first time an estimate is repeated to a customer as a commitment, correct it — gently and immediately, because the pattern sets in the first month.

**Say no with alternatives.** "That is not possible" ends conversations with domain founders; "that version needs data we do not have, and here is what we can do with what we have" keeps you in them. You will be saying this often.

**Write down the technical decisions and the reasons.** In a company without other engineers, this document is the only institutional memory, and it is what lets the second engineer start usefully rather than re-deriving everything.

**Own the boring obligations.** Security, access, backups, personal data. Nobody else will raise them, and the first customer with a procurement department will ask.

**Push to hire the second engineer earlier than feels affordable.** Being the single point of failure is bad for the company and worse for you — a holiday, an illness or a resignation should not be an existential event.

**Set your own review point.** Six months in, ask whether the product is closer to something customers pay for, whether the founders adjusted to technical reality, and whether you are still learning. Those three answers tell you whether to commit further.

**Keep your own record of what you built.** Early-stage companies rarely document anything, and if the company does not survive, your account of the systems you designed, the decisions you made and what they achieved is the only evidence that remains. Write it as you go rather than reconstructing it afterwards from memory.

## Real example

### An engineer in Rotterdam who asked to see the customer contract

An engineer is approached by two founders from the construction industry building a tool to predict project overruns. Their domain knowledge is impressive, three contractors have signed letters of intent, and there is funding for about eighteen months.

He does the feasibility check. The data would come from the contractors' project systems — which turn out to be three different platforms, one of which exports only PDFs. Nobody had looked at this.

He asks to see what was promised. The letters of intent describe a level of accuracy nobody has tested, on data nobody has seen.

Rather than declining, he proposes something: a four-week paid engagement to assess feasibility with real data from one contractor, before he decides about joining.

The month is revealing. The data is worse than expected and the integration is most of the work — and, unexpectedly, a simpler product emerges: not prediction, but consistent reporting across the three systems, which is what the contractors actually complain about.

He joins as technical lead, with the scope rewritten and the accuracy claims removed from the customer conversations. The company is still running, selling the simpler product.

His conclusion: the founders were right about the industry and wrong about the solution, and four weeks of checking saved everyone eighteen months.

## Key Takeaways

- Domain founders bring genuine advantages — problem knowledge, customer access, commercial judgement — and cannot assess technical claims, which becomes your responsibility.
- Do the feasibility check before accepting: does the data exist today, has the core claim been tested, and is the difficulty in the modelling or in the integration?
- Ask to see what has been promised to customers in writing; it is the most revealing document in the company.
- Establish who decides technical questions, whether that authority is in your contract, and whether a second technical hire is planned and funded.
- Treat equity as possible upside rather than compensation, and decline when the product depends on data nobody has.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate approached by domain founders) Is a startup without technical founders a bad idea?
Not at all — domain founders with a strong technical hire is a proven combination. The risk is that nobody can assess technical claims until you arrive, so the feasibility check is yours to do.

### (Scenario: candidate checking the idea) What should I verify before accepting?
Whether the data exists today in usable form, whether the core claim has been tested on real data, what has been promised to customers in writing, and whether the difficulty is modelling or integration.

### (Scenario: candidate negotiating the role) What should be in the contract?
Technical authority and title, a reasonable salary, an infrastructure and tooling budget, and clarity on funding and the next milestone. Equity should be upside, not compensation.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about being alone) What if I would be the only technical person?
Ask whether a second technical hire is planned and funded, and whether a technical advisor exists. Sole technical responsibility in an early-stage company is heavy and needs experience.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure) Is there a way to test the situation first?
Yes — propose a short paid feasibility engagement with real data before committing. Serious founders welcome it, and the result usually improves the product whichever way you decide.
