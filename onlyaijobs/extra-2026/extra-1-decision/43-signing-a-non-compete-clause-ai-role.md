The offer is good and the contract arrives with a clause you skim, read again, and then read a third time: for twelve months after leaving, you may not work for any company active in the same field, anywhere in the Netherlands, on pain of a penalty per day of violation. For a data professional whose field is "AI and data", that wording could describe almost any employer you might want next. Non-compete clauses are common in Dutch contracts, frequently broader than they need to be, and far more negotiable before signing than after. Understanding what the law actually requires turns an intimidating paragraph into a conversation.

This article provides general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, consult an employment lawyer, your union or the free information service **Juridisch Loket**.

## What Dutch Law Requires

Under **Article 7:653 of the Dutch Civil Code**, a non-compete clause must be agreed in writing with an adult employee.

**In a fixed-term contract**, the clause is only valid if the employer states in writing that it is necessary because of substantial business or service interests, with reasons specific to the role. A generic justification copied into every contract is vulnerable.

**In a permanent contract**, no such written justification is required at the time of signing, but a court can set the clause aside in whole or in part, or limit its duration or scope, if the employee is unfairly disadvantaged in relation to the employer's interest.

**A court may also award compensation** for the period during which the employee is restricted, where the clause significantly hinders them from working elsewhere.

**The clause can lose force** if the employment relationship changes substantially — a significantly different role — and the clause becomes considerably more onerous as a result.

The practical summary: these clauses are not automatically enforceable as written, and Dutch courts regularly narrow them. That is not a reason to sign carelessly, because litigation is expensive and slow, and the prospect of it alone can deter a future employer.

## The Three Clauses People Confuse

**Non-compete (concurrentiebeding).** Restricts working for competitors or starting a competing business. The broadest and most restrictive.

**Client non-solicitation (relatiebeding).** Restricts working for or approaching the employer's clients. Narrower, and often the more relevant one for consultancies and secondment firms, where it can block you from joining the client where you have worked.

**Confidentiality.** Protects the employer's information rather than restricting where you work. Reasonable confidentiality obligations are normal, and trade secrets are separately protected under Dutch law implementing the EU directive.

Many contracts contain all three, plus a penalty clause attaching a fixed amount per violation or per day. Read them together: a modest non-compete with an aggressive penalty clause is still a serious restriction.

## What Makes a Clause Reasonable or Not

| Element | Reasonable | Unreasonable |
|---|---|---|
| Scope of activity | Specific competitors or a defined market segment | "Any company active in AI or data" |
| Geography | The region where the employer actually operates | The whole country, or Europe |
| Duration | Six to twelve months | Two years or more |
| Justification | Tied to specific knowledge, clients or projects | Boilerplate in every contract |
| Compensation | Some payment during the restricted period | None |
| Penalty | Proportionate, or none | A large fixed sum per day |

In AI and data roles specifically, the "same field" formulation is the problem. A forecasting specialist restricted from "companies using machine learning" is effectively restricted from the labour market, which is exactly the disproportion courts look at.

## Why Employers Ask For It

Not always to trap you. The legitimate concerns are real: you may have access to model architectures, pricing logic, customer data, research direction or commercial strategy. For consultancies and secondment firms, the concern is losing a client relationship rather than knowledge.

The illegitimate version is using a broad clause as a retention tool — making leaving frightening rather than protecting anything specific. You can usually tell the difference by asking what exactly the clause is protecting. A specific answer describes a competitor, a project or a client. A vague answer describes the employer's discomfort with people leaving.

## What to Negotiate

Non-competes are among the most negotiable terms in a Dutch contract, because the employer usually wants protection against one specific scenario rather than the sweeping version their template contains.

**Narrow the activity.** Name two or three actual competitors instead of a market description.

**Narrow the geography** to where the employer genuinely competes.

**Shorten the duration.** Six months is often accepted where twelve was proposed.

**Add a carve-out** for roles that could not harm them — a different sector, a different function, an academic or public-sector position.

**Remove or reduce the penalty clause,** which is frequently the part that actually deters future employers.

**Ask for compensation** during any restricted period.

**Request a release commitment**: agreement that the employer will confirm in writing, on request, whether it intends to enforce the clause for a specific new role. This costs them nothing at signing and is enormously useful later.

Ask before signing, once, in a single message covering all points. Employers expect it from experienced candidates, and under pay transparency rules the whole offer conversation is already more explicit than it used to be.

## When to Refuse Outright

- The clause covers the entire field, the entire country and more than a year, with a penalty and no compensation.
- You are being asked to sign it after you have already started, without anything in return.
- It is in a fixed-term contract with no written justification specific to your role.
- The employer refuses any amendment and cannot explain what is being protected.
- You work in a narrow specialism where the clause would genuinely leave you unemployable — a common situation in fields with few Dutch employers.

Walking away over a clause feels disproportionate until you consider that it governs the move after this one, which you cannot predict today.

## If You Already Signed One

Do not assume you are trapped. Establish what the clause actually says, whether the formal requirements were met, and whether your role has changed materially since signing.

Then ask your current employer directly whether they intend to enforce it for the specific role you have in mind. Many will say no in writing, particularly where the new employer is not a real competitor, and that letter resolves the matter.

If they insist and the restriction is disproportionate, legal advice is worthwhile: Dutch courts can limit or set aside these clauses, and the prospect of that sometimes produces a negotiated release. Tell your prospective employer early rather than late — most are familiar with the situation, and discovering it during a notice period is far worse than knowing in advance.

## What Employers Can Protect Without Restricting You

A useful argument in the negotiation is that most of what an employer legitimately worries about is already protected by other means, which makes a sweeping non-compete unnecessary rather than merely unfair.

**Confidential information** is protected by a confidentiality clause and, for genuine trade secrets, by Dutch law implementing the EU trade secrets directive. You may not take model architectures, datasets, pricing logic or customer information regardless of where you work next.

**Client relationships** are protected by a client non-solicitation clause, which is narrower and more defensible than a general prohibition on working in the field.

**Intellectual property** in work created for the employer belongs to them under the Copyright Act and, for relevant inventions, under the Patents Act.

**Colleagues** can be protected by a staff non-solicitation clause preventing you from recruiting former teammates.

Presented together, these cover nearly every real concern. That allows you to say something reasonable in the negotiation: you accept confidentiality, client and staff clauses in full, and ask that the general non-compete be narrowed to named competitors for a limited period. Employers who genuinely want protection accept this readily. Employers who wanted a retention mechanism do not — which is itself worth learning before you sign.

## When the Clause Is Presented Matters

Watch the timing. A non-compete introduced with the original offer is normal. One that appears in the final contract after everything was agreed verbally, or one presented for signature on your first day, deserves more scrutiny: the negotiating moment has passed and both sides know it. If that happens, it is entirely reasonable to say you need to read it properly before signing and to raise amendments then, even if it delays the start by a few days.

## Why This Matters More in AI Than in Most Fields

A non-compete written in general terms lands differently on a data professional than on most other employees, for reasons worth spelling out in the negotiation.

**The specialism is narrow.** A clause covering "organisations active in artificial intelligence" now describes banks, insurers, retailers, hospitals, grid operators and the public sector. A restriction intended to cover a handful of rivals can, read literally, cover most of the employers who would hire you.

**Geography does not limit it.** Much of this work can be done from anywhere, so a clause with no geographic scope is not softened by distance in the way it once was for a regional sales role.

**The skills are the product.** Employers occasionally argue that your modelling approach or tooling knowledge is itself protectable. It is not, in any general sense: what you learned remains yours, while their data, code and documented methods do not.

**Roles change quickly.** You may sign as an analyst and be doing something substantially different two years later. Where the role has changed significantly, the original clause may no longer hold up in the form it was signed, which is one reason employers sometimes ask for a fresh signature after a promotion.

None of this means the clause is unenforceable. It means the proportionality argument — what is actually being protected, against what cost to you — is unusually strong in this field, and that is the argument worth making calmly before you sign rather than angrily afterwards.

## Real example

### A data engineer in Eindhoven who narrowed the clause

A data engineer accepts an offer from a high-tech supplier. The contract contains a twelve-month non-compete covering "companies active in machine learning or industrial automation" throughout the Netherlands, with a penalty per day of violation.

Rather than signing or walking away, he sends one message: he is happy to accept a clause protecting their genuine interests, and proposes naming three specific competitors, limiting it to six months, and removing the daily penalty in favour of ordinary damages.

The employer accepts two of the three: the named competitors and the six months, keeping a reduced penalty. The negotiation takes four days.

Three years later he moves to a logistics company that uses machine learning extensively — a company that the original clause would have covered and the amended one does not. Nobody has to argue about anything, which is exactly what the four days bought.

## Key Takeaways

- Non-compete clauses must be in writing, and in fixed-term contracts require a written justification specific to the role, while permanent-contract clauses can still be limited or set aside by a court.
- Distinguish non-compete, client non-solicitation and confidentiality — and read any penalty clause alongside them.
- Reasonableness turns on activity, geography, duration, justification, compensation and penalty; "any company active in AI" is the classic disproportion.
- Negotiate before signing: name actual competitors, shorten the duration, add carve-outs, reduce the penalty and ask for a release commitment.
- If you already signed one, ask your employer to confirm in writing whether they will enforce it, and take advice if the restriction is disproportionate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate reading a contract) Are non-compete clauses enforceable in the Netherlands?
They can be, but courts regularly limit or set them aside where the employee is unfairly disadvantaged, and fixed-term contracts require a written justification specific to the role.

### (Scenario: candidate in a specialised field) What if the clause covers my whole field?
That is the classic disproportion. Negotiate it down to named competitors and a shorter period before signing, and take advice if the employer refuses.

### (Scenario: candidate with an existing clause) I already signed one. What now?
Check the wording and whether your role has changed, then ask your employer to confirm in writing whether they will enforce it for the specific role you want.

### (Scenario: consultant or seconded professional) Which clause matters most for consultancy work?
Usually the client non-solicitation clause, which can prevent you from joining a client where you were placed, regardless of any non-compete.

### (Scenario: candidate about to negotiate) What is realistic to ask for?
Named competitors instead of a market description, six months instead of twelve, carve-outs for unrelated sectors, a reduced penalty and a written release commitment.
