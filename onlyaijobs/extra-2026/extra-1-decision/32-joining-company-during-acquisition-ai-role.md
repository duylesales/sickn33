Halfway through the process, the news arrives — sometimes from the recruiter, sometimes from a press release, occasionally from a colleague who saw it first. The company you are about to join is being acquired, or is merging, or has just been bought by a private equity firm. The role you interviewed for was designed by people who may not be making decisions in six months. Candidates react in two predictable ways: withdrawing immediately, or ignoring it because the work still sounds good. Both skip the useful middle, which is finding out what the deal actually means for the job you were offered.

## What Changes and What Does Not

The first thing worth knowing is that your employment position is better protected in the Netherlands than most candidates assume.

Under Dutch law implementing the European transfer-of-undertaking rules, when a business or part of a business transfers to another owner, the employees working there transfer automatically with their existing terms and conditions. Dismissal because of the transfer itself is not permitted, though reorganisations for other reasons remain possible afterwards. Collective agreement terms generally continue to apply for a period.

Two caveats matter. These rules apply to transfers of an undertaking; a pure share purchase leaves your employer legally the same entity, so nothing transfers — the shareholder changes, not the contract. And protection of terms is not protection of your role's content, your manager, your team or your project.

## Why Data Teams Are Particularly Exposed

An acquisition rarely eliminates the need for logistics or accounting. Data teams are different, for three reasons.

**Duplication.** Both organisations usually have data capabilities, and the acquirer's platform typically wins. Roles that exist twice become one.

**Roadmap displacement.** The interesting project you were hired for competes with integration work that has a hard deadline, and integration usually wins.

**Strategic reversal.** A parent company may have its own view on whether AI development happens locally or centrally, which can turn an ambitious team into a support function.

Against that, acquisitions also create work: merging data landscapes, harmonising definitions, rebuilding reporting and governance. If you enjoy that kind of problem, an acquisition is an opportunity rather than a threat — it is simply a different job than the one advertised.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing

The employer will not be able to answer everything, and that is normal during a deal. How they handle the questions is itself informative.

- **Has the transaction been signed, or is it still in negotiation?** Announced intentions fall through regularly.
- **Is this a share purchase or a transfer of the business?** It determines whether transfer-of-undertaking rules apply to you.
- **Has the works council given its advice?** Under the Works Councils Act, works councils have an advisory right on decisions such as transfers of control, and that process reveals the intended structure.
- **Who will my manager report to after completion?** Vague answers mean the structure is undecided.
- **Does the acquirer have an existing data team, and what does it do?** The single best predictor of whether your role survives intact.
- **Is my role part of the plan presented to the acquirer?** Roles in the business case tend to survive; roles added just before a deal often do not.
- **What happens to equity or option schemes?** Deals frequently trigger acceleration, cancellation or conversion, and the terms differ per scheme.
- **Will the collective agreement or employment conditions change?**

## Timing Matters More Than the Deal Itself

**Before announcement.** You may be joining without knowing. Nothing to do here beyond ordinary diligence, though an employer who knows and says nothing has told you something about their honesty.

**Announced, not completed.** The most uncertain window. Regulatory review may be required — in some cases by the Authority for Consumers and Markets — and plans are provisional. This is when asking questions is most useful and answers are least certain.

**Recently completed.** Integration planning is underway and the first reorganisations often follow within months. Ask directly whether a reorganisation is planned and what has already been decided.

**Two years after.** Usually stable again, and the acquirer's culture has become the culture.

## Signals That Argue for Joining Anyway

- The acquirer has no comparable data capability, so yours is the one being bought.
- Your role appears in the integration plan with a named owner.
- The hiring manager is staying and has been given a defined mandate.
- The acquisition is strategic rather than financial, with investment rather than cost reduction as the stated logic.
- You would find integration work genuinely interesting.
- The offer includes protection: a longer notice period, a defined scope in writing, or a severance arrangement if the role changes materially.

## Signals That Argue for Waiting

- The acquirer has a large existing data team doing similar work.
- Nobody can tell you who your manager will report to.
- The deal is driven by cost synergies, which usually means headcount reduction somewhere.
- Your role was created recently, which makes it easier to unwind.
- You would be leaving a stable permanent position for this.
- The people interviewing you seem as uncertain as you are, and say so.

## What You Can Negotiate in This Situation

Uncertainty is a negotiating position, and reasonable employers accept that.

Ask for the scope of the role to be described in the contract or an accompanying letter, so that a material change is identifiable later. Ask about a severance arrangement if the role is eliminated within the first year — unusual, but not unheard of when an employer genuinely needs a specific hire during a deal. Ask for a start date after completion, if that is feasible, so you join a known structure. And if the offer includes equity, ask specifically what the transaction does to it, in writing.

If the employer refuses all of this while acknowledging the uncertainty, weigh that. You would be accepting the risk alone.

## If You Are Already Employed There

For completeness, the reverse situation follows the same logic. Your terms are protected on a transfer of undertaking, but your role content is not. The practical advice is to document what you own, keep your network warm, and use the integration period deliberately — post-merger data work is genuinely valuable experience, and being the person who harmonised two data landscapes is a story that travels well.

Also watch the works council process, which is where the intended structure becomes visible earliest, and the GDPR questions that surface when customer and employee data from two organisations are combined. Those questions frequently need someone who understands both the data and the rules — which can be exactly the role you make for yourself.

## The First Hundred Days After a Deal

If you join during or shortly after a transaction, the early months follow a recognisable pattern, and knowing it helps you use them well.

**Weeks one to four: information gathering.** The acquirer maps what exists, usually through workshops and inventories. Your systems, your data landscape and your roadmap get documented by people you have not met. Being the person who explains things clearly here is unusually visible, and it is the cheapest reputation you will ever build.

**Weeks four to twelve: duplication decisions.** Where both organisations have capability, choices get made about which platform, which team and which way of working continues. Roles are rarely cut at this stage, but direction is set.

**Month three onwards: integration work begins.** Data landscapes must be merged, definitions harmonised, reporting rebuilt. This is where a new joiner can genuinely add value, because you have no history in either organisation and no attachment to either version.

Three practical habits help. Document what you own early, because nobody else can. Meet your counterparts on the other side quickly rather than waiting to be introduced. And keep delivering something small and visible, since teams that produce during integration are rarely the ones reorganised.

The period is uncomfortable and finite. Professionals who treat it as an opportunity to learn how two data landscapes are merged end up with experience that is scarce and portable.

## Real example

### An ML engineer in Amsterdam who asked about the acquirer's team

An ML engineer receives an offer from a scale-up, and a week later the company announces it is being acquired by a larger international group. The recruiter insists nothing will change.

He asks three questions. Does the acquirer have a data team? Yes, around forty people, in another country. Is the role in the business case presented to them? The hiring manager does not know. Who will she report to after completion? Undecided.

He does not withdraw, but he changes the terms. He asks for the role's scope to be described in an annex to the contract, a start date after the expected completion, and written confirmation of what happens to the option scheme.

The employer agrees to the scope annex and the start date, and confirms the options will be cancelled with a cash settlement. He accepts, aware that the role may change. It does — within a year the team's mandate narrows and he spends nine months on integration work. He stays two years, learns a great deal about merging data landscapes, and leaves on his own timing.

## Who Is Buying Matters More Than the Fact of the Deal

"Being acquired" describes very different futures depending on the buyer, and the distinction is usually knowable before you sign.

**A strategic buyer in the same sector** typically wants the product, the customers or the technology. Duplication risk is highest here, because they often already have a data team — but so is investment, if your capability is the thing they lacked.

**A strategic buyer entering a new market** is buying expertise it does not have. This is usually the most favourable case for a data team: you are the reason for the purchase rather than an overlap to be resolved.

**A private equity or investment owner** is buying a financial return over a defined horizon. That can mean investment in growth, and it can mean cost discipline and a focus on measurable efficiency. Ask what the stated plan is, and ask whether the management team is staying.

**A merger of near-equals** produces the longest uncertainty, because the structure is negotiated rather than imposed and decisions take longer.

One question cuts through all of it: what problem is the buyer solving by making this purchase? If the honest answer is "they want what this team does", your position is strong. If it is "they want the customer base", the data team is a cost line in someone's model.

## Key Takeaways

- On a transfer of undertaking, Dutch rules transfer employees with their existing terms and prohibit dismissal because of the transfer; a share purchase leaves your employer legally unchanged.
- Protection covers your terms, not your role content, manager, team or project — which is where acquisitions actually bite for data teams.
- Duplication with the acquirer's existing data capability is the strongest predictor of whether your role survives intact.
- Ask whether the deal is signed, whether the works council has advised, whether your role is in the business case and what happens to equity.
- Negotiate a scope annex, a start date after completion and written clarity on options; uncertainty is a legitimate negotiating position.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate mid-process during an acquisition) Should I withdraw if the company is being acquired?
Not automatically. Find out whether the acquirer has a comparable data team, whether your role is in the business case and who your manager will report to.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about job security) Am I protected if the business is transferred?
Dutch transfer-of-undertaking rules transfer employees with their existing terms and prohibit dismissal because of the transfer, though later reorganisations for other reasons remain possible.

### (Scenario: candidate offered options) What happens to my stock options in an acquisition?
It depends entirely on the scheme: acceleration, cancellation with or without settlement, or conversion. Ask for written confirmation before signing.

### (Scenario: candidate asking about process) What does the works council have to do with it?
Works councils have an advisory right on decisions such as transfers of control, and that process usually reveals the intended structure earlier than public announcements.

### (Scenario: candidate seeking protection) What can I negotiate during a deal?
A written description of the role's scope, a start date after completion, clarity on equity, and occasionally a severance arrangement if the role is eliminated early.
