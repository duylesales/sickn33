You finish a degree or a retraining programme and discover that "entry level" in the Dutch AI market is not one thing. There are graduation internships, traineeship programmes with impressive names, ordinary junior vacancies and consultancy or secondment firms that will hire you quickly and place you somewhere else. They look similar from the outside, they pay very differently, and they commit you to very different things. Choosing between them deserves more thought than most graduates give it, because the route determines what you learn in your first two years and how easily you can move afterwards.

## The Four Routes

**Internship or graduation project.** A learning position, formalised in an internship agreement between you, your educational institution and the host organisation. Typically a few months, often with an allowance rather than a salary.

**Traineeship.** An employment contract, usually one or two years, combining work with a structured training programme. Run either by an employer for its own needs or by a company that trains you and places you with clients.

**Junior role.** An ordinary employment contract for a defined job, with normal salary and conditions and no training programme attached beyond the usual.

**Consultancy or secondment start.** An employment contract with a firm that places you at clients, often with training in the first months and assignments afterwards.

## A Direct Comparison

| Dimension | Internship | Traineeship | Junior role | Consultancy/secondment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | Internship agreement | Employment contract | Employment contract | Employment contract |
| Duration | Months | One to two years | Open-ended or fixed term | Open-ended, assignments vary |
| Pay | Allowance, not legally required | Usually below market junior level | Market rate for the role | Often competitive, with a margin taken |
| Structured training | Via the institution | Central promise of the route | Rarely formalised | Common at the start |
| Who you work for | Host organisation | Employer or client | Employer | Client, while employed by the firm |
| Exit freedom | Ends naturally | May carry training repayment terms | Normal notice period | May carry client non-solicitation terms |
| Best for | Exploring, finishing a degree | Structure and a peer group | Doing the actual work | Breadth and fast exposure |

## What Each Route Is Genuinely Good For

**Internships** are the cheapest way to find out whether a domain suits you, and the most reliable route into a company that does not usually hire juniors. For students, a graduation project is also the only route that satisfies your programme's requirements at the same time.

**Traineeships** provide what most junior roles lack: a structured curriculum, a cohort of peers starting at the same time and a defined development path. For someone who worries about being the only inexperienced person in a team, that structure is worth something real.

**Junior roles** get you doing the work immediately at a normal salary, inside a team that needs you for what you produce rather than for what you might become. When the team is good, this is usually the fastest route to competence.

**Consultancy and secondment** give breadth: several sectors and data landscapes in a few years, plus training budgets that individual employers rarely match. For people who do not yet know which domain they want, that exposure is valuable.

## The Questions That Separate Good From Bad Versions

Every route has strong and weak versions. These questions identify which one you are looking at.

**For an internship:** Who supervises me, and how often? Is the data available before I start? Is there a realistic chance of a job afterwards, and has that happened before?

**For a traineeship:** What exactly is the training — hours, content, who delivers it? Am I employed by you or placed with clients? Is there a training cost repayment clause, and under what conditions does it apply? What did last year's cohort do afterwards?

**For a junior role:** Who reviews my code? Is there anyone more senior in this discipline? What is the first project?

**For consultancy or secondment:** How are assignments chosen, and can I decline one? What happens between assignments? Is there a client non-solicitation clause that would stop me joining a client later?

## Pay and Contract Terms

**Internship allowances** are not legally required in the Netherlands for genuine learning internships, though they are common and increasingly expected in technical fields. If an "internship" involves ordinary productive work rather than learning, it may in substance be employment, with the obligations that follow.

**Traineeship salaries** are frequently below the market rate for a junior role, on the argument that training has value. Sometimes that is fair. Sometimes it is a discount without a corresponding curriculum, which is why asking for the training schedule in writing matters.

**Training repayment clauses** deserve attention. Since the implementation of the EU directive on transparent and predictable working conditions, training that an employer is legally or contractually obliged to provide for the job must be free of charge and cannot be subject to repayment. Other training can carry a clearly agreed repayment clause, typically decreasing over time. A traineeship that would cost you thousands to leave after a year is a commitment, not a gift.

**Pay transparency.** As the EU Pay Transparency Directive is implemented, employers must give you pay information before the interview, which makes comparing these routes considerably easier than it used to be.

## How Each Route Affects Your Second Job

Hiring managers reading a CV two years later care about evidence, not labels.

An internship that produced something concrete — a model in use, a documented pipeline, a report that changed a decision — reads well. An internship with nothing to show reads as time passed.

A traineeship at a known programme signals selection and structure, which helps. A traineeship where you spent a year on reporting tasks while attending occasional workshops does not, and interviewers ask what you actually built.

A junior role is judged entirely on what you did. That is the most honest and the least forgiving.

A consultancy start reads as breadth, and raises one predictable question: have you ever lived with the consequences of your own work? Prepare an answer that shows ownership beyond delivery.

## For International Graduates

If you studied in the Netherlands, the orientation year for highly educated persons allows you to work without a separate permit during a defined window. This makes internships and junior roles straightforward during that period. Traineeships and consultancy roles are employment, so check whether the employer can sponsor afterwards if needed, and confirm current conditions with the immigration service before committing. Time-limited permits generally argue for routes that start earning and building experience quickly.

## Combining Work and Study

A fifth option sits across the others: taking a junior role while studying part-time. For career switchers in particular, this often beats choosing between earning and learning.

Dutch higher education offers part-time and distance programmes, including at the Open Universiteit, alongside shorter accredited courses and micro-credentials. Many employers contribute to the costs, and some allow part of the study during working hours — which is worth asking about at the offer stage rather than after starting.

Two practical points. First, check the training cost repayment terms before agreeing. Training an employer is legally or contractually required to provide for your job must be free of charge and cannot carry a repayment clause; other study can, typically decreasing over time. Second, be realistic about capacity. A demanding junior role plus a part-time programme is sustainable for many people and exhausting for some; agreeing a four-day week for the duration is a common and sensible compromise.

The advantage over a traineeship is that you keep a normal salary and a real job while the structured learning happens in parallel — and the qualification belongs to you rather than to a programme you may want to leave.

## The Route Most People Underestimate

The junior role at an ordinary regional employer — a manufacturer, an insurer, a hospital, a water board — is the option graduates most often overlook, because it has no programme name and no cohort. It frequently offers the best combination of real work, normal pay and a short commute.

The single thing to check is whether anyone will review your work. A junior role with a competent reviewer beats a prestigious traineeship with vague training. A junior role with nobody to learn from is the one case where a structured programme is clearly worth the lower salary.

## Switching Routes Later

None of these choices is permanent, and the transitions between them are worth knowing before you start.

**From internship to employment** is the most common and the easiest, especially at the host organisation. It works best when you were visible beyond your supervisor, delivered something people use and raised the question of a job before your final month rather than on your last day.

**From traineeship to a direct role** happens frequently at the end of a programme, either with a client where you were placed or elsewhere. Two things matter: whether a repayment clause still applies, and whether a client non-solicitation clause limits joining the organisation where you worked.

**From consultancy to in-house** is a well-worn path after two to four years, and employers value the breadth. The predictable interview question is about ownership, so prepare an example where you lived with the consequences of your own work rather than handing it over.

**From a junior role to a structured programme** is rarer, because programmes usually target recent graduates. If structure is what you are missing, an internal move or a mentor arrangement is generally easier to obtain than a place in a traineeship.

The general pattern: it is easier to move from breadth towards depth than the reverse. If you are genuinely undecided, the route that keeps the most doors open is the one where you do real work early and can point to something concrete a year later.

## Real example

### A graduate in Tilburg choosing between three routes

A data science graduate in Tilburg has three options: a six-month graduation-style internship at a logistics company, a two-year traineeship at a consultancy with a training programme and a lower salary, and a junior analyst role at a regional insurer twenty minutes from home.

She asks the questions. The internship has a supervisor with time and data ready, but no budget for a subsequent hire this year. The traineeship turns out to combine two weeks of introductory training with client placements, and a repayment clause applying if she leaves within eighteen months. The insurer has an actuarial team, a named reviewer for her work and a specific first project on claims handling times.

She takes the junior role. Within a year she has built something used weekly and learned more about insurance than any curriculum would teach. Two years later she moves to an insurtech company that wanted exactly that domain experience.

The traineeship was not a bad option. It was a worse one for her, once she knew what the training actually contained.

## Key Takeaways

- The four entry routes differ in legal basis, pay, training, freedom to leave and what they teach.
- Internships suit exploration and access to employers who rarely hire juniors; traineeships suit people who want structure and peers.
- Junior roles get you doing real work at market pay, provided someone competent reviews it.
- Consultancy and secondment give breadth quickly, with client non-solicitation terms worth checking before signing.
- Ask for the training schedule, the repayment clause and last year's cohort outcomes in writing before choosing a programme over a normal job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: graduate comparing offers) Is a traineeship better than a junior job?
Only when the training is substantial and documented. A junior role with a competent reviewer often teaches more than a programme with occasional workshops and a lower salary.

### (Scenario: student considering an internship) Do internships in the Netherlands have to be paid?
An allowance is not legally required for genuine learning internships, though it is common in technical fields. Work that is mainly productive rather than educational may count as employment.

### (Scenario: candidate offered a traineeship) Can I be required to repay training costs if I leave?
Not for training the employer must legally or contractually provide for the job. Other training can carry a clearly agreed repayment clause, so read it before signing.

### (Scenario: graduate weighing consultancy) Is starting at a consultancy a good first job?
It offers breadth and training, with less ownership of long-term outcomes. Check how assignments are allocated and whether a client non-solicitation clause limits future moves.

### (Scenario: international graduate) Which route suits someone on an orientation year permit?
Routes that start real work quickly, since the permit window is limited; confirm sponsorship possibilities with the immigration service before committing to a longer programme.
