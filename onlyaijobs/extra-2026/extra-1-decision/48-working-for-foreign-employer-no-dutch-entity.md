The approach arrives by message: an interesting company abroad, a strong salary in another currency, work you would enjoy, and you would stay where you live. Somewhere in the third conversation it emerges that they have no Dutch entity, and that the arrangement will run through "our global employment partner" or, more casually, "you can invoice us monthly". Both can be entirely legitimate. Both change your position in ways that matter — social security, pension, protection during illness, what happens if the relationship ends. In a market where remote hiring across borders has become ordinary, this is a decision worth understanding rather than trusting.

This article provides general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Arrangements differ per case, and it is worth paying for an hour of professional advice before signing. The free service **Juridisch Loket** can also point you in the right direction.

## The Three Structures You Will Encounter

**Employer of record.** A local provider employs you in the Netherlands and makes you available to the foreign company, handling payroll, wage tax and social security. You get a Dutch employment contract with a company you have never heard of, while working for the client.

**Direct employment by the foreign employer.** They employ you under a foreign or Dutch-law contract while you work from the Netherlands. This is possible, and it creates obligations for them around social security and, depending on their presence, wage tax.

**Contractor arrangement.** You invoice as a self-employed professional. Simple to start, and the arrangement raises questions under Dutch rules on employment relationships if the reality looks like employment.

Each has a different risk profile, and the one you are offered often reflects the employer's convenience rather than your interests.

## Why Social Security Is the Part to Get Right

Within the EU, social security coordination is governed by **Regulation 883/2004**, and the basic rule is that you are insured where you work rather than where your employer is established. If you live and work in the Netherlands, Dutch social security generally applies, and someone must pay the contributions.

Where a foreign employer has no Dutch establishment, arrangements exist under EU implementing rules by which the employee may take on the administration of contributions on the employer's behalf. That is legitimate and administratively significant: it means you are responsible for something an employer normally handles.

What matters practically is the outcome: are you covered for unemployment, illness and disability through the Dutch system, and who is paying? Ask the employer to state this in writing. Coverage gaps are discovered at exactly the wrong moment.

## Which Employment Law Applies

Under the **Rome I Regulation**, parties can choose the law governing an employment contract, but that choice cannot deprive the employee of the protection of mandatory provisions of the law that would otherwise apply — generally the law of the country where the work is habitually carried out.

In practice, if you live and work in the Netherlands, core Dutch protections tend to apply regardless of what the contract says it is governed by. That includes areas such as dismissal protection, notice, minimum holiday and sick pay obligations, although the analysis is case-specific.

The complication is enforcement rather than entitlement. Asserting a right against an employer with no Dutch presence is slower and more expensive than against one down the road, which is a practical argument for structures that put a Dutch entity in the chain.

## What You Lose Without a Dutch Employer

**Pension.** Most Dutch employees accrue pension through an employer scheme, and many sectors have mandatory industry funds. A foreign employer with no Dutch entity frequently offers nothing, and the salary premium rarely compensates for a missing employer contribution.

**Sick pay in practice.** Dutch employers have long-running obligations to continue paying wages during illness and to work on reintegration. Whether and how a foreign employer meets those is something to clarify explicitly rather than assume.

**Occupational health support.** Access to an occupational physician and a reintegration process is standard here and often absent in a foreign arrangement.

**Mortgage acceptance.** Lenders assess income stability and contract type. A foreign employment contract or a contractor arrangement makes borrowing meaningfully harder, particularly in your first years.

**Everyday administration.** Payslips in a familiar format, annual statements, holiday allowance, thirteenth month: all of this is simpler when a Dutch entity runs payroll.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing

- **Which structure is this, exactly?** Employer of record, direct employment or contractor.
- **Who registers and pays social security contributions, and under which arrangement?**
- **Which law governs the contract, and which country's courts?**
- **What happens if I am ill for six months?** Ask for the specific answer, not a reassurance.
- **Is there a pension contribution, and to which scheme?**
- **How is wage tax handled?** If nothing is withheld, you will owe it yourself, so plan for it.
- **What notice applies on both sides?**
- **Who provides equipment and covers home working costs?**
- **If an employer of record is used, which company is my legal employer, and what are their terms?**

## When the Arrangement Is Genuinely Fine

- A reputable employer of record with a Dutch contract, payroll, social security and ideally a pension arrangement.
- A large foreign employer with an established process for hiring in the Netherlands and named advisers.
- A contractor arrangement where you genuinely operate as an independent professional, with multiple clients, your own tools and control over how you work.
- A compensation package that explicitly covers what is missing — pension substitute, insurance, allowances — rather than a headline salary that quietly excludes them.

## When to Decline or Restructure

- The employer cannot explain who pays social security.
- You are asked to invoice while working exclusively for them, on their schedule, under their direction — which is employment in substance, and the Dutch rules on employment relationships are being enforced more actively.
- No pension, no illness arrangement and no compensation for either.
- Contract governed by a distant jurisdiction with disputes to be settled there.
- The only reason for the structure is to avoid obligations, which tells you how other obligations will be treated.
- You need mortgage stability in the next two years.

## The Self-Employment Trap

The contractor route deserves particular caution for AI and data professionals, because the work often looks exactly like employment: one client, integrated into their team, their tools, their meetings, their priorities. Dutch rules on the assessment of employment relationships look at how the relationship works in practice rather than at the label on the invoice, and enforcement in this area has been tightened.

If you go this route deliberately, structure it deliberately: more than one client where possible, your own equipment, your own working method, a real contract for services, proper insurance for disability and a pension arrangement you fund yourself. If the client insists you behave as an employee but be paid as a contractor, they are asking you to carry a risk they have chosen not to.

## What to Negotiate

Ask for the structure that puts a Dutch entity in the chain, even if it costs them a little more. Ask for a pension contribution or an explicit salary uplift that you can invest yourself. Ask for the illness scenario to be written down. Ask for equipment and home working costs. And ask for notice terms that are symmetrical.

If the employer is serious about hiring in the Netherlands, these requests are normal and often already anticipated. If they are improvising, your questions will show it — and improvisation is the main risk in these arrangements.

## The Tax Side, in Outline

Tax is where these arrangements most often produce an unpleasant surprise in the second year, so it is worth understanding the shape of it even though the details are individual.

**Withholding may not happen automatically.** A Dutch employer withholds wage tax from your salary, so the amount that arrives is yours. A foreign employer without a Dutch withholding obligation may pay you gross, which means the tax is still due — later, from you, in a lump sum. People who spend the gross figure discover this at exactly the wrong moment.

**Set money aside from month one.** If nothing is withheld, treat a substantial share of each payment as not yours. A provisional assessment arrangement with the tax authority can spread the payments across the year instead of leaving one large bill.

**Contractor status changes your obligations further.** Invoicing brings VAT questions, administration requirements and annual filings, alongside the income tax itself.

**Special arrangements may not apply.** Facilities for incoming employees, such as the expatriate scheme, generally attach to conditions that include the employer's position in the Dutch system. Do not assume such a facility is available in a structure without a Dutch employer; check before counting on it.

**Two countries can both take an interest.** Treaties determine which country taxes what, and cross-border arrangements are precisely where an hour with an adviser pays for itself many times over.

The practical rule is simple: never compare a gross foreign figure with a Dutch net salary. Work out what actually reaches your account after tax, contributions, pension you must fund yourself and insurance you must buy, and compare that.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Groningen who asked who pays what

A data scientist is offered a role by a foreign technology company with no Dutch entity. The salary is higher than local offers and the work is genuinely interesting. The proposal is that she invoices monthly as a freelancer.

She asks three questions: who pays social security, what happens if she is ill for six months, and what pension arrangement exists. The answers are, respectively, that she would arrange it herself, that it would be "discussed if it happened", and none.

She does not decline. She counters: employment through an employer of record with a Dutch contract, or a contractor arrangement with a rate uplift covering disability insurance, a pension contribution and the absence of sick pay.

The company chooses the employer of record. Her net position is slightly below the original headline figure and substantially better in everything that is not salary. Two years later, when she applies for a mortgage, the Dutch employment contract is what makes it straightforward.

## Key Takeaways

- Foreign employers hire in the Netherlands through an employer of record, direct employment or a contractor arrangement, and each has a very different risk profile.
- Within the EU, social security generally follows where you work; establish in writing who registers and pays.
- Mandatory protections of the country where you habitually work tend to apply regardless of the contract's chosen law, but enforcement across borders is slower and costlier.
- What is usually missing is pension, a clear illness arrangement, occupational health support and mortgage-friendly documentation.
- Negotiate a structure with a Dutch entity in the chain, an explicit pension or uplift, and written answers on illness, notice and equipment.
