The team is six people and will be twelve by the end of the year, or twelve and heading for twenty-five. It is presented as the reason to join, and it is a genuine signal: somebody with budget has decided this capability matters. It is also the condition that produces the most predictable set of problems in data teams — onboarding that nobody has capacity to do, conventions that fork three ways, seniors who stop building because they are teaching, and a correction eighteen months later when the growth stops. Rapid hiring is neither good nor bad news. It is a situation with a known failure pattern, and you can find out in one conversation whether this team is managing it.

## Why Teams Scale Suddenly

**New funding or a new mandate.** An investment round, a strategic decision, a regulatory programme with a budget attached.

**A backlog that became visible.** The organisation finally counted what it was not doing.

**Insourcing.** Replacing consultancy spend with employees, which often means hiring several people at once.

**A reorganisation.** Teams merged or a central function created, where growth is partly relabelling.

**Competitive panic.** A peer organisation built a data team, so this one must.

The first three usually come with a plan. The last two frequently do not, and the difference shows up in whether anyone can describe what the additional people will work on.

## What Fast Hiring Does to a Team

**Onboarding debt.** Each new person needs weeks of an experienced colleague's time. Hire four at once and the experienced colleagues stop producing, which is rarely reflected in anyone's planning.

**Conventions fork.** Three new engineers arrive with three sets of habits, and without an owner for standards, the codebase acquires dialects within a quarter.

**Seniors become teachers.** Sometimes welcome, often unplanned, and it changes the job of the people who were the reason you wanted to join.

**Ownership blurs.** Who maintains what becomes unclear precisely when the number of things to maintain is growing.

**Culture dilutes.** Not in the atmospheric sense, but in the practical one: the shared understanding of how work is done here stops being shared.

**Quality dips before it recovers.** Almost universal, and survivable if it is expected.

## The Senior Ratio Question

The single most useful number is how many experienced people there are relative to new ones.

A team of six with four seniors that hires six juniors will be strained but fine. A team of six with one senior that hires six people of any level is heading somewhere difficult, because there is no capacity to review, teach or hold standards.

Ask directly: of the people already here, how many have more than three years of relevant experience, and how many of the planned hires are senior? Then ask who is expected to onboard the new arrivals, and whether their delivery expectations have been reduced accordingly. The answer to that second question tells you whether the growth has been planned or merely approved.

## What It Means for You, by Level

**If you are senior,** expect to spend a significant part of your first year teaching and standard-setting, whether or not the role describes it. That is an opportunity if you want lead experience and a frustration if you came to build.

**If you are mid-level,** this is often an excellent moment to join: responsibility arrives faster than in a stable team, and the gap between you and the seniors narrows quickly.

**If you are junior,** it depends entirely on the senior ratio. A growing team with mentoring capacity is the best place to start a career; one without it is the worst, because nobody has time to correct you.

## Questions to Ask

- **How many people are here now, how many are planned, and by when?**
- **What will the new people work on?** A hiring plan without a work plan is a warning.
- **How many of the current team are experienced, and who onboards new joiners?**
- **Has anyone's delivery expectation been reduced to make room for onboarding?**
- **Who owns technical standards?**
- **How did the last three new joiners find their first months?** Ask to speak to one of them.
- **What happened to the team's output during the last growth spurt?**
- **What would cause the hiring to stop?**

## Warning Signs

- Nobody can describe what the additional people will do beyond "there is a lot to do".
- The team lost several people recently and the growth is partly replacement, unmentioned.
- There is no onboarding material and no plan to create any.
- One senior person is the answer to every question about who knows something.
- Hiring is being driven by a headcount target rather than by a work plan.
- Recent joiners are unavailable to speak to.

## What Happens When Growth Stops

Every rapid expansion ends, and the ending matters. If the growth was tied to funding or a programme, the correction can be abrupt: hiring freezes, and occasionally a reduction, with the most recent arrivals feeling most exposed regardless of how selection formally works in a Dutch reorganisation.

This is not a reason to avoid growing teams. It is a reason to ask what the growth is funded by, and to notice whether the team is being built around durable work — systems the organisation depends on — or around a programme with an end date.

## When to Accept

- The hiring plan is matched by a work plan you can understand.
- The senior ratio supports the growth, and onboarding is someone's explicit responsibility.
- Standards have an owner.
- You are mid-level or senior and want responsibility to arrive quickly.
- Recent joiners speak well of their first months.
- The growth is funded by something durable rather than a single programme.

## When to Decline

- One senior person is carrying everything.
- You are junior and nobody has capacity to teach you.
- There is no plan for what the new people will do.
- The team is growing while quietly losing people.
- Onboarding is described as "you will pick it up".
- The growth depends entirely on funding that ends next year.

## How to Arrive Well

Write your own onboarding document as you learn, and give it to the next person. In a fast-growing team this is the most appreciated thing a new joiner can do, and it makes you visible immediately.

Adopt the existing conventions before proposing better ones, even if yours are better. A team acquiring three dialects at once does not need a fourth opinion in week two.

Take ownership of something specific and unambiguous, early. In teams where ownership is blurring, being clearly responsible for one thing is both useful and protective.

Find the person who has been there longest and spend time with them. In a team where most people are new, institutional memory is concentrated and precious.

And expect the first quarter to feel chaotic. In a team that is doubling, it is chaotic for everyone, including the people who look settled.

## The Hiring Process Itself Is Evidence

A team that is hiring quickly reveals a great deal through how it hires, and you experience that process directly before you have to decide anything.

**Did the technical conversation have substance?** Teams under pressure to fill seats sometimes lower the bar without admitting it. If your technical interview was superficial, ask yourself who else passed through it — your future colleagues were assessed the same way.

**Who interviewed you?** If you met only the manager and a recruiter, the team has no say in who joins it, which usually means onboarding is nobody's responsibility either.

**How fast was the process?** Speed is often good and can indicate desperation. A process that moved from application to offer in five days, for a role that requires judgement, is worth a question about how many people they still need to hire.

**Was the role described consistently?** In fast-scaling teams, roles are frequently created faster than they are defined, and inconsistent descriptions predict a first quarter spent negotiating what your job is.

**Did anyone push back on you?** A good sign, oddly. Interviewers who probe and disagree are protecting a standard; interviewers who agree with everything are filling a seat.

Ask, directly: how many people have you hired this year, and how many did you turn down? The ratio tells you whether a standard is being held, and managers who are proud of their hiring answer it immediately.

## Real example

### An engineer in Amsterdam who asked to speak to the last person hired

An engineer is offered a role at a company whose data team is going from seven to sixteen within a year. The pitch is the growth itself.

He asks what the nine new people will work on. The manager has a specific answer: three on a platform migration, four on two named product areas, two on governance work required by a compliance deadline. That answer is the thing that reassures him most.

He then asks to speak to the most recent joiner, who is candid — the first six weeks were disorganised, there was no onboarding material, and it improved after the team appointed someone to own it.

He accepts, and in his first month writes the onboarding guide he wished he had received, which is adopted for the next five hires.

Nine months in, the team is fourteen and the quality dip everyone warned about happened and passed. The two governance hires turn out to be the most secure roles in the team, because their work is tied to an obligation rather than to a budget.

His conclusion: the growth was not the reason to join or to stay — the work plan behind it was.

## Key Takeaways

- Rapid hiring signals investment and reliably produces onboarding debt, forking conventions, blurred ownership and a temporary quality dip.
- The decisive number is the senior ratio and whether anyone's delivery expectation has been reduced to make room for onboarding.
- A hiring plan without a work plan is the clearest warning available; ask specifically what the new people will do.
- Junior candidates should join only where mentoring capacity exists; mid-level candidates often benefit most from the speed at which responsibility arrives.
- Ask what funds the growth, because expansions end, and teams built around durable systems survive the correction better than teams built around a programme.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate offered a role in a growing team) Is rapid hiring a good sign?
It signals investment and creates predictable strain. Whether it is good depends on the senior ratio, whether onboarding is owned by someone and whether there is a work plan behind the headcount.

### (Scenario: junior candidate) Should I join a fast-growing team early in my career?
Only if there is mentoring capacity. A growing team with experienced people is an excellent place to start; one without them offers responsibility and no correction.

### (Scenario: senior candidate) What should I expect as a senior hire?
A significant share of your first year spent onboarding others and setting standards, whether or not the vacancy mentions it. Treat that as the job rather than as an interruption.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the plan) What is the clearest warning sign?
A hiring plan with no work plan. If nobody can say what the additional people will do beyond "there is a lot to do", the growth is a headcount target.

### (Scenario: candidate thinking ahead) What happens when the growth stops?
Hiring freezes and occasionally a correction. Ask what funds the expansion and whether the team is being built around durable systems or a programme with an end date.
