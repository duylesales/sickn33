Having two offers is a good problem, and it is still a problem. The pressure is real: both employers want an answer, one deadline is earlier than the other, and the comparison that looked obvious yesterday looks different this morning. Most people in this situation reach for the one number they can compare directly — the salary — and decide on that. Two years later, the people who regret their choice rarely mention money. They mention a manager, a stack that never got fixed, a commute that wore them down, or work that turned out to be reporting rather than modelling. A structured comparison takes an evening and is worth considerably more than the difference between two salary offers.

## Step One: Compare the Whole Package, Not the Salary

In the Netherlands, gross salary is only part of the picture. Put both offers side by side on the same terms.

**Holiday allowance.** Usually at least 8% of gross annual salary. Check whether quoted figures include or exclude it, because that alone can distort a comparison.

**Thirteenth month or bonus.** A fixed thirteenth month is very different from a discretionary bonus.

**Pension.** Employer contribution, employee contribution and scheme type. Pension schemes are changing under the Future Pensions Act, and a strong employer contribution can outweigh a modest salary difference over time.

**Travel reimbursement.** A full public transport card, a mobility budget or a per-kilometre allowance differ substantially, especially with a longer commute.

**Working hours.** A 36-hour full-time week versus 40 hours changes your effective hourly rate by a noticeable margin.

**Leave.** Statutory minimum versus a more generous arrangement, and whether extra days can be bought or sold.

**Training budget.** For AI roles, a real budget with protected time is worth more than most people assume.

**Collective agreement.** If one employer falls under a cao, salary scales, increases and conditions may be more predictable.

As the EU Pay Transparency Directive is implemented, employers must provide pay information before interviews and base pay on objective criteria — which makes it reasonable to ask where each offer sits within its range and what determines movement inside it.

## Step Two: Compare the Work Itself

Package comparisons are easy and rarely decisive. The work is harder to compare and matters more.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What is the first project, concretely? | Vague answers predict vague roles |
| Who else does this work here? | Peers determine how fast you grow |
| Does anything reach production, and how? | Models in notebooks are a career risk |
| Who reviews my work? | No review means no feedback loop |
| Where does the data come from, and can I see it? | Data maturity shapes everything you can do |
| Who uses the results, and do they want them? | An engaged sponsor is worth more than a title |
| What does the roadmap look like beyond this project? | Tells you whether the role has a second year |

If one employer answers these specifically and the other speaks in ambitions, you have learned something more important than the salary difference.

## Step Three: Compare the People

You will spend more waking hours with these people than with most of your friends.

Notice who you met. Did you speak to your future manager, or only to HR and a recruiter? Did you meet anyone who would be a peer? Were technical conversations curious or competitive?

Ask each employer a question that reveals culture: what happened the last time a project failed, and what changed afterwards. The answers differ enormously, and evasiveness is itself an answer.

If you were not offered a conversation with your direct manager before deciding, ask for one. Any reasonable employer will arrange it, and a refusal is information.

## Step Four: Compare the Commute Honestly

Convert both offers into weekly travel time using the stated office days, door to door at the hour you would actually travel. Three office days at fifty minutes each way is five hours a week; the same schedule at fifteen minutes is an hour and a half.

Then ask what happens when things go wrong: whether start times are flexible, whether an extra home day is possible in practice, and what the last kilometre looks like from the station or car park. Commute is the factor candidates most consistently underweight at the offer stage and most consistently cite when they leave.

## Step Five: Read Both Contracts Before Deciding

Ask for the draft contracts, not only the offer letters. Differences that matter for AI professionals include the probation period, which is capped by law and shorter than people expect; a non-compete clause, which in a fixed-term contract is only valid with written justification based on substantial business interests; intellectual property clauses that may reach beyond work created for the employer; training cost repayment conditions; and the contract type and duration.

Two offers that look identical financially can differ significantly here, and these terms are far easier to negotiate before you accept.

## A Scoring Method That Avoids Self-Deception

Write down your criteria before you look at the offers again, and weight them. A simple version: work content 30%, people and manager 25%, growth and learning 20%, total package 15%, commute and flexibility 10% — adjusted to your own situation.

Score each offer on each criterion, then check the result against your instinct. When the arithmetic and the instinct disagree, that disagreement is the useful part: either a criterion is weighted wrongly, or something you have not articulated is bothering you about one of the offers. Both are worth finding out before you sign.

## Talk to Someone Who Already Works There

Both employers have shown you their best version: the manager who has prepared, the office on a good day, the roadmap as it is meant to go. One conversation with a current employee gives you something different.

Ask each employer to arrange a short call with a future colleague — not a manager, and ideally someone who joined in the last year or two. Most reasonable organisations agree. Someone who joined recently remembers what surprised them, which is exactly what you want to know.

Useful questions: what surprised you in the first three months, what took longer than expected, how are decisions actually made, and what would you change if you could. Also ask what happened to the last person in a role like the one you are considering.

If you cannot get such a conversation, use your own network. People are generally willing to describe their employer honestly to someone considering joining, particularly if you ask specific questions rather than "is it a good place to work".

Two notes of caution. One person's experience is one data point, coloured by their team and manager. And a very negative account deserves a follow-up question about what they expected, because mismatched expectations produce unhappy employees at perfectly good organisations. Weigh it, do not let it decide alone.

## Managing Deadlines Without Burning Either Bridge

Deadlines are rarely as fixed as they sound. If one employer needs an answer before you have heard from the other, three things are legitimate and normal.

Ask the first employer for a short extension, with a reason and a specific date. Most agree; a refusal to give you a few days to make a considered decision tells you something about how they will treat you later.

Tell the second employer honestly that you have an offer with a deadline and ask whether their process can be accelerated. This is standard practice and usually produces a clear answer quickly.

Do not accept an offer you intend to reconsider. Withdrawing an acceptance is possible before a contract is signed, but in a small market it costs reputation, and the emotional cost of doing it badly is higher than most people expect.

## Ask What Happens After the First Year

Both offers describe a starting point. Few describe what comes after it, and that is where the difference between two similar roles usually shows up.

Ask each employer the same three questions. What does someone in this role typically work on in their second year? How have people in this position developed — internally, or by leaving? What would make you consider this hire a success twelve months from now?

The answers separate organisations that think about careers from those filling a gap. An employer who can describe a second-year project, name two people who grew from this role and state a concrete success measure has thought about the position as more than a vacancy.

Vague answers are not automatically disqualifying, particularly at smaller employers hiring their first data professional. But they shift weight towards the other factors: if nobody can describe where this leads, the work itself and the people you would learn from have to carry the decision.

It is also worth asking what happens if the first project fails. Every data project might, and the answer tells you whether you would be working somewhere that treats a negative result as information or as a personal failure.

## The Reversibility Test

When two options remain genuinely close, ask which mistake is easier to correct.

Probation periods in the Netherlands are short and capped by law, which cuts both ways: you can leave a bad fit quickly, and so can the employer. A role that builds transferable skills — production experience, a portfolio you can show, a domain that exists in many companies — keeps future options open. A role that is comfortable but narrow can quietly reduce them.

If neither offer is clearly better on substance, choose the one that leaves you with more choices in two years.

## Real example

### A data scientist in Breda with two offers and one spreadsheet

A data scientist in Breda holds two offers. The first, from a scale-up in Amsterdam, pays noticeably more, with hybrid work and three office days. The second, from a regional manufacturer twenty minutes from home, pays less, offers a thirteenth month and a full travel allowance, and falls under a collective agreement.

She builds the comparison. Including holiday allowance, the thirteenth month, pension contributions and travel reimbursement, the gap narrows substantially. Weekly commuting differs by almost four hours. On the work itself, the scale-up describes an existing team and a clear production pipeline; the manufacturer describes a first data hire with a specific quality-inspection problem and an engaged sponsor, but no peer to review her code.

She asks both employers one more question — who reviews my work — and the manufacturer arranges a part-time external mentor as part of the offer. That changes the balance.

She takes the manufacturer role, with the mentor arrangement in writing. Two years later she leads a small team there. The spreadsheet did not make the decision; it showed her which question was still unanswered.

## Key Takeaways

- Compare total packages on equal terms: holiday allowance, thirteenth month, pension, travel reimbursement, hours, leave, training budget and any collective agreement.
- The work, the people and whether anything reaches production matter more than the salary difference in almost every retrospective account.
- Convert commutes into weekly hours using real office days and real travel times before deciding.
- Read both draft contracts for probation, non-compete, IP and training repayment clauses, which are easier to negotiate before accepting.
- Weight your criteria in advance, and when the score and your instinct disagree, find out why before signing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing salaries) How do I compare two Dutch job offers fairly?
Put both on the same basis: gross salary plus holiday allowance, thirteenth month, pension contributions, travel reimbursement, working hours, leave and training budget.

### (Scenario: candidate with a deadline) Can I ask an employer for more time to decide?
Yes — ask for a specific short extension with a reason. Most employers agree, and an outright refusal says something about the organisation.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure which matters most) Which factor do people most often regret ignoring?
Commute and the quality of the manager and team. Salary differences fade; a long daily journey and a poor working relationship do not.

### (Scenario: candidate with two similar offers) What should I do when both offers score almost the same?
Choose the one that leaves you with more options in two years — usually the role that builds production experience and transferable skills.

### (Scenario: candidate who accepted and hesitates) Can I withdraw an acceptance before signing?
Legally it may be possible before a contract exists, but it damages your reputation in a small market. Ask for time before accepting instead.
