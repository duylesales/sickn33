An offer arrives from a company nobody in your study group has heard of: a manufacturer, a regional insurer, a hospital, a water board. The work is data work, the commute is twenty minutes and the people seemed reasonable. Meanwhile, the technology companies you actually applied to have either rejected you or gone silent. The instinct is to hold out — those are the employers that look right on a CV, and everyone says the first job sets the direction of a career. That instinct is worth examining, because for AI and data work specifically, the assumption behind it is often wrong.

## What the Two Starts Actually Look Like

**At a technology company**, you usually join an existing team with established practices: version control, code review, deployment pipelines, experimentation infrastructure, someone senior who reviews your work. You learn engineering discipline quickly and see how mature systems are run. You are also one of many, working on a defined slice, with limited exposure to why the business cares.

**At a non-tech employer**, you often join a small team or become the first or second data person. Practices may be immature, data access can be painful, and nobody may be able to review your code. In exchange you sit close to the problem, talk directly to the people who use your results, and see the full path from question to decision.

Neither is universally better preparation. They build different halves of the same career.

## A Direct Comparison

| Dimension | Technology company | Non-tech employer |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering practices | Usually strong | Variable, sometimes absent |
| Mentoring and review | Often available | Depends entirely on the team |
| Breadth of responsibility | Narrower, deeper | Broad, sometimes uncomfortably |
| Domain exposure | Limited | High — you learn a real industry |
| Impact visibility | Diffuse | Direct and immediate |
| CV recognition | Higher on paper | Lower, unless you explain it well |
| Data maturity | Generally higher | Frequently the main challenge |
| Salary | Often higher | Varies; commute and conditions may compensate |
| Risk of isolation | Low | Real, and worth checking before accepting |

## The Argument for Saying Yes

**Experience beats theory immediately.** Six months of imperfect real work teaches more than six months of applying. Hiring managers know the difference.

**Domain knowledge is scarce and valuable.** Understanding how a production line, claims process or care pathway actually works is the part employers consistently say is hard to teach. It compounds: a data scientist who understands insurance is more valuable in insurance than a stronger generalist.

**Broad responsibility accelerates learning.** Being involved in defining the question, finding the data, building the model and explaining the result gives you the full picture years earlier than a narrow role would.

**Visible impact is easier to achieve.** In an organisation without an analytics tradition, a genuinely useful forecast or dashboard can change how a department works, which is a far better interview story than a marginal improvement to a mature system.

**These employers are where much of the demand is.** In the Netherlands, a significant share of AI and data work sits in manufacturing, logistics, energy, healthcare, agrifood, insurance and the public sector — often at companies within commuting distance of where you already live, including employers such as Heijmans or IT service providers like Cegeka Nederland that show up in these markets.

## The Argument for Waiting

**You may not learn engineering discipline.** If nobody reviews your code and nothing reaches production, you can spend two years building habits you will later have to unlearn.

**Isolation is a genuine risk.** Being the only data person, with no peer to ask, is difficult early in a career and is the most common reason first data hires leave within a year.

**Some organisations do not know what they want.** "We want to do something with AI" without a sponsor, data access or a concrete problem produces frustration rather than experience.

**The next step can be harder to explain.** A first role at a well-known employer opens doors mechanically; a role at an unknown company requires you to explain what you did.

**Tooling may be limited.** If budget for cloud resources or modern tools is absent, your technical skills may stagnate.

## The Questions That Actually Decide It

Rather than weighing prestige, ask the employer six specific questions.

1. **Who will review my work?** A named person, internal or external, matters more than anything else on this list.
2. **What data is available today, and can I see a sample?** This separates real projects from ambitions.
3. **Which concrete problem am I hired to work on first?** A specific answer indicates a thought-through role.
4. **Who is the internal sponsor, and will they use the results?** Analytics nobody uses damages a career.
5. **What is the budget for tools, cloud and training?** A data hire without infrastructure is set up to fail.
6. **Has anyone here done this before?** If not, ask whether they will fund an external mentor or peer network.

Good answers to four or more of these make a non-tech role a strong start. Vague answers to most of them are a warning regardless of the employer's size or reputation.

## What the First Year Usually Looks Like in Each Setting

Abstract comparisons are less useful than a realistic picture of the first twelve months.

**At a technology company**, the first weeks are usually structured: an onboarding programme, access to systems that already work, a mentor or buddy and a first task scoped small enough to succeed. You learn the team's conventions before you change anything. By month three you are contributing to an existing codebase; by month six you own a component. The risk is narrowness — a full year can pass in which you never speak to a customer or see how the business decides anything.

**At a non-tech employer**, the first weeks are usually unstructured. You spend them finding out where the data lives, who owns which system and what people actually do all day. The first real task often turns out to be a data quality problem rather than a modelling problem. By month three you may have delivered something small that people use; by month six you have probably had at least one uncomfortable conversation about why a promised system cannot be built with the data that exists. The risk is drift — a year of firefighting reporting requests with nothing durable to show.

Both risks are manageable if you name them early. In a technology company, ask deliberately to join a project with business exposure. In a non-tech organisation, insist on one durable deliverable per half year — a documented pipeline, a model in production, a dashboard people rely on — and protect the time for it.

The honest summary: technology companies teach you how things should be built, non-tech employers teach you why anyone wants them built at all. Careers that go well usually contain both, in either order.

## Making a Non-Tech Role Work for Your Next Move

If you take it, structure the job so it builds transferable evidence.

**Insist on version control and documentation from day one,** even if nobody asks. This is the single habit that separates a portfolio you can show from work that vanishes.

**Get one thing into production,** however small. A model that runs on a schedule and is used weekly is worth more in interviews than three analyses in notebooks.

**Keep a record of impact,** in the organisation's own terms: hours saved, waste reduced, planning accuracy improved, complaints handled faster.

**Build an external network deliberately.** Meetups, communities and conferences replace the peers you do not have internally.

**Learn the regulated side.** Many non-tech sectors now face obligations under the EU AI Act, and experience with documentation, data governance and human oversight is increasingly valuable elsewhere.

## Ask the Colleagues, Not Only the Manager

Managers describe the role they intend. Colleagues describe the role that exists. Before accepting a first data job at any employer, ask to speak with someone who is not your future manager — ideally someone in an adjacent function, such as an analyst, an engineer, a planner or a quality specialist who would use your output.

Three questions produce most of the useful information. What happens to the analyses this organisation already makes — are they used or filed? How long does it take to get access to a new data source? What did the last person in a similar role spend their time on?

The answers reveal the gap between ambition and practice more reliably than any interview with a hiring manager. An organisation where a planner says "we asked for a forecast last year and are still waiting for data access" is telling you something a job description never will.

If an employer will not arrange such a conversation before you decide, that is itself informative — particularly for a role where you would be the first or only data person and dependent on goodwill across departments.

## A Note on Salary and Conditions

Technology employers often pay more in base salary, but the comparison deserves care. As the EU Pay Transparency Directive is implemented, employers must provide pay information before the interview, which makes it easier to compare like with like. Look at the whole package: holiday allowance, pension contribution, travel reimbursement, training budget, working hours and the commute itself. A role twenty minutes away at a slightly lower salary can leave you better off in both money and time than one an hour away.

Dutch probation periods are short and legally capped, which cuts both ways: employers take more chances on junior candidates, and you have a defined window to conclude that a role is not what was described.

## Real example

### A graduate in Zwolle who took the "boring" job

A data science graduate in Zwolle receives one offer: a junior analyst role at a regional insurer, twenty-five minutes from home. She has been rejected by four Randstad technology companies and is tempted to keep waiting.

Before deciding, she asks the six questions. The answers are mixed: no other data scientist, but a senior actuary who will review her work, a specific first project on claims handling times, a named sponsor in operations, a modest cloud budget and agreement to fund an external mentor for a year.

She takes it. In the first year she builds one model that runs weekly and is used by a planning team, learns more about insurance than any course could teach, and documents everything in a repository the company had never had. In her second year she leads a small project with two colleagues.

When she eventually moves, it is to an insurtech scale-up that specifically wanted someone who understood claims processes. The "boring" first job was the reason she was hired.

## Key Takeaways

- Technology employers usually teach engineering discipline; non-tech employers usually teach domain knowledge, breadth and visible impact.
- Much of the Dutch AI and data demand sits outside technology companies, often within commuting distance of where candidates already live.
- The deciding factors are review of your work, available data, a concrete first problem, an engaged sponsor, budget and prior experience in the organisation.
- If you take a non-tech role, enforce version control, get one thing into production, record impact and build an external network.
- Compare total conditions including commute, not only base salary, and remember that probation periods are short in both directions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: graduate with one offer) Is a junior AI role at a non-tech company a bad start?
Not inherently — it usually offers broader responsibility, domain knowledge and visible impact. The risk is isolation and weak engineering practices, which you should check before accepting.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about their CV) Will a non-tech employer hurt my next application?
Only if you cannot explain what you did. Evidence of production work, documented code and measurable impact matters more to hiring managers than a recognisable logo.

### (Scenario: candidate offered a first data hire role) What is the biggest risk of being the first data person?
Having nobody to review your work and no sponsor to use the results. Ask who will review your code and which decision your output will support.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing salaries) Should I take a lower salary closer to home?
Compare total conditions: holiday allowance, pension, travel reimbursement, training budget and weekly commuting time, which is often worth more than a modest salary difference.

### (Scenario: candidate in a non-tech role) How do I keep my technical skills sharp outside a tech company?
Use version control and documentation from day one, get at least one model into production, fund or request an external mentor and stay active in communities.
