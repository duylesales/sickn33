A vacancy asks for a PhD or equivalent experience, five years of production machine learning, deep learning at scale, cloud infrastructure, MLOps, strong statistics, business stakeholder management, and preferably Dutch. You have most of it, not all. The question is whether applying is a reasonable use of an evening or an exercise in optimism. The honest answer is that it depends on which parts you are missing — and that the list in front of you is usually not what it appears to be.

## Why AI Vacancies Are Written This Way

Most AI job descriptions are wish lists assembled from several sources: a hiring manager describing the ideal colleague, an HR template, a previous vacancy for a different role and sometimes an existing employee's profile. Nobody reviews the final list and asks whether a single human being possesses all of it.

Three forces make this worse in AI hiring specifically. The field moves fast, so employers list every technology they have heard of. Titles are inconsistent, so employers overspecify to compensate. And many organisations are hiring their first or second data professional, so they describe a team's worth of skills in one vacancy.

Experienced hiring managers know this. Many will tell you privately that they expect the best candidate to meet perhaps two thirds of the list.

## Sorting the List Into Four Categories

Before deciding, re-read the vacancy and sort every requirement into one of four buckets.

**Hard gates.** Requirements that cannot be waived: a legally required qualification, the right to work in the Netherlands or eligibility for sponsorship, a security clearance for defence-related work, or a language level genuinely needed to do the job.

**Core skills.** The two or three abilities that the role's daily work depends on. For a production ML role, this might be software engineering and deployment experience; for an analytics role, SQL and statistical reasoning; for a research role, methodological depth.

**Context skills.** Domain knowledge, specific tools or industry familiarity. These are learnable within months and are frequently listed as requirements when they are really preferences.

**Decoration.** Everything added to make the role sound ambitious: a long list of frameworks, buzzwords, "experience with LLMs", certifications nobody checks.

If you meet the hard gates and the core skills, missing context skills and decoration is normal. If you are missing a core skill, the calculation changes.

## A Practical Decision Rule

| Your situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Meets hard gates and all core skills | Apply, even if you match half the full list |
| Meets hard gates, missing one core skill but have a close equivalent | Apply and address it explicitly |
| Meets hard gates, missing two or more core skills | Apply only if the role is junior or the company is clearly building |
| Fails a hard gate | Do not apply; find a comparable role without that gate |
| Cannot tell which skills are core | Ask — a short message to the contact person is legitimate |

## Why the "60%" Framing Misleads

The often-quoted idea that some groups apply only when they meet nearly all requirements while others apply at around 60% is widely repeated, and the underlying research is less precise than the number suggests. What matters is not a percentage but which requirements you miss. Meeting 80% of a list while missing the single skill the job actually needs is worse than meeting 55% while having all three core abilities.

Counting bullets is the wrong method. Identifying the two or three things the role cannot function without is the right one.

## How to Address Gaps in Your Application

If you apply while missing something, say so in a way that shows judgement rather than hiding it.

**Name the closest equivalent.** "I have not worked with Kubernetes specifically, but I have deployed and monitored models in production on a managed cloud platform, including rollback and alerting."

**Show speed of learning with evidence.** Point to a time you picked up a comparable tool quickly, with a concrete result.

**Do not apologise.** One clear sentence is enough; an application full of caveats reads as a lack of confidence.

**Never claim what you cannot demonstrate.** In AI roles, technical interviews surface exaggerations quickly, and recovering from that is harder than admitting a gap.

## What Employers Do When Nobody Meets the List

It helps to know what happens on the other side when a vacancy attracts no perfect candidate — which, for AI roles, is most of the time.

Hiring managers usually respond in one of four ways. They lower the requirement that turns out to be least essential, often the specific technology stack. They split the role, hiring a stronger engineer and pairing them with an existing analyst, or the reverse. They hire someone with a strong foundation and budget for training, especially where the domain knowledge is the harder part to find. Or they leave the vacancy open for months and quietly accept that the work is not getting done.

The first three outcomes all favour candidates who applied despite gaps. The fourth is the one that produces vacancies sitting online with very specific wording for half a year.

This is also why applying with a named gap and a credible plan works better than applying silently and hoping nobody notices. A hiring manager weighing whether to relax a requirement needs a concrete reason to believe you will close it. "I have not used that framework, but I built the equivalent with another one and can show the code" gives them that reason. Silence forces them to assume the worst, and a list of unproven claims forces them to test everything.

## Signals That the Employer Is Flexible

Some vacancies tell you, if you read carefully.

**Separate "must have" and "nice to have" sections.** A genuine attempt to distinguish the two suggests a thought-through role.

**A realistic number of requirements.** Five to eight bullets indicates the employer has prioritised; twenty indicates a wish list.

**Language about growth.** "You will learn", "we support development", a mentoring or buddy structure.

**Salary range published.** Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, employers must provide pay information before the interview; a published range often signals an employer that has organised its hiring properly.

**An invitation to ask questions,** with a named contact person rather than a generic inbox.

## Signals That the Bar Is Genuinely High

Equally, some vacancies mean what they say. A role that is clearly the organisation's technical lead, a research position tied to a specific methodology, or a regulated setting requiring documented experience with high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act may have little flexibility. So may a vacancy that has been open a long time with very specific wording — that combination often means the employer is waiting for exactly one profile.

## Requirements That Are Really About Something Else

Several common requirements are proxies. Reading what they stand for tells you whether you meet the underlying need.

**"Five years of experience"** usually means independence: can you own a project without supervision and make sensible decisions alone? Four years of directly relevant ownership frequently satisfies it.

**"PhD or equivalent"** usually means research capability: can you read a paper, judge whether a method fits and evaluate honestly? Equivalent experience often exists outside academia.

**"Fluent Dutch"** varies. For roles with daily contact with operational staff, customers or public bodies it is genuine. For an English-speaking team it is sometimes copied from a template, and asking is legitimate.

**"Experience with our exact stack"** usually means: will you be productive quickly? Demonstrating the same concepts in a comparable tool normally answers it.

**"Strong communication skills"** usually means: can you explain a model's limitations to someone who will act on it? Evidence from any field counts.

Reading proxies correctly does not mean ignoring requirements. It means matching your evidence to what the employer is actually worried about.

## What You Risk and What You Do Not

Applying costs an evening. A rejection costs nothing beyond that, and Dutch recruitment practice — including the NVP Recruitment Code followed by many employers — expects candidates to be informed of the outcome and to receive reasons on request.

What you should not do is apply indiscriminately to compensate for uncertainty. Twenty rushed applications produce fewer interviews than five considered ones, and they consume the time that the considered ones require. Selectivity is not about modesty; it is about where your evenings go.

## Seniority Labels Are Even Less Reliable Than Skill Lists

Alongside the requirements list sits another unreliable signal: the seniority label. In Dutch AI vacancies, "junior", "medior" and "senior" are used inconsistently, and the same title can describe very different expectations at two employers in the same city.

At a young company with a small data team, "senior" may mean three years of experience and a willingness to work independently. At a bank or a research institute, the same word may require a decade, formal model governance experience and a track record of leading others. Meanwhile, some organisations avoid the labels altogether and describe a role only by tasks.

The practical method is the same as for skills: read the described work, not the label. Ask yourself whether you have done the things the role's day-to-day involves — owning a project end to end, deciding on approach, being accountable when something breaks in production, mentoring someone. If yes, a "senior" label is not a barrier even with fewer years than stated. If no, a "senior" title with an easy-looking list may still be a stretch.

Years of experience requirements deserve the same scepticism. They are usually a rough proxy for independence, not a hard gate. A candidate with four years of directly relevant production experience is often a better fit for a "five years required" role than someone with eight years in an adjacent field — and most hiring managers know this.

## Real example

### A software engineer in Enschede who applied anyway

A backend engineer in Enschede with six years of experience finds an ML engineering vacancy at a regional manufacturer. The list includes production machine learning experience he does not have, deep learning frameworks he has only used in courses, cloud infrastructure he knows well and software engineering practices he knows very well.

He sorts the list. Hard gates: none he fails. Core skills for this role, judging by the description of daily work — deploying and maintaining models, data pipelines, working with production systems — are mostly things he does. The deep learning experience appears in the "nice to have" section.

He applies, writing two sentences about the gap: the models he has deployed were built by others, and he has been closing that gap through a specific project he links to. He gets an interview, where the hiring manager explains that the team already has a data scientist and needs someone who can make models run reliably — which is exactly his profile. He is hired. The list had described an ideal; the job needed something narrower.

## Key Takeaways

- Most AI vacancies are wish lists assembled from several sources, not specifications a single person meets.
- Sort requirements into hard gates, core skills, context skills and decoration before deciding.
- Apply when you meet the hard gates and core skills, even if your overall match looks low.
- Counting the percentage of bullets you meet is the wrong method; identifying the two or three essentials is the right one.
- Address gaps explicitly with the closest equivalent and evidence, and never overstate what you can demonstrate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate hesitating over a long requirements list) Should I apply if I meet only part of the requirements?
Yes, if you meet the hard gates and the two or three core skills the daily work depends on. Missing context skills and buzzwords is normal.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure what counts as essential) How do I know which requirements are genuinely required?
Read the description of daily work rather than the bullet list, look for a separate must-have section, and ask the contact person if it is unclear.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about honesty) Should I mention skills I am missing in my application?
Yes, briefly — name the closest equivalent and show evidence of learning quickly. Do not claim experience you cannot demonstrate in an interview.

### (Scenario: candidate seeing very long vacancies) Why do AI vacancies list so many requirements?
They are often assembled from multiple sources, inflated by fast-moving technology and inconsistent job titles, and sometimes describe a whole team's skills.

### (Scenario: candidate applying widely) Is it better to apply to many roles or a few?
A few considered applications generally produce more interviews than many rushed ones, because tailoring is what distinguishes applications in high-volume processes.
