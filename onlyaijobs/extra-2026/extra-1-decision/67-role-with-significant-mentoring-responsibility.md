The vacancy is for a senior role, and somewhere in the middle of the description is a sentence about coaching junior colleagues, guiding trainees or supporting career changers into the team. In the interview it becomes clearer: two juniors, an intern each semester, and an expectation that you will bring them up to a level where they can work independently. Some candidates read this as the organisation getting two jobs for one salary. Others read it as the natural next step in a senior career. Both readings are sometimes right, and which one applies depends on things you can find out before accepting.

## Why These Roles Have Multiplied

The Dutch market for experienced AI and data people has been tight for years, and employers have drawn the obvious conclusion: if senior candidates are scarce and expensive, grow them.

That has produced traineeship programmes, structured junior tracks, partnerships with hogescholen and universities for internships and graduation projects, and a steady flow of career changers arriving from adjacent fields. All of them need someone experienced to learn from, and that person is increasingly written into the senior vacancy rather than assumed.

The consequence for you is that mentoring has moved from something you did informally to something you are hired and evaluated for — at least in principle.

## What the Role Actually Involves

**Reviewing work in detail,** which takes far longer than reviewing a peer's, because the useful review explains why rather than what.

**Pairing and unblocking.** Interruptions, by design. A junior who is stuck for two days when ten minutes would have helped is a failure of availability, not of ability.

**Designing work that is learnable.** Choosing tasks that stretch without drowning, which is a genuine skill and one nobody teaches.

**Explaining the domain,** repeatedly, because context is the thing juniors lack most and the thing that is least written down.

**Giving feedback that lands,** including the uncomfortable kind, and in a working culture where directness is the norm but tact still matters.

**Occasionally, formal responsibilities:** input into reviews, sign-off on internship assessments, contributions to development plans.

## What You Gain

**Leadership experience without management.** You learn whether you enjoy developing people before taking on responsibility for their employment, which is a much safer order.

**Sharper fundamentals.** Explaining why a validation design is wrong forces a clarity that solitary work never demands.

**Visibility and reputation.** In most organisations, the person who develops others is noticed, and juniors who become strong colleagues remember who taught them.

**A route to lead roles.** Where a technical lead track exists, mentoring is usually the qualifying experience.

**Durable professional relationships.** In a market this small, people you trained turn up again — sometimes as the hiring manager.

## What It Costs

**Throughput.** Your personal output drops, and in organisations that measure delivery without accounting for coaching, this is experienced as underperformance.

**Fragmentation.** Mentoring is interrupt-driven and deep technical work is not. Weeks fill with short, valuable conversations and leave little continuous time.

**Emotional labour.** Some of it is genuinely draining, particularly with people who are struggling, anxious or in the wrong role.

**Your own development, if nobody is above you.** Being the most experienced person in a team of juniors means you learn from nobody, which is a serious risk over several years.

## The Question Almost Nobody Asks

Is the mentoring recognised, in the two ways that matter: time and evaluation?

**Time.** Is there an explicit allocation — a proportion of the week, a reduced delivery expectation, a formal role in a programme — or is it expected on top of a full workload? The second is the most common arrangement and the reason these roles sour.

**Evaluation.** Does developing people count in performance and promotion decisions, or do reviews measure only what you personally delivered? Ask how the last person who invested heavily in juniors was assessed. If nobody can answer, mentoring is invisible work here.

An employer who has thought about both has designed the role. One who has thought about neither is hoping.

## Structured Versus Improvised

**Structured looks like:** a defined programme with a curriculum or milestones, agreed time, a second mentor so you are not the single point of failure, a plan for what juniors do after the programme, and someone accountable for the programme's success other than you.

**Improvised looks like:** "we've hired two juniors and you'd be the senior", no allocation, no plan, no end state, and an assumption that experience automatically transfers by proximity.

Improvised arrangements are not always bad — many work through goodwill — but they place the entire risk on you, and they collapse the moment delivery pressure arrives.

## The Hidden Risk: Becoming the Support Function

The failure mode to watch for is drifting into being the team's permanent helpdesk. It happens gradually: you are the person who knows things, so every question comes to you, and eventually your calendar is other people's problems.

Three habits prevent it. Hold defined availability rather than being permanently interruptible, so questions batch naturally. Answer by teaching how to find the answer, not by providing it, which is slower once and faster forever. And write things down, because the third time you explain something it belongs in documentation rather than in a conversation.

## Questions to Ask Before Accepting

- **How many people would I be responsible for, at what level, and for how long?**
- **How much time is allocated, and what delivery is expected alongside?**
- **Does this count in performance and promotion decisions here?**
- **Is there a programme, or would I be designing it?**
- **Who else mentors, so that I am not the only source?**
- **Who would I learn from?** The most important question if you would be the most senior person.
- **What happened to the last cohort — where are they now?**
- **Is there a technical lead track this leads towards?**

## When to Accept

- You genuinely enjoy explaining things, and you have evidence of that rather than an assumption.
- Time is allocated and the expectation on your own delivery is adjusted.
- There is someone above you technically, or an external route to keep learning.
- Mentoring counts formally in how you are evaluated.
- You want a lead role eventually and need the experience.
- The juniors are properly hired into real roles rather than parked in the team.

## When to Decline

- It is expected on top of a full workload.
- You would be the only senior person, with nobody to learn from.
- The organisation measures individuals purely on delivery.
- You dislike interruption and know it.
- The juniors were hired to reduce salary costs rather than to be developed.
- You are not yet senior enough yourself; mentoring before you have done the work well is unfair to both sides.

## How to Do It Well

Set availability deliberately: two or three fixed windows a week, plus a channel for genuine blockers. Review work in person for the first months, because written comments teach far less than a fifteen-minute conversation.

Give real work with real consequences, scoped so that failure is survivable. Juniors learn from owning something that matters, not from tasks designed to be safe.

Write the things you repeat. A short document on how the team validates models, or how the domain works, saves you dozens of conversations and outlives you.

Protect one deep block a week for your own technical work, and defend it. And find your own source of learning outside the team — a peer group, an external reviewer, a community — because the biggest risk in a mentoring-heavy role is that everyone develops except you.

## Mentoring Career Changers Is a Different Job

A large share of people entering Dutch data teams are not recent graduates. They are former teachers, nurses, logistics planners, accountants and researchers who retrained, and mentoring them differs enough from mentoring juniors that it is worth knowing before you accept.

**The gaps are narrower and deeper.** Career changers often have strong domain understanding, professional maturity and stakeholder skills that a graduate lacks entirely. What they are missing tends to be specific and technical: engineering practice, statistical intuition, or comfort with ambiguity in code.

**Confidence is uneven.** Someone who led projects in their previous field can find it disorienting to be a beginner again, and the discomfort shows up as either over-caution or reluctance to ask basic questions. Both are addressed by normalising not knowing things.

**They have transferable judgement.** Their instinct about whether a result is plausible is frequently better than a junior's, because they have worked in the world the data describes. Treating that as an asset accelerates everything.

**Pace expectations need managing.** Retraining programmes are intense and short, and people arrive expecting to be productive immediately. Being explicit that the first six months are for learning prevents a lot of unnecessary anxiety.

**The teaching is more targeted.** With a graduate you build broadly; with a career changer you often fill three specific gaps well and then step back.

If the role involves a retraining or traineeship intake, ask what the previous cohort looked like, since mentoring five career changers and mentoring five graduates are genuinely different assignments.

## Real example

### A senior engineer in Zwolle who asked how it would be measured

A senior data scientist is offered a role at an insurer with two juniors and a graduation intern attached. The salary is good and the work sounds solid.

She asks two questions. How much time is allocated for coaching, and how is it evaluated? The answers are vague: "as much as you need" and "we look at the team's results".

She pushes gently, and the manager, to his credit, goes away and comes back with something concrete: a fifth of her time formally allocated, a reduced individual delivery expectation for the first year, and coaching written into her objectives.

The year is harder than she expected. One junior progresses quickly; the other struggles and eventually moves to a different role after a difficult conversation she has to lead. Her own output is visibly lower than the previous year.

At her review, the allocation is what protects her: the reduced delivery expectation was agreed in writing, and the coaching is assessed as a result rather than as an absence.

Two years later she leads the team. Her view is that the job itself was rewarding and precarious in equal measure, and that the fifteen minutes she spent asking how it would be measured was the most valuable part of the negotiation.

## Key Takeaways

- Mentoring-heavy senior roles have multiplied because the Dutch market for experienced AI people is tight and employers are growing their own.
- The gains are leadership experience without management responsibility, sharper fundamentals, visibility and a route to lead roles.
- The costs are reduced throughput, a fragmented week, emotional labour and stalled development if nobody above you exists.
- Ask the two questions almost nobody asks: is time allocated, and does mentoring count in performance and promotion decisions?
- Protect yourself by setting fixed availability, teaching how to find answers, documenting what you repeat and keeping one deep block a week.

## Where to Start

If you want a role with real coaching content, look for employers running structured junior or traineeship programmes rather than those with juniors and no plan — and see which of them are within reach of home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: senior candidate offered a coaching role) Is mentoring good for my career?
Usually yes. It is the standard qualifying experience for lead roles and it sharpens your own fundamentals, provided the time is allocated and the work is recognised.

### (Scenario: candidate assessing the offer) What should I ask before accepting?
Whether time is explicitly allocated, whether coaching counts in performance and promotion decisions, who else mentors and who you would learn from.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about output) Will my own delivery suffer?
Yes, measurably. That is only a problem if the expectation is not adjusted, which is why the allocation should be agreed in writing.

### (Scenario: candidate who would be the most senior) What is the biggest risk?
Having nobody to learn from. If you would be the most experienced person in the team, arrange external review or a peer group before accepting.

### (Scenario: candidate avoiding the helpdesk trap) How do I keep it manageable?
Fixed availability windows rather than constant interruption, teaching people how to find answers, documenting what you repeat and protecting one deep block a week.
