Brussels is where much of Europe's AI rulebook is written, negotiated and, increasingly, enforced. The EU AI Act, adopted as Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, created obligations for providers and deployers of AI systems and new governance structures, including the European AI Office within the European Commission. Around these institutions, a whole ecosystem of consultancies, law firms, think tanks, industry associations and civil society organisations works on AI policy. For machine learning engineers and data scientists, AI policy jobs in Brussels offer a way to apply technical expertise to questions that shape how AI is built and used across the continent. This guide explains which organisations hire, what technical experts contribute, how recruitment works and what the transition from engineering to policy really involves.

## Why Technical Expertise Matters in AI Policy

For many years, technology policy in Brussels was dominated by lawyers, economists and political scientists. As AI regulation moves from drafting to implementation, technical expertise has become essential. Regulators and companies need people who can answer concrete questions: how are general-purpose AI models trained and evaluated, what does robustness testing look like in practice, how can bias be measured, what documentation is realistic, which risks can technical mitigations address and which cannot?

Engineers who can bridge the gap between technical reality and legal text are rare and valued. They help write guidance that is feasible to implement, evaluate whether companies meet their obligations and advise organisations on how to comply without abandoning useful innovation.

## Who Hires for AI Policy Jobs in Brussels

**The European Commission and the AI Office.** The AI Office, part of the Commission's Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology (DG CONNECT), plays a central role in implementing the AI Act, particularly for general-purpose AI models, and in coordinating with national authorities. It has recruited technology specialists alongside policy officers and legal experts. Other Commission services deal with data, research funding, competition and digital markets.

**Other EU institutions and bodies.** The European Parliament's committees and political groups need advisers who understand technology. The Council, Member States' permanent representations and the European Data Protection Supervisor also work on AI-related files.

**Standardisation.** The AI Act relies on harmonised standards to specify technical requirements. European standardisation bodies, notably through the joint CEN-CENELEC technical committee on artificial intelligence (JTC 21), develop these standards with experts from industry, academia and public bodies. Participation is often through national standardisation bodies.

**Consultancies and law firms.** Many consultancies and law firms have Brussels practices advising clients on AI regulation, compliance programmes and public affairs. Technical experts help translate regulatory requirements into engineering processes.

**Industry associations and companies.** Technology companies and industry associations maintain Brussels offices to follow and influence policy. They hire people who understand both the technology and the legislative process.

**Think tanks and civil society.** Research institutes, digital rights organisations and consumer groups analyse AI policy and advocate for particular approaches. They value technical analysis to support their positions.

**Universities.** The Vrije Universiteit Brussel, with a long-standing AI Lab, and the Université libre de Bruxelles conduct research on AI and on law, ethics and governance of technology, and collaborate with policy organisations.

## How Recruitment Works in the EU Institutions

Recruitment in the EU institutions follows specific procedures that differ from private employers.

**Permanent officials** are usually recruited through competitions organised by the European Personnel Selection Office (EPSO). Competitions can be general or specialised, and successful candidates are placed on reserve lists from which institutions recruit.

**Temporary and contract agents** are recruited for specific needs, often faster than through competitions. Specialised technical profiles, including in AI, have been recruited through such channels.

**Seconded national experts** are staff from national administrations who work in EU institutions for a limited period, paid partly by their home administration.

**Traineeships** — such as the Commission's paid traineeship programme — offer five-month placements for recent graduates and are a common entry point.

EU recruitment generally requires citizenship of an EU Member State for most positions, knowledge of at least two EU languages and suitable qualifications. Selection processes can take many months, so plan accordingly and keep an eye on vacancy announcements from the institutions and the AI Office.

## Skills That Matter

- **Technical depth:** hands-on experience with ML models, evaluation, data pipelines and deployment, ideally including large language models.
- **Understanding of legislation:** the AI Act's risk-based structure, obligations for high-risk systems and general-purpose AI models, and its interaction with GDPR, the Digital Services Act and product safety law.
- **Writing:** clear, precise writing for non-technical readers — briefs, position papers, guidance.
- **Stakeholder communication:** explaining technical issues to lawyers, politicians and the public without oversimplifying.
- **Patience with process:** policy moves slowly and involves compromise.
- **Languages:** English dominates the Brussels policy world, but French is widely used, and other EU languages are assets.

## Living in Brussels

Brussels is a multilingual, international city. French and Dutch are its official languages, and English is common in the EU quarter and international organisations. The city is well connected by train to Paris, Amsterdam, London and Cologne. Housing is generally more affordable than in Paris or Amsterdam, although popular neighbourhoods near the EU institutions are in demand. Belgium has high personal income taxes; EU officials are subject to a separate EU tax regime, and Belgium has a special tax regime for certain inbound employees and researchers, which was reformed in recent years. Check which rules apply to your situation.

## What the Work Looks Like Day to Day

AI policy work differs substantially from engineering work, and it helps to understand the rhythm before making the move.

**Reading and writing.** A large share of the day is spent reading legislation, consultation responses, research papers and stakeholder submissions, and writing briefing notes, position papers, guidance drafts and correspondence. Clarity and precision in writing are essential.

**Meetings and negotiation.** Policy is made through discussion: internal coordination meetings, stakeholder consultations, working groups, expert panels and negotiation between institutions. Technical experts often explain what is feasible and what the consequences of different options would be.

**Technical analysis.** In technical roles, you may evaluate model documentation, design evaluation methodologies, analyse incident reports, review standards drafts or run experiments to test claims. This is where engineering skills are most directly applied.

**Long time horizons.** Legislative files and guidance can take months or years. The satisfaction comes from shaping frameworks that affect many organisations, rather than shipping a feature next week.

For some engineers, this rhythm is energising; for others, it is frustrating. Talking to people in similar roles before switching is the best way to find out which applies to you.

## Understanding the AI Act as a Technical Expert

Anyone pursuing AI policy jobs in Brussels should know the structure of the AI Act well. In broad terms, it follows a risk-based approach. Certain AI practices considered unacceptable are prohibited. AI systems classified as high-risk — for example in certain areas of employment, education, access to essential services, law enforcement and as safety components of regulated products — are subject to requirements on risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity. Transparency obligations apply to certain other systems, such as those interacting with people or generating synthetic content. Providers of general-purpose AI models have specific obligations, with additional ones for models considered to pose systemic risk. The obligations apply in stages over several years after entry into force.

For technical experts, the most interesting questions often lie in implementation: how to evaluate robustness in practice, what adequate data governance means for a specific use case, how to document training data for large models, how to measure and mitigate bias, and how to design human oversight that works. Codes of practice, guidelines and harmonised standards translate the legal requirements into concrete practice, and technical experts are needed to draft and review them.

## Building a Bridge From Engineering

Moving from engineering to policy rarely happens in one step. Practical ways to build the bridge include:

1. **Take on compliance work in your current role.** Volunteer for AI Act readiness, model documentation, risk assessments or responsible AI initiatives.
2. **Write publicly.** Short articles explaining technical aspects of AI regulation for non-technical readers demonstrate exactly the skill policy employers need.
3. **Engage with consultations.** The EU and national authorities regularly run public consultations; responding thoughtfully builds knowledge and visibility.
4. **Join standardisation work.** National standardisation bodies accept experts for AI committees, often through their employers.
5. **Take courses in law and governance.** Short programmes on EU law, data protection or AI governance give you vocabulary and frameworks.
6. **Network in Brussels.** Policy events, panels and conferences are frequent and often open; attending builds relationships.

## Pay and Career Progression

Pay in AI policy roles varies widely. EU institution salaries follow published grades and include allowances, and the EU tax regime applies to officials and other staff. Consultancies and law firms can pay well, especially at senior levels, while think tanks and NGOs typically pay less but offer mission-driven work. Compared with senior engineering roles at large technology companies, many policy roles pay less; the trade-off is influence, variety and the opportunity to shape how AI is governed. Career progression in policy depends heavily on reputation and network, and many professionals move between institutions, industry and civil society over their careers.

## Beyond Brussels: National Authorities

The AI Act is enforced not only in Brussels. Each Member State designates national competent authorities, including market surveillance authorities, which supervise AI systems on their markets. These authorities also need technical experts. If moving to Brussels is not an option, national authorities in your own country — or data protection authorities, which already handle many AI-related questions under GDPR — can offer similar work closer to home. In the Netherlands, for example, supervisors such as the Dutch Data Protection Authority have taken on coordinating roles in algorithm supervision. Experience at a national authority is also valuable if you later apply to EU-level roles.

## Real example

### An ML engineer moving into AI governance

An ML engineer with six years of experience building NLP models at a Dutch company is increasingly interested in how the AI Act will affect her work. She volunteers for her company's internal AI Act readiness project, mapping which systems might be high-risk and drafting documentation templates.

Encouraged by this experience, she follows AI Office and Commission vacancy announcements and applies to consultancies with Brussels AI governance practices. A consultancy hires her as a technical AI governance consultant. Her first projects involve helping clients inventory their AI systems, assess risk categories and design evaluation processes. She finds that her engineering background makes her credible with clients' technical teams, while she learns legal concepts from colleagues. After two years, she applies for a technical role in an EU body, drawing on both engineering and governance experience.
