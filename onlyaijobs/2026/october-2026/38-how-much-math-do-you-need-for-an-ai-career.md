"Do I need to be good at math to work in AI?" is a question that rarely gets a clear answer, because the answer differs completely by type of role — and that nuance often gets lost in generic advice.

## What Applied Work Actually Requires

For most applied data science and ML engineering roles, a working understanding of linear algebra, probability and statistics is enough — enough to understand why a model does what it does, without having to formulate new mathematical proofs yourself. Libraries and frameworks do most of the computation; your job is to interpret and apply it.

## What Research Roles Actually Require

Research positions — developing new architectures or methods — require a deeper, active level of mathematical reasoning, comparable to what a master's in mathematics or physics requires. This is a genuinely higher level, not just "more of the same."

## Why These Two Are Often Confused

Advice about "math needed for AI" often combines both categories into a single answer, leading to unnecessary discouragement among people who want to do applied work, or underestimation among people considering research.

## A Deeper Look at What "Working Understanding" Means Concretely

For applied work, a working understanding of linear algebra means, for example, being able to explain why a matrix multiplication happens in a neural network, without necessarily being able to reproduce the proofs behind matrix decompositions. It means recognising when a model overfits from a learning curve, without being able to derive the full statistical theory of generalisation. That is a real, valuable level of knowledge — just a different level from what a research role requires.

## Comparison Table: Applied Level vs. Research Level, by Topic

| Topic | Applied level required | Research level required |
|---|---|---|
| Linear algebra | Understanding why operations happen | Deriving new mathematical properties yourself |
| Probability and statistics | Choosing and interpreting metrics correctly | Developing new statistical methods |
| Optimisation | Knowing why a model doesn't converge | Designing new optimisation algorithms |

## Why Testing Your Own Level Works Better Than a Generic Topic List

Instead of ticking off a list of mathematical topics, a more reliable test is to try to explain a recent AI concept relevant to you to someone without a technical background, without using mathematical notation. If you can, you probably have the level applied work requires. If you notice you can only reproduce formulas without explaining the intuition, that's a signal to invest more deeply — whichever path you eventually choose.

## Which Math You Actually Use in Which Role

In an applied data analyst role, you mainly use descriptive statistics, probability distributions and reasoning about uncertainty in conclusions. Regression and classification come up, but the mathematical depth you need stays limited to understanding what a method does and when its assumptions are violated.

In a machine learning engineering role, an understanding of optimisation is added: why a model does or doesn't converge, what learning rate means, how regularisation works. Again, it's about working understanding rather than derivation skills; you need to diagnose why training goes wrong, not construct a new optimisation algorithm.

In specialised roles around signal processing, such as those discussed in the Twente regional analysis, the emphasis shifts to frequency analysis and linear systems theory — a different mathematical area from what generic machine learning courses focus on.

In research roles, the level is qualitatively different: you need to follow and construct proofs and read mathematical literature independently. This is the level a mathematics or physics degree trains, and self-study rarely reaches.

## Named Resources That Match Each Level

For applied roles and for filling gaps efficiently, a few well-known resources stand out:

- **3Blue1Brown's "Essence of Linear Algebra."** A visual video series that builds geometric intuition for vectors, matrices and transformations — ideal for the "understand why" level.
- **Khan Academy.** Free courses on probability, statistics and calculus that let you refresh specific concepts without enrolling in a full programme.
- **"An Introduction to Statistical Learning."** By James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani, this book explains core machine learning methods with limited mathematical prerequisites and is freely available from the authors.
- **"Mathematics for Machine Learning."** By Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, published by Cambridge University Press, this book covers linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimisation specifically as needed for machine learning, and is also freely available from the authors — a good bridge towards a more rigorous level.

For research ambitions, a formal programme is usually the realistic route. Dutch universities offer pre-master programmes for students whose previous education lacks required courses, and the **Open Universiteit** offers flexible mathematics and computer science courses for working adults.

## The Dutch Education Context

In Dutch secondary education, **Mathematics B (wiskunde B)**, which focuses on functions, calculus and geometry, is a common admission requirement for bachelor's programmes in artificial intelligence, computer science, data science and econometrics at research universities. Students without it can often take a deficiency course or exam before admission. At universities of applied sciences, requirements vary by programme. For students choosing a study path, this means the question "how much math do I need" also has a practical admissions dimension — checking programme requirements early prevents surprises.

## How to Fill a Gap in Your Mathematical Foundation Efficiently

The most common mistake is following an extensive, theoretical course that starts at the foundations and only reaches the applicable part after months. A more efficient approach starts from the concrete problem you ran into and works back to the underlying mathematics. If you don't understand why a model overfits, learn exactly the statistical concepts involved — the bias-variance trade-off, generalisation, validation strategies — instead of working through a full statistics course, most of which isn't relevant to your situation.

This approach has two practical advantages: motivation, because learning what you need now is much easier to sustain, and retention, because knowledge applied directly to a real problem sticks much better. What is useful to do systematically is learning to read notation. Much practically useful literature is inaccessible to people who can't decipher mathematical notation, while the underlying ideas are often quite understandable.

## Why the Question Itself Is Sometimes the Wrong Question

What employers in the Dutch market most often miss isn't mathematical depth but the ability to translate a vague business problem into a solvable question, to work with messy data, and to explain results to people without a technical background. These skills correlate only weakly with mathematical aptitude. That doesn't mean mathematics doesn't matter — without a working understanding of uncertainty and evaluation you'll systematically make wrong choices. But a candidate who focuses all their energy on raising their mathematical level while neglecting communication and problem framing is probably optimising in the wrong direction.

## What This Means for People Who Doubt Whether They're Suited

Many people asking this question are really asking whether they're smart enough for the field. The honest answer is that the mathematical requirements for the vast majority of applied work are within reach of anyone willing to invest time consistently — it's a matter of exposure and practice, not innate talent. The process is uncomfortable: working with concepts you don't yet fully understand, and accepting that understanding comes gradually. Anyone who interprets that discomfort as proof of unsuitability often stops exactly at the point where understanding would have formed.

## How Employers Test This in Practice

For applied roles, employers rarely test mathematical skill directly; there are no derivations or proofs. What does happen is that an interviewer sketches a scenario and assesses whether you ask the right questions: which metric fits here, what happens if the classes are imbalanced, how do you know whether this difference is significant. For research roles, it's different: questions do require following or constructing a mathematical argument.

## What You Can Do Without a Formal Mathematical Education

Build intuition through application rather than theory: work with real data, build models, and let the questions that arise determine what you learn next. Supplement that with targeted, short deep dives into specific concepts when you notice your understanding falls short. And be honest in applications about what you do and don't master — employers in the applied segment regularly hire people without a formal mathematical background, but value candidates who can pinpoint where their understanding ends far more than those who hide it.

## A Self-Check: Five Questions That Reveal Your Applied Level

Instead of a long topic list, try answering these five questions in plain language. If you can answer most of them clearly, your mathematical foundation is likely sufficient for applied roles.

1. **Why can a model with 95% accuracy be useless?** A good answer mentions class imbalance and the need for metrics such as precision, recall or a cost-weighted measure.
2. **What does a confidence interval around a model's error actually tell you?** A good answer explains uncertainty due to sampling, not the probability that the model is "right."
3. **Why does a model perform well on training data but poorly on new data?** A good answer names overfitting and the bias-variance trade-off, and suggests validation strategies.
4. **What does gradient descent do, and what happens if the learning rate is too high?** A good answer describes iterative minimisation and overshooting or divergence.
5. **Why is correlation in your data not enough to recommend a business action?** A good answer distinguishes correlation from causation and mentions confounding.

If one of these questions stumps you, that points to exactly the concept worth studying next — which is the problem-driven learning approach this article recommends.

## A Four-Week Refresher Plan for Students

| Week | Focus | Suggested resource | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Vectors, matrices, transformations | 3Blue1Brown's linear algebra series | Explain matrix multiplication in a neural network layer in your own words |
| 2 | Probability, distributions, expectation | Khan Academy probability units | Simulate a distribution in Python and compare with theory |
| 3 | Statistics for evaluation | The resampling and evaluation chapters of "An Introduction to Statistical Learning" | Cross-validate a simple model and interpret the variance of the scores |
| 4 | Optimisation basics | The optimisation chapter of "Mathematics for Machine Learning" | Implement gradient descent on a small problem and experiment with learning rates |

Four weeks of focused refreshing won't turn you into a researcher, but it closes the most common gaps that trip up applied candidates in interviews.

## Key Takeaways

- Applied AI roles require a working understanding of linear algebra, probability and statistics; research roles require active mathematical reasoning at master's level.
- The math you use differs by role: statistics for analysts, optimisation for ML engineers, systems theory for signal processing, proofs for research.
- 3Blue1Brown, Khan Academy, "An Introduction to Statistical Learning" and "Mathematics for Machine Learning" match different levels.
- In the Netherlands, Mathematics B is a common admission requirement for AI-related bachelor's programmes; pre-masters and the Open Universiteit help fill gaps later.
- Fill gaps from concrete problems, learn to read notation, and don't neglect problem framing and communication.

## Where to Start

First decide which type of role you actually aim for — applied or research — before judging whether you know "enough math." The bar is structurally different for each.
