In many AI hiring processes, the second interview round is where most time is lost — not because the round itself takes too long, but because it often repeats questions already asked in the first round, without anyone explicitly noticing.

## Why This Overlap Happens

Different interviewers prepare their own questions without knowing what a colleague already asked in the previous round. The result: a candidate answers the same question about a project twice, while other questions about the role remain unanswered.

## What a More Effective Second Round Does Differently

**A short briefing between rounds one and two.** Five minutes in which the first interviewer shares what has been discussed and what is still open prevents most overlap.

**A clear, separate purpose for each round.** If round one tests technical depth and round two tests collaboration and communication, that must be agreed explicitly beforehand — not implicitly assumed.

**Letting the candidate indicate what has already been covered.** Simply asking "has this come up in your previous conversation?" saves time and shows respect for the candidate.

## Why This Delivers More Than Shortening the Process

The problem is rarely that there are too many rounds — it's that rounds overlap without anyone correcting it. A short briefing between rounds solves more than simply cutting a round.

## Why Speed Matters in the Dutch AI Labour Market

The Dutch labour market for ICT and data professionals has been tight for years. **UWV**, the Employee Insurance Agency, publishes a labour market tightness indicator per occupational group and region, and ICT occupations have consistently been classified as tight or very tight in recent years. In practice, that means strong AI candidates are often in several processes at once. A process that loses a week to overlapping rounds or slow scheduling doesn't only waste interviewer time — it increases the chance that the preferred candidate accepts another offer before the final round.

## A Deeper Look at Why Nobody Feels Responsible for Overlap

Overlap between interview rounds rarely arises from carelessness by one specific interviewer — it happens because nobody explicitly has the role of overseeing the whole interview process. Each interviewer prepares their own round with the best intentions, but without a designated process owner, nobody is responsible for coherence between rounds.

## Comparison Table: A Process With and Without a Briefing Between Rounds

| Aspect | Without a briefing between rounds | With a short briefing between rounds |
|---|---|---|
| Chance of repeated questions | High | Low |
| Candidate experience | Can feel frustrating | Feels thoughtful and coordinated |
| Implementation effort | None, but costs time with every candidate | Five minutes per round, once established as a habit |
| Quality of the final decision | Based on possibly overlapping information | Based on genuinely complementary information |

## Designing a Two-Round Process for an AI Role

A clear division of purposes prevents overlap by design:

| Round | Purpose | Typical format | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Round 1** | Technical depth and motivation | Project walkthrough and technical questions, or discussion of a take-home assignment | Hiring manager and a senior technical team member |
| **Round 2** | Collaboration, problem framing and fit with stakeholders | Case discussion with a realistic, ambiguous problem; conversation with a stakeholder from outside the data team | A team member not involved in round 1 and a stakeholder |
| **Final conversation** (optional) | Terms, questions and mutual commitment | Informal conversation about the offer | Hiring manager |

Each round has its own scorecard with criteria that don't repeat those of the other round. The hiring manager — or a named recruiter — is the process owner responsible for the briefing and the timeline.

## The Five-Minute Briefing Template

Between rounds, the process owner shares a short written or verbal briefing with the next interviewers:

1. **What was covered:** topics, projects discussed and questions asked.
2. **What was strong:** evidence that doesn't need retesting.
3. **What remains open:** concerns or areas that couldn't be assessed.
4. **The purpose of this round:** the criteria this round must assess.
5. **Candidate questions so far:** what the candidate asked, so the next interviewers can address it.

Share only job-relevant observations in the briefing, and keep it in the recruitment system rather than in informal chat channels, since interview notes are personal data under the **GDPR**.

## Handling Salary Early, Not in the Final Round

A common cause of late-stage delays is discovering in the last round that salary expectations don't match. Under the **EU Pay Transparency Directive**, employers must give applicants information about the starting pay or pay range before the interview, and may not ask about pay history. Sharing the range in the vacancy or before round one prevents a second or third round from being spent on a candidate who will decline the offer.

## Reference Checks and Timing

Reference checks are often scheduled after the final round, adding days. If you use them, ask for the candidate's consent and references during round two, so checks can run in parallel with the final decision. Inform candidates beforehand that references will be contacted, in line with the **NVP Recruitment Code**, and handle the information under the **Dutch Data Protection Authority**'s guidance on applicant data, including deletion within four weeks after the procedure unless the candidate consents to longer retention.

## Scheduling: The Hidden Delay

Much of the time lost between rounds isn't in the interviews at all, but in finding calendar slots. Practical measures help:

- **Reserve interview slots in advance** for the duration of the vacancy.
- **Schedule round two at the end of round one** if the first impression is positive, instead of waiting for a separate email exchange.
- **Offer video interviews** for rounds where in-person presence adds little, and in-person meetings where meeting the team matters.
- **Communicate the timeline** to candidates at the start: how many rounds, what each involves and when they will hear back.

## A Benchmark: A Two-to-Three-Week Process

For most AI roles, a well-organised process can move from first interview to offer in two to three weeks without cutting corners:

| Day | Step |
|---|---|
| Day 0 | Application reviewed; candidate invited within three working days |
| Day 3–5 | Round 1: technical depth and motivation |
| Same day | Briefing written; decision on round 2 communicated within two working days |
| Day 7–10 | Round 2: collaboration, problem framing and stakeholder conversation |
| Day 10–12 | Debrief with independent scores; references contacted with consent |
| Day 12–15 | Offer conversation, with salary range already known from the vacancy |

Processes that take six weeks or more rarely assess candidates better; they mostly contain waiting time between steps.

## Candidate Communication That Prevents Drop-Out

Short, predictable communication keeps strong candidates engaged:

- **After applying:** "Thanks for applying. We'll let you know within three working days whether we invite you for a first conversation."
- **Before round 1:** "The process has two rounds. Round 1 focuses on your technical experience; round 2 on collaboration and a practical case. We aim to decide within three weeks."
- **After round 1:** "Thank you for today. You'll hear from us within two working days about the next step."
- **After round 2:** "We're completing our evaluation and will contact you by [date]."
- **After a rejection:** A brief, specific reason and an offer of a short feedback conversation where possible.

Candidates who know what to expect are less likely to accept another offer out of uncertainty.

## Common Mistakes in Second Rounds

1. **Repeating the technical interview** because the second interviewer doesn't trust the first.
2. **Adding a senior executive "just to meet"** without a defined purpose or criteria.
3. **Introducing a new assignment** late in the process that wasn't announced at the start.
4. **Discussing salary for the first time** after two rounds.
5. **Letting the debrief slip** for a week because interviewers are unavailable.

Each of these adds days without adding information — and each can be prevented by agreeing the process design before the vacancy opens.

## Remote and Hybrid Interview Rounds

Many AI teams interview partly online, which changes where time is lost:

- **Test the setup in advance.** A shared screen for a case discussion or a code walkthrough should work before the candidate joins; technical delays eat into the time available for assessment.
- **Use the same scorecard online and offline.** Candidates interviewed by video shouldn't be judged on different criteria from those interviewed in person.
- **Reserve in-person time for what benefits from it.** Meeting the team, seeing the workplace and informal conversation are the parts of the process where physical presence adds most; technical rounds often work well online.
- **Mind time zones and travel.** For international candidates, combine rounds on one day where possible, and reimburse travel costs for in-person rounds when you can.
- **Record decisions, not conversations.** Keep written evidence notes rather than recordings, which raise additional privacy questions.

## Why This Is an Easy, Permanent Improvement

Once a short briefing between interview rounds becomes a fixed habit within a team, it no longer costs extra effort per application — it's simply how the team interviews. That makes it one of the few process improvements that, once introduced, maintains itself.

## Measuring the Effect

Track two simple numbers before and after introducing briefings: the average number of days between round one and the final decision, and the offer acceptance rate. Add one question to a short candidate survey: "Did the interview rounds feel coordinated?" Together, these show whether the change improves both speed and candidate experience. On a specialist platform such as **OnlyAIJobs**, where candidates can compare several AI vacancies at once, a smooth process is part of what makes an employer stand out.

## What Candidates Notice About Your Process

Candidates for AI roles often compare notes — with friends, former colleagues and in online communities. They notice when two interviewers ask the same question, when nobody seems to know what happened in the previous round, and when a decision takes weeks without explanation. They also notice the opposite: interviewers who build on earlier conversations, a clear timeline that is kept, and a process that respects their time. In a small professional community, those impressions travel. A well-coordinated second round is therefore not only an efficiency gain, but part of how your organisation is perceived as an employer by the next candidates you want to attract.

## Key Takeaways

- Most time in second rounds is lost to overlap with the first round, not to the number of rounds.
- A tight Dutch ICT labour market, as reflected in UWV's tightness indicator, makes slow processes costly.
- Give each round a separate purpose and scorecard, and appoint a process owner.
- Use a five-minute briefing template, handle salary ranges early under the Pay Transparency Directive, and run reference checks in parallel.
- Reserve interview slots in advance, communicate the timeline, and measure days to decision and offer acceptance.

## Where to Start

Introduce a short briefing between interview rounds, and give each round an explicit, separate purpose known to everyone involved before the conversation starts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer who thinks cutting a round is the solution) Is cutting an interview round the best way to save time?
Not necessarily — the problem is more often overlap between rounds than the number of rounds itself. A short briefing between rounds solves more.

### (Scenario: employer who doesn't know why overlap happens) Why do interviewers often ask the same questions as a colleague did earlier?
Because they prepare their own questions without knowing what was already discussed in the previous round.

### (Scenario: employer who wants to prevent this) What is the simplest way to prevent overlap between rounds?
A short, five-minute briefing between interviewers before the next round starts.

### (Scenario: employer unsure whether candidates may indicate this) May I ask a candidate whether something has already been discussed?
Yes — it saves time and shows respect for the candidate, instead of blindly repeating the same question.

### (Scenario: employer unsure whether each round needs a separate purpose) Should each interview round have a different purpose?
Ideally, yes, explicitly agreed in advance — that prevents several rounds from unintentionally testing the same thing.
