Two interviewers asking a candidate open-ended, unplanned questions will each end up evaluating different things, shaped by whatever came to mind in the moment — including unconscious preferences that have nothing to do with job performance. That inconsistency is easy to mistake for a thorough, well-rounded evaluation, when it's actually closer to noise.

## What Structuring an Interview Actually Involves

**The same core questions for every candidate at a given stage.** This doesn't eliminate follow-up questions specific to a candidate's background, but it ensures every candidate is evaluated against the same baseline criteria, not whatever an interviewer happened to think of.

**Defined evaluation criteria decided before the interview, not after.** Deciding what a strong answer looks like in advance, rather than rationalizing a gut reaction afterward, reduces the influence of factors unrelated to the job.

**Independent scoring before group discussion.** Interviewers who write down their evaluation before discussing with colleagues avoid anchoring on the first strong opinion in the room, which otherwise tends to dominate the group's final judgment.

## Why This Doesn't Have to Slow Down Hiring

Structuring an interview is mostly a preparation cost, done once per role rather than per candidate — it doesn't meaningfully add interview time, and it often shortens post-interview debate because the evaluation criteria are already shared and explicit.

## Where to Start

Write down three to five core questions and what a strong answer looks like before your next round of interviews for a role, and have interviewers score independently before discussing as a group.

## A Deeper Look at Why "Gut Feeling" Feels Reliable But Often Isn't

Interviewers who rely on unstructured gut feeling often report high confidence in their judgments, and that confidence is itself part of the problem — it's not correlated with actual accuracy the way people assume. Research across many hiring contexts (not specific to AI roles) consistently shows that structured, criteria-based evaluation outperforms unstructured impression in predicting actual job performance, even though unstructured evaluation feels more natural and more confident in the moment.

## A Simple Structuring Template for an AI Role Interview

| Interview stage | Core question set (example) | What "strong" looks like, defined in advance |
|---|---|---|
| Technical depth | Walk through a past project's key decisions | Clear reasoning, awareness of tradeoffs, honest about what didn't work |
| Collaboration | Describe handling disagreement with a colleague | Specific example, constructive resolution, no blame-shifting |
| Problem framing | Given an ambiguous prompt, how would you approach it | Asks clarifying questions, states assumptions explicitly |

## Why Structuring Doesn't Mean Losing the Human Element of an Interview

Some interviewers resist structured interviews because they fear it turns a conversation into a checklist exercise, losing the ability to build rapport or read a candidate as a whole person. In practice, structure applies to what gets evaluated and how it's scored — it doesn't prevent a warm, conversational tone, follow-up questions specific to what a candidate says, or genuine back-and-forth. The structure lives in the evaluation criteria, not in making the conversation itself robotic.

No — it ensures a shared baseline while still allowing follow-up questions specific to a candidate's background and answers.
