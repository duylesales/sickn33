Two interviewers asking a candidate open-ended, unplanned questions will each end up evaluating different things, shaped by whatever came to mind in the moment — including unconscious preferences that have nothing to do with job performance. That inconsistency is easy to mistake for a thorough, well-rounded evaluation, when it's actually closer to noise.

## What Structuring an Interview Actually Involves

**The same core questions for every candidate at a given stage.** This doesn't eliminate follow-up questions specific to a candidate's background, but it ensures every candidate is evaluated against the same baseline criteria, not whatever an interviewer happened to think of.

**Defined evaluation criteria decided before the interview, not after.** Deciding what a strong answer looks like in advance, rather than rationalising a gut reaction afterwards, reduces the influence of factors unrelated to the job.

**Independent scoring before group discussion.** Interviewers who write down their evaluation before discussing with colleagues avoid anchoring on the first strong opinion in the room, which otherwise tends to dominate the group's final judgement.

## Why This Doesn't Have to Slow Down Hiring

Structuring an interview is mostly a preparation cost, done once per role rather than per candidate — it doesn't meaningfully add interview time, and it often shortens post-interview debate because the evaluation criteria are already shared and explicit.

## What the Research Says

The case for structured interviews rests on decades of research in personnel psychology. A widely cited meta-analysis by **Schmidt and Hunter (1998)** found that structured interviews predict job performance considerably better than unstructured interviews. A later re-analysis by **Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022)**, which corrected methodological issues in earlier work, lowered many validity estimates across selection methods — but structured interviews came out as one of the strongest predictors among commonly used methods.

The practical conclusion is consistent across this research: consistency in questions and scoring improves the quality of hiring decisions, while the feeling of confidence that unstructured interviews produce isn't a reliable indicator of accuracy.

## A Deeper Look at Why "Gut Feeling" Feels Reliable but Often Isn't

Interviewers who rely on unstructured gut feeling often report high confidence in their judgements, and that confidence is itself part of the problem — it isn't correlated with actual accuracy the way people assume. First impressions, similarity to the interviewer, fluency in the interview language and confidence of delivery all influence unstructured judgements, even though none of them are what the job requires.

## Two Types of Structured Questions

| Type | What it asks | Example for an AI role | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Behavioural question** | How the candidate acted in a past situation | "Tell me about a time a model you built performed worse in production than in testing. What did you do?" | Actual past behaviour and reflection |
| **Situational question** | How the candidate would act in a hypothetical situation | "A stakeholder insists on launching a model you believe is biased against a customer group. How do you handle it?" | Judgement and reasoning about realistic dilemmas |

Combining both types gives a fuller picture: behavioural questions show what candidates have done, situational questions show how they reason about what they haven't encountered yet.

## A Simple Structuring Template for an AI Role Interview

| Interview stage | Core question (example) | What "strong" looks like, defined in advance |
|---|---|---|
| Technical depth | Walk through a past project's key decisions | Clear reasoning, awareness of trade-offs, honest about what didn't work |
| Collaboration | Describe handling disagreement with a colleague | Specific example, constructive resolution, no blame-shifting |
| Problem framing | Given an ambiguous prompt, how would you approach it? | Asks clarifying questions, states assumptions explicitly |
| Responsible AI | How would you check a model for unfair outcomes? | Names concrete checks, understands limits of metrics, involves stakeholders |

## Scoring With Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales

A **behaviourally anchored rating scale (BARS)** describes what an answer looks like at each score level, so that a "4" means the same thing to every interviewer. An example for the problem framing question:

| Score | Anchor description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Jumps straight to a solution without clarifying the problem |
| 2 | Asks one clarifying question, then proposes a generic solution |
| 3 | Clarifies the goal and main constraints, proposes a reasonable approach |
| 4 | Clarifies goal, constraints and data availability; proposes an approach and names risks |
| 5 | As 4, and proposes how to validate the approach early with stakeholders before building |

Writing anchors takes an hour per question the first time, but makes scores comparable across interviewers and candidates.

## Running the Debrief

1. **Independent scores first.** Each interviewer submits scores and short evidence notes before the meeting.
2. **Discuss by criterion, not by candidate impression.** Go through the scorecard criterion by criterion, comparing evidence.
3. **Let the most junior interviewer speak first.** This reduces the influence of seniority on the discussion.
4. **Separate evidence from inference.** "The candidate didn't mention validation" is evidence; "the candidate is careless" is an inference to be tested against other evidence.
5. **Record the decision and reasoning.** Brief documentation helps consistency across candidates and supports feedback to candidates.

## The Legal Frame in the Netherlands

- **Equal treatment.** Under the **Equal Treatment Act**, selection must not discriminate on grounds such as origin, religion, belief, sex, sexual orientation or nationality; age and disability are protected under separate acts. Structured questions linked to job requirements reduce the risk of unlawful questions. The **Netherlands Institute for Human Rights** assesses complaints.
- **Questions to avoid.** Questions about pregnancy plans, religion, health or family situation are generally not allowed unless directly relevant to the job and legally justified.
- **Recruitment code.** The **NVP Recruitment Code** recommends transparency about the procedure and equal treatment of candidates, which structured interviews naturally support.
- **Interview notes.** Scorecards and notes are personal data. The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** advises deleting applicant data within four weeks after the procedure ends unless the candidate consents to longer retention.

## AI Tools in Interviews: What Is and Isn't Allowed

Some vendors offer AI tools that analyse recorded interviews. Under the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act**, AI systems used for recruitment and selection — including evaluating candidates in interviews — are classified as high-risk, with requirements for risk management, human oversight and transparency. More strictly, the Act has prohibited, since February 2025, AI systems that infer emotions of people in the workplace, except for medical or safety reasons. Tools claiming to read a candidate's enthusiasm, honesty or stress from facial expressions or voice therefore raise serious legal concerns. For AI teams in particular, using such tools would also send a poor signal about the organisation's understanding of responsible AI.

## Why Structuring Doesn't Mean Losing the Human Element

Some interviewers resist structured interviews because they fear it turns a conversation into a checklist exercise. In practice, structure applies to what gets evaluated and how it's scored — it doesn't prevent a warm, conversational tone, follow-up questions specific to what a candidate says, or genuine back-and-forth. The structure lives in the evaluation criteria, not in making the conversation robotic.

## A Sample Scorecard for a Machine Learning Engineer Interview

| Criterion | Core question | Score (1–5) | Evidence notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production thinking | "Describe how you took a model from notebook to production. What broke?" | | |
| Evaluation judgement | "How did you decide the model was good enough to launch?" | | |
| Data quality awareness | "Tell me about a time the data turned out to be wrong. How did you find out?" | | |
| Collaboration | "Describe a disagreement with a product owner or stakeholder about a model." | | |
| Responsible AI | "How would you check whether a model disadvantages a group of users?" | | |
| Overall recommendation | Hire / no hire, with one sentence of reasoning | | |

Interviewers fill in the evidence column during or right after the interview, using the candidate's own words where possible. That makes the debrief a comparison of evidence rather than a comparison of impressions.

## Training Interviewers in One Short Session

Structured interviews only work if interviewers apply them consistently. A short training session of about an hour covers most of what is needed:

1. **Why structure matters.** Briefly explain the research and the risks of unstructured judgement.
2. **Practising anchors.** Have interviewers score two example answers individually, then compare scores and discuss differences — this calibrates the team quickly.
3. **Follow-up questions.** Practise neutral follow-ups such as "What did you do next?" or "What would you do differently?" that deepen evidence without steering the candidate.
4. **Legal boundaries.** Review which questions are not allowed under equal treatment rules, and how to respond if a candidate raises sensitive topics themselves.
5. **Note-taking.** Agree on recording evidence, not opinions, and on where notes are stored and when they are deleted.

Repeat a short calibration exercise once or twice a year, or when new interviewers join the panel.

## Interviewing Candidates Who Work in a Second Language

Dutch AI teams often interview international candidates in English, which is a second language for most interviewers and many candidates. Unstructured interviews tend to reward fluency and confident delivery, which can disadvantage strong candidates whose English — or Dutch — is less polished. Structure helps here in three ways:

- **Scoring content, not delivery.** Anchors describe what a strong answer contains, so interviewers focus on reasoning and evidence rather than accent or speed of speech.
- **Sharing questions in writing.** For complex case questions, showing the question on screen or on paper reduces misunderstandings caused by listening alone.
- **Allowing clarification.** Explicitly inviting candidates to ask for a question to be rephrased makes the interview test the job-relevant skill rather than listening comprehension under pressure.

If a role genuinely requires strong Dutch or English communication, assess that as a separate, explicit criterion — not implicitly through every answer.

## Rolling It Out in One Afternoon

1. **Define four or five criteria** for the role, based on what success looks like after six months.
2. **Write one or two core questions per criterion**, mixing behavioural and situational questions.
3. **Write anchors** for scores 1, 3 and 5 for each question.
4. **Brief all interviewers** in thirty minutes on the questions, anchors and debrief rules.
5. **Review after five candidates** whether questions discriminate between candidates and adjust.

## Key Takeaways

- Unstructured interviews introduce inconsistency that feels like thoroughness but acts like noise.
- Research from Schmidt and Hunter to Sackett and colleagues supports structured interviews as one of the stronger predictors of job performance.
- Combine behavioural and situational questions, score with behaviourally anchored rating scales, and score independently before the debrief.
- Structured questions help comply with Dutch equal treatment law and the NVP Recruitment Code; handle notes under GDPR retention guidance.
- AI interview analysis is high-risk under the AI Act, and emotion recognition in the workplace is prohibited.
