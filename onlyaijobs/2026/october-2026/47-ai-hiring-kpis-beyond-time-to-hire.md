Time-to-hire is easy to measure, which is exactly why so many hiring processes optimize for it — but a fast hire who leaves within a year, or who never becomes productive, is a worse outcome than a slower hire who stays and performs well. Speed alone measures the wrong thing.

## Metrics That Actually Predict Long-Term Success

**Time-to-productivity, not just time-to-hire.** How long after starting does a new hire ship meaningful work independently? This tells you more about hiring and onboarding quality combined than speed of the hiring process alone.

**Retention at the 12-month mark, segmented by source.** Tracking which channels produce hires who stay past a year — versus those who churn quickly — reveals which sourcing methods are actually worth the investment, not just which are fastest.

**Quality-of-hire feedback from the hiring manager, six months in.** A structured check-in asking whether the hire matches what was expected surfaces mismatches earlier than waiting for a resignation to reveal the problem.

## Why Time-to-Hire Alone Distorts Decisions

Optimizing purely for speed pushes toward the fastest available candidate rather than the best fit, and can mask a pattern of rushed, low-quality hires behind an impressive-looking metric.

## Where to Start

Add at least one longer-horizon metric — 12-month retention by source, or a structured 6-month quality check-in — alongside time-to-hire, so speed isn't the only signal shaping your hiring decisions.

## A Deeper Look at Why Time-to-Hire Became the Default Metric in the First Place

Time-to-hire dominates hiring dashboards largely because it's the easiest metric to capture automatically from an applicant-tracking system, not because anyone deliberately decided it was the most important thing to measure. Retention and productivity metrics require deliberate follow-up long after the hiring process itself has closed, which most recruiting teams aren't structurally set up to track — the metric that's easiest to measure crowds out the metrics that actually matter, simply through the path of least resistance.

## A Minimal Viable Tracking System Beyond Time-to-Hire

| Metric | Data needed | Who should own tracking it |
|---|---|---|
| 12-month retention by source | Hire date, source, departure date if applicable | Recruiting/People team, reviewed quarterly |
| 6-month quality-of-hire check-in | A short structured survey to hiring managers | Hiring manager, prompted by People team |
| Time-to-productivity | Hiring manager's judgment of when a hire became independently productive | Hiring manager, self-reported |

## Why Starting Small Is Better Than Waiting for a Perfect System

Teams sometimes delay adding any metric beyond time-to-hire because they want a fully automated, comprehensive system first. A simple, manually-tracked six-month check-in started this quarter produces more useful information a year from now than a perfect system still being designed a year from now. The value of these metrics compounds with time in use — the earlier you start, even imperfectly, the sooner you have real data to act on.

## Why Hiring Metrics Are Harder for AI Roles Specifically

Measuring hiring quality is difficult in general, but several characteristics of AI and data roles make it harder still, and understanding why prevents organisations from drawing false conclusions from their own data.

The first is that time-to-productivity is genuinely longer and more variable than for many other technical roles. As the onboarding analysis elsewhere on this site describes, a data hire frequently spends the first months on access, data archaeology and context-building before producing anything measurable. An organisation measuring productivity at ninety days may be measuring onboarding quality rather than hiring quality, and the two require different responses.

The second is that individual contribution is harder to isolate. A model's business impact depends on data quality, on adoption by operational staff, and on decisions made by others — which means a strong hire in a poorly prepared organisation may show worse measurable results than a weaker hire in a well-prepared one. Attributing that difference to hiring quality is a mistake that recurs frequently.

The third is that the sample sizes are small. Most Dutch organisations described on this site hire a handful of data practitioners over several years, which means statistical conclusions about channel effectiveness or assessment validity rest on very few observations. This does not make measurement useless, but it does mean treating patterns as suggestive rather than conclusive.

## A Minimal Metric Set That Small Organisations Can Actually Sustain

Given those difficulties, the practical question is what a small organisation can measure without building infrastructure it cannot maintain.

Three data points, recorded for every technical hire, provide most of the available value. The first is source: how did this person come to us, recorded consistently enough to compare channels later. The second is a hiring manager assessment at six months, answering a single question — knowing what you know now, would you make this hire again — recorded honestly. The third is whether the person is still employed at twelve and twenty-four months, and if not, the reason as best it is understood.

These three items take minutes to record per hire and, accumulated over several years, answer the questions that matter: which channels produce hires who work out, whether the process is improving, and whether departures cluster around identifiable causes.

Organisations attempting more elaborate measurement frequently abandon it within a year because the maintenance burden exceeds the perceived value. A minimal set that is actually sustained produces more insight than a comprehensive framework that lapses.

## Interpreting the Results Without Overreacting

Once data accumulates, the interpretation requires care, particularly given the small sample sizes discussed above.

A single unsuccessful hire from a particular channel says almost nothing. Three unsuccessful hires from the same channel, against a background of successes elsewhere, is worth investigating. The threshold for action should be higher than most organisations apply, because reacting to noise produces process churn that itself degrades hiring quality.

Departures require particular care in interpretation. A person leaving after eighteen months for a genuinely better opportunity is not evidence of a hiring failure, and organisations that treat all turnover as a quality signal will systematically undervalue channels that produce ambitious, capable people. The distinction discussed in the retention analysis elsewhere on this site — between avoidable and unavoidable departures — applies directly to how departure data should be read.

The most reliable signal across small samples is the hiring manager's six-month assessment, precisely because it aggregates many observations the manager has made into a judgement that no single metric captures. Organisations that collect this consistently and take it seriously generally learn more than those pursuing quantitative sophistication their sample sizes cannot support.

## Connecting Hiring Metrics to Process Changes

Measurement produces value only when it changes something, and the link between metric and action deserves explicit attention.

If retention by source shows a channel producing consistently short-tenured hires, the response is to examine what that channel selects for — often speed of availability rather than fit — rather than simply to abandon it. If six-month assessments cluster negatively for hires from a particular assessment format, that format's validity is in question, as discussed in the assessment design analysis elsewhere on this site.

If time-to-productivity is consistently long across all hires regardless of source, the finding is probably about onboarding rather than hiring, and the response belongs in the onboarding process rather than in candidate selection.

This mapping from finding to response should be decided before the data arrives, because deciding afterward invites the interpretation that requires least change. Organisations that agree in advance what they would do if a particular pattern emerged act on their data considerably more often than those that collect first and decide later.

## What Not to Measure

A final observation: some commonly tracked hiring metrics actively mislead for AI roles, and dropping them improves decision-making.

Application volume is the clearest example. High volume is frequently a symptom of a poorly targeted posting rather than a sign of attractiveness, as the job description analysis elsewhere on this site argues. An organisation optimising for application volume is optimising for screening burden.

Cost-per-hire, taken alone, similarly misleads. The cheapest channel that produces hires who leave within a year is more expensive than an apparently costly channel producing hires who stay, once the costs described in the mismatch analysis elsewhere on this site are counted.

Interview-to-offer ratios can be informative but are frequently over-interpreted at small sample sizes, and organisations that set targets for them tend to distort their own processes to meet the target rather than improving the underlying decisions.

The general principle is to measure outcomes rather than activity, and to resist metrics that are easy to collect but weakly connected to whether hiring is actually working.

## Measuring the Candidate Experience Alongside the Outcomes

One dimension that outcome metrics miss entirely is how the process felt to the people who went through it, and in the small professional communities described throughout this site this matters more than its neglect suggests.

A short question to candidates at the conclusion of a process — successful or not — asking whether the process was clear, timely and respectful of their time, produces information available nowhere else. Candidates who declined an offer are a particularly valuable source, since they have no reason to flatter and frequently explain something the organisation could not see.

The reason this matters commercially rather than merely ethically is that candidate experience propagates. In a market where the relevant community for a specialisation may number in the hundreds nationally, a process that consistently frustrates candidates accumulates a reputation that reaches people before they ever apply. Several employers described in the regional analyses on this site compete in exactly such markets.

Collecting this feedback costs a single question and reading the answers. Acting on it — shortening a process that candidates found excessive, adding communication where they experienced silence — addresses precisely the issues the hiring process analysis elsewhere on this site identifies as driving candidate withdrawal.
