A carefully selected candidate can still leave within a few months — not because the hiring decision was wrong, but because the first weeks gave no clear picture of what was expected. Onboarding is often treated as an administrative formality, while technically and relationally it is as important as the selection process itself.

## What Is Different About Onboarding for AI Roles

**Access to data and systems is often arranged more slowly than for a regular software role.** A new data scientist waiting two weeks for access to the datasets they need loses not only time but also motivation — arrange this before the first working day, not after.

**The existing state of data and models must be explained explicitly, not discovered.** A new hire who has to work out alone which datasets are reliable and which models are outdated wastes weeks on something half an hour of documentation could have prevented.

**An early, small technical contribution works better than a long observation period.** A new employee who delivers something small but visible within the first two weeks builds trust faster than someone who "shadows" for weeks without concrete output.

## Why This Is Specifically About AI Roles

Data and model access is technically and organisationally more complex to arrange than access to a regular codebase, which means AI onboarding more often fails on practical, fixable matters than on anything fundamental.

## A Deeper Look at Why Data Access So Often Gets Delayed

Access to a codebase is usually a matter of creating one account. Access to production data often requires coordination with several parties — a data owner, a security officer, sometimes a legal review because of privacy-sensitive data — who simply don't exist in a regular software role. Companies structurally underestimate how many separate approvals are needed, and therefore start requesting them too late.

## The Named Rules Behind Data Access

Understanding the frameworks behind those approvals helps you plan them in time:

- **GDPR.** Access to personal data must be limited to what an employee needs for their tasks. That principle of data minimisation and purpose limitation means access can't simply be granted "to everything."
- **Data Protection Officer.** Organisations that process personal data on a large scale or in sensitive categories must appoint a Data Protection Officer (*functionaris gegevensbescherming*), who often has to be consulted about new data uses.
- **Data protection impact assessment (DPIA).** For high-risk processing — common in AI projects using large datasets about people — a DPIA must be carried out before processing starts. If the new hire's first project requires one, start it before they arrive.
- **ISO/IEC 27001.** Many organisations use this information security standard, which requires formal access control procedures, including role-based access, approval and periodic review.

## Comparison Table: Onboarding Checklist for an AI Hire

| Action | When to arrange | Consequence if too late |
|---|---|---|
| Access to required datasets | Before the first working day | Weeks of delay, demotivated new hire |
| Documentation of existing models | First week | New hire rediscovers known problems alone |
| First small, visible task | First two weeks | Trust builds more slowly |
| Introduction to relevant stakeholders | First month | New hire lacks context for priorities |

## Why a Checklist Alone Isn't Enough Without an Owner

An onboarding checklist that nobody specifically monitors quickly dilutes into a document nobody really follows. Explicitly appoint someone — not necessarily the direct manager — responsible for actually ticking off each item before and during the first weeks. Many teams pair this with an onboarding buddy: a colleague the new hire can ask small questions without feeling they are bothering their manager.

## Legal and Practical Obligations in the First Weeks

- **Written information about terms of employment.** Under the **Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions Act**, employers must inform new employees in writing about essential terms of employment within a short period after they start, and provide mandatory training free of charge.
- **AI literacy.** Since February 2025, the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act** requires organisations that provide or deploy AI systems to take measures to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff working with those systems. Onboarding is a natural moment to explain internal AI policies, approved tools and responsible use — also for data specialists, who need to know the organisation's specific rules.
- **Working conditions.** The **Working Conditions Act** requires employers to address psychosocial workload, including work pressure. New hires eager to prove themselves are particularly vulnerable to overload.
- **International hires.** New employees from abroad must register with their municipality to obtain a citizen service number (BSN), needed for salary payment, among other things. Plan time for this in the first week, or use a regional expat centre that combines formalities.

## A Concrete Onboarding Plan for the First Thirty Days

Before the first working day, access to all relevant systems and datasets should be arranged, including the necessary approvals. This sounds obvious, but for AI roles it is the most common cause of a slow start. Whoever starts this process on the first working day typically loses two to three weeks.

In the first week, a new employee should get a structured overview of which data exists, how reliable each source is, and which known pitfalls exist. This doesn't have to be an extensive document — a one-hour conversation with someone who knows the history, supplemented by short notes, prevents weeks of solo archaeology.

In the second week, a first small, well-defined piece of work should be available that can be finished within two weeks and visibly delivers something. It is less about delivering value than about giving the new employee an early success and the team a first impression of working together.

In weeks three and four, the new employee should meet the most important stakeholders outside the direct team — the people who will ultimately use what is built. In industrial and institutional contexts, the quality of these relationships often determines whether the work is used at all.

## Why the First Hire Needs Different Onboarding From the Fifth

Organisations hiring their first data specialist can't apply a standard onboarding process. There is no team to join, no existing conventions to learn, and no colleague who knows the pitfalls in the data. Part of onboarding must therefore be replaced by explicit introductions to the people who know the operational processes, as they carry the domain knowledge that would otherwise come from a data colleague.

Expectation management matters more too. A first data hire without a model in production after three months is probably functioning normally — they must build the foundations a fifth hire would find in place. Finally, external professional contacts are a necessity for this employee, not a luxury; employers who free up time and budget for professional events invest directly in their only data specialist's effectiveness.

## How to Evaluate and Improve Onboarding

The simplest evaluation is a conversation after six to eight weeks asking what helped most, what was missing, and where the new employee unnecessarily lost time. The information is still fresh, and the employee is still enough of an outsider to see what an established colleague no longer notices.

A short, living checklist updated after every new hire is more effective than an extensive onboarding document written once and then left to age. Updating it costs a few minutes per hire and saves days for every following hire. Over several hires, the fifth becomes productive within weeks where the first needed months — not because the person is better, but because the organisation has learned.

## What Good Onboarding Delivers in Retention Terms

AI employees rarely leave primarily for salary; they leave because their work doesn't reach production, because their role turned out different from what was promised, or because they feel unable to be effective. All three are shaped in the first months. A new hire still waiting for data access after two weeks, still unsure after a month which datasets are reliable, and without anything visible after three months forms exactly the impression that leads to early departure. The cost of an early departure far exceeds the investment in careful onboarding.

## The Role of the Manager in the First Months

The most important element is frequency of contact at the start. A short weekly conversation in the first two months — not as an assessment but as an opportunity to ask questions that feel too small for a separate meeting — prevents small ambiguities from piling up into major misunderstandings.

The second element is explicit expectation setting. A new employee who doesn't know what they'll be assessed on after three months fills that in themselves, often differently from what the manager meant.

The third element is protection against overload. A manager who actively prioritises and explicitly gives permission not to do things prevents early exhaustion, a real risk among ambitious new hires.

## Connecting a New Employee With the Right People

The most valuable connection is with whoever knows the operational processes best — rarely a manager, often someone with years of hands-on experience whose knowledge is documented nowhere. The second is with the eventual user of the work: the planner, quality manager or policy officer who must act on the results. The third, often forgotten, is with whoever manages the systems where the data lives, which in practice determines how quickly new sources become accessible.

## What a New Employee Can Do Themselves

When onboarding falls short, a new hire can ask explicitly in the first week for a conversation with whoever knows the history of the data, ask for a small, well-defined first task if none is offered, and document what they run into and wish they had known. That last step gives the organisation exactly the material the next new hire will need.

## Key Takeaways

- AI onboarding most often fails on practical matters such as delayed data access, not on fundamental problems.
- GDPR data minimisation, Data Protection Officer consultation, DPIAs and ISO/IEC 27001 access procedures explain why data access takes time — start before day one.
- Written terms under the Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions Act, AI literacy under the AI Act, workload under the Working Conditions Act and BSN registration for international hires belong in the first weeks.
- Use a 30-day plan: access before day one, a data overview in week one, a small visible task in week two, stakeholder introductions in weeks three and four.
- Appoint an owner and a buddy, hold weekly manager check-ins, and update a living checklist after every hire.

## Where to Start

Arrange data and system access before the first working day, document the state of existing data and models explicitly, and give a new hire a small, visible task within two weeks.
