Two candidates can each list "built a machine learning project" on their CV and mean completely different things by it. A hiring manager reviewing dozens of similar-sounding bullet points has learned to look past the label to a few specific signals that actually predict whether the project reflects real skill.

## What Differentiates a Project That Helps

**A problem with a real, checkable outcome.** A project where you can point to a specific result — even a modest one — and explain why it matters, carries more weight than a generic tutorial project with an impressive-sounding name.

**Evidence of iteration, not a single attempt.** Mentioning what you tried first, why it didn't work, and what you changed shows a process a hiring manager can evaluate, unlike a project described only by its final state.

**Something a stranger can actually verify.** A public repository, a brief write-up, or a working demo lets someone check your claim rather than take it on faith — this matters more than the project's apparent sophistication.

## What Doesn't Move the Needle Much

Following a well-known tutorial exactly, without a personal twist or extension, signals that you can follow instructions — a real but limited skill compared to defining and solving your own problem. Hiring managers have seen the same tutorial project many times and generally recognize it immediately.

## Where to Start

Pick one project you've already done and make its outcome, iteration process and verifiability explicit and visible — often more valuable than starting an entirely new project from scratch.

## A Deeper Look at Why Verifiability Matters More Than Sophistication

A hiring manager reviewing dozens of CVs has learned to discount claims that can't be checked — "built a recommendation system" means very little without a way to see the actual code, the actual write-up, or a working demo. Verifiability isn't about proving you're not lying; it's about giving a busy reviewer a fast, low-effort way to confirm your claim is real, which itself is a small act of consideration that a hiring manager notices, even unconsciously.

## Comparing How the Same Project Reads With and Without Verifiable Evidence

| Description style | How it reads to a hiring manager |
|---|---|
| "Built a machine learning model for X" (no link) | Unverifiable claim, discounted by default |
| "Built a machine learning model for X — repo here, brief write-up here" | Verifiable, worth ten minutes of a reviewer's time to check |
| "Built and deployed a simple version of X, live demo here" | Strongest signal — shows both modeling and deployment follow-through |

## Why a Slightly Messy, Real Project Beats a Polished, Generic One

A project with visible rough edges — a README that honestly notes what doesn't work yet, a commit history that shows iteration and occasional dead ends — often reads as more credible than a project polished to look flawless, because real work looks like the former and hiring managers know this from their own experience. Resist the instinct to hide the messy parts of a project; they're often the most convincing evidence that the work is genuinely yours.

## Choosing a Project Topic That Serves Your Actual Search

The choice of what to build is more consequential than most candidates treat it, because a project that connects to the specific market segment you are targeting does considerably more work than a generically impressive one.

If you are targeting the industrial and manufacturing employers described in the regional analyses on this site, a project involving sensor data, time series with irregular sampling, or anomaly detection under class imbalance speaks directly to what those employers deal with daily. If you are targeting research-adjacent employers, a project demonstrating careful experimental design and honest treatment of uncertainty speaks their language. If you are targeting the governance-adjacent roles discussed elsewhere, a project that documents model limitations rigorously and considers failure modes systematically demonstrates precisely the disposition those roles require.

This alignment costs nothing extra — you have to build something regardless — but it transforms the project from generic evidence of competence into specific evidence of fit. A hiring manager reading about a project that resembles their own problems reads it differently from one reading about a technically similar project in an unrelated domain.

The practical step is to decide which two or three segments you are actually targeting before choosing what to build, rather than building first and hoping the relevance is apparent.

## Working With Data You Collect Yourself

A specific recommendation worth expanding: projects built on data you gathered yourself carry disproportionate weight relative to the effort involved.

The reason is that collecting data forces engagement with questions that prepared datasets have already answered invisibly. What exactly are you measuring, and does that measurement actually capture what you care about? What is missing, and is the missingness random or systematic? How consistent is the collection process over time? These are precisely the questions that determine whether a real-world project succeeds, and they are entirely absent from a project that begins with a downloaded CSV.

The data need not be exotic. Sensor readings from an inexpensive device, images you photograph under varying conditions, records you extract from a public source that was never designed for analysis — all of these produce the relevant difficulty. The specific subject matters less than the fact that you had to make collection decisions and live with their consequences.

An additional benefit is differentiation. A hiring manager reviewing many portfolios sees the same public datasets repeatedly, and a project built on data nobody else has automatically stands apart before any assessment of the modelling itself.

## Documenting a Project So It Actually Communicates

A project that a reviewer cannot quickly understand does not help you, regardless of its technical quality, and the documentation is frequently where otherwise strong projects fail.

The structure that works opens with the problem and why it matters, in two or three sentences that assume no prior context. It then states what you built and what the outcome was, before any technical detail. Only after that does it describe the approach, and it explains decisions rather than steps — the distinction discussed in the interview preparation analysis elsewhere on this site applies equally here.

Critically, it includes a section on limitations. What does the system get wrong? Under what conditions would it fail? What would you do differently? Candidates fear this section undermines the project; in practice it is frequently the section that persuades an experienced reviewer that you understand what you built.

Length matters too. A reviewer spending five minutes on your project will not read three thousand words. A clear page that communicates the essentials, with deeper detail available for anyone who wants it, respects the reader's time and consequently gets read.

## How Many Projects Are Enough

Candidates frequently ask how many projects a portfolio should contain, and the honest answer is that two or three well-understood projects outperform six shallow ones consistently.

The reason is that portfolio projects function primarily as interview material rather than as a body of work to be evaluated in isolation. A project you can discuss fluently for twenty minutes — the decisions, the failures, the alternatives you rejected — provides substantially more value than three projects you can each describe for two minutes. Interviewers probe depth, and shallow projects collapse under probing in a way that damages rather than helps.

There is a secondary consideration about coverage. If your two or three projects together demonstrate problem framing, honest evaluation and some engagement with deployment, they cover the dimensions most employers care about. Additional projects demonstrating the same dimensions add little; a project demonstrating a dimension the others miss adds more.

The practical guidance is to stop adding projects once you have covered those dimensions, and to invest remaining effort in deepening your understanding of what you have already built rather than accumulating more.

## What to Do With Projects That Did Not Work

An underused category of portfolio material is the project that failed, and candidates discard these when they frequently contain the most useful interview material available.

A project where the approach did not work, where you discovered midway that the data could not support the question, or where the result was inconclusive, demonstrates something a successful project cannot: that you can recognise failure, diagnose it, and articulate what you learned. Given how much real technical work consists of approaches that do not pan out, this is directly relevant evidence.

The presentation matters. A failed project presented as an excuse reads poorly; the same project presented as a clear account of a hypothesis, why it seemed reasonable, what actually happened, and what you concluded reads as professional maturity. Many experienced interviewers find this material more informative than a polished success.

The practical implication is to keep notes on projects that did not work rather than abandoning them entirely, and to consider explicitly including one in your portfolio material — not as your primary evidence, but as a demonstration of a quality that successful projects cannot show.

## Where to Host and How to Present Your Work

Practical questions about presentation arise once the work exists, and a few conventions are worth knowing.

A public code repository remains the baseline expectation for technical roles, and its README is the document most reviewers will actually read. Treating that file as the primary communication — following the structure described earlier rather than the default listing of installation instructions — converts a repository from something a reviewer skims into something that actually communicates.

A short written piece, whether on a personal site or any public writing platform, adds a dimension a repository cannot: it demonstrates that you can explain technical work in prose, a capability the regional analyses on this site consistently identify as valued across Dutch employers, particularly in the industrial and institutional contexts where technical staff routinely communicate with non-technical colleagues.

A working demonstration, where the project permits one, is the strongest single addition. It need not be polished; a modest deployed version that a reviewer can actually interact with converts an abstract claim into something concrete in a way no amount of description achieves.

What matters less than candidates assume is visual polish. Reviewers evaluating technical candidates are looking for evidence of thinking, not for design quality, and effort spent on presentation aesthetics generally returns less than the same effort spent on clearer explanation.

## When Portfolio Work Stops Being Necessary

A final practical note: portfolio projects matter most early in a career and progressively less as professional experience accumulates, and recognising when to stop investing in them prevents wasted effort.

For a candidate with no professional experience in the field, portfolio work is the primary evidence available and deserves substantial investment. For a candidate with two or three years of relevant professional work, the professional work becomes the primary evidence, and portfolio projects shift to a supporting role — useful for demonstrating a specific capability your professional work has not covered, less useful as general proof of competence.

For a candidate with substantial professional experience, portfolio projects rarely change hiring outcomes and the same time is usually better spent on the professional visibility discussed in the networking analysis elsewhere on this site.

The transition point is worth recognising because candidates sometimes continue investing heavily in portfolio work long after it has stopped being the binding constraint on their search, while neglecting the professional relationships that have become more consequential.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate with a generic tutorial project) Does following a well-known tutorial project help my job search?
It signals you can follow instructions, but hiring managers recognize these projects easily and they carry less weight than a project with a personal twist or a self-defined problem.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure what makes a project stand out) What actually differentiates a strong side project from a weak one?
A real, checkable outcome, evidence of iteration (what you tried and changed), and something a stranger can verify — a repository, write-up or demo.

### (Scenario: candidate with an unfinished or modest project) Does a project need to be sophisticated to be worth showing?
No — a modest project with a clear, checkable outcome and visible iteration is often more convincing than an impressive-sounding but unverifiable one.

### (Scenario: candidate wondering whether to build something new) Should I start a new project, or improve one I've already done?
Often better to make an existing project's outcome, process and verifiability explicit, rather than starting from scratch — the improvement itself demonstrates the iteration hiring managers look for.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure how to present a project) How should I describe a project to a hiring manager most effectively?
Describe the outcome, what you tried that didn't work, and what you changed — not just the final architecture or tool list.
