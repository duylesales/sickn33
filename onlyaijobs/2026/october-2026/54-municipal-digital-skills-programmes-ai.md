Many municipalities run digital skills programmes aimed at residents — basic digital and data literacy, introductory technical courses, retraining support. These programmes teach real capability, but they rarely connect participants to concrete evidence that local jobs using those skills actually exist nearby.

## The Gap This Creates

A resident who completes a digital skills course has a new capability but often no clear next step — they're pointed towards a general job board that shows a city name, not a specific local employer at a specific distance from home. The skill and the opportunity exist; the connection between them is the missing piece.

## The Named Landscape of Digital Skills Support in the Netherlands

Dutch municipalities don't run digital skills programmes in isolation. Several named organisations and schemes form the landscape they work within:

**Public libraries and Government Information Points.** Many public libraries host a **Government Information Point** (*Informatiepunt Digitale Overheid*), where residents get help with digital government services. Libraries also offer courses such as **Digisterker**, which teaches residents to use digital government services, and other introductory digital skills courses.

**Stichting Lezen & Schrijven.** This foundation works on basic skills — reading, writing, numeracy and digital skills — and supports municipalities in reaching residents with low basic skills.

**Learning and Work Desks.** In the labour market regions, **Learning and Work Desks** (*Leerwerkloketten*), run by partners including municipalities, UWV and education institutions, give free advice on learning and career development to residents.

**SLIM subsidy.** Through the **SLIM** scheme, small and medium-sized enterprises can receive subsidies for training and development of their employees — a route for local employers to upskill staff in digital and data skills.

**Participation Act.** For residents receiving social assistance, municipalities are responsible for reintegration under the **Participation Act**. Digital skills training is often part of reintegration trajectories.

**National and European policy.** The **Dutch Digitalisation Strategy** sets government-wide priorities for digitalisation, including digital inclusion and skills. At EU level, the **Digital Decade** programme includes a target for at least 80% of adults to have basic digital skills by 2030.

## How This Pairs With Existing Programmes

**A concrete next step at programme completion.** Pointing participants towards a visible, address-level list of local AI, machine learning, data and related vacancies gives the programme's outcome somewhere specific to go, rather than a vague "now go find a job."

**A feedback loop for programme design.** Seeing which local employers list which kinds of roles can inform what the next cohort should actually learn, grounding curriculum decisions in visible local demand rather than a generic national curriculum.

**A low-cost addition, not a new initiative.** This doesn't require new infrastructure — it's a pointer added to an existing programme's closing materials.

## What This Is Not

This isn't a claim that a digital skills programme alone creates jobs, or that every participant will find a matching local role. It addresses a narrower, specific gap: connecting a programme's outcome to visible, checkable local opportunity.

## Matching Programme Levels to Realistic Next Steps

Not every digital skills programme prepares residents for data jobs, and honesty about that makes the pairing credible:

| Programme level | Typical content | Realistic next step |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic digital skills** | Using email, government websites, online safety | Further courses; jobs where basic digital skills are required but not the core |
| **Digital skills for work** | Office software, spreadsheets, online collaboration | Administrative and operational roles with digital tasks; entry to further training |
| **Introductory data skills** | Spreadsheets at an advanced level, basic data analysis, visualisation | Junior reporting or data support roles, internships, work-study routes |
| **Retraining towards IT and data** | SQL, programming basics, data analysis tools | Junior data analyst, data support or IT roles, often with employer-based training |

A reference to local AI and data vacancies is most useful at the last two levels, while at the first two it mainly serves as motivation — showing residents where further learning can lead.

## Why AI Literacy Makes This More Urgent

Since February 2025, the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act** has required organisations that provide or deploy AI systems to take measures to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff working with those systems. That obligation applies to employers of all sizes, including local SMEs and public organisations. As a result, basic understanding of what AI tools do — and their limits — is becoming relevant well beyond specialist roles. Municipal programmes that add a short module on responsible AI use prepare residents for workplaces where this knowledge is increasingly expected.

## A Deeper Look at the "Skills Without a Destination" Problem

Municipal digital skills programmes are often measured on completion rates and satisfaction scores — reasonable metrics for the training itself, but silent on what happens after a resident finishes. That creates a structural blind spot: a programme can be "successful" by its own metrics while still failing to connect participants to real outcomes, simply because nobody measured that connection.

## Comparing Programme Outcomes With and Without a Concrete Next Step

| | Programme without a clear next step | Programme with a visible local job resource added |
|---|---|---|
| Participant's immediate next action | Unclear, often defaults to a generic job board | Concrete: check specific local vacancies |
| Programme's measurable impact | Limited to completion and satisfaction | Can extend to job-search engagement and follow-up training |
| Cost to add | — | Minimal — a link and brief explanation |

## A Concrete Scenario

A medium-sized municipality runs a twelve-week course in spreadsheet and data basics for residents looking for work, in cooperation with the local library and the regional Learning and Work Desk. In the final session, the course leader shows participants how to search vacancies by distance from home and category, and discusses three real local vacancies with a data component: a planning assistant at a distribution centre, a reporting employee at a care organisation and an administrative data role at a housing corporation. Participants leave with a concrete list, not a general instruction. Three months later, the municipality asks participants — voluntarily and anonymously — whether they applied for such roles or started follow-up training.

## Involving Local Employers

The pairing works best when employers are involved from the start:

- **Invite employers to the final session** to explain what their data-related roles involve in practice.
- **Point SMEs to the SLIM scheme** for training their own staff, which can create internal openings.
- **Encourage employers to make vacancies visible** with an exact location, so participants living nearby can find them.
- **Use Employer Service Points** in the labour market region to reach employers already working with the municipality.

## A Sample Closing Session Plan

A 90-minute closing session can turn the final meeting of a programme into a concrete bridge to local work:

| Time | Activity | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–15 min | Recap of skills learned, with short participant examples | Build confidence in what participants can now do |
| 15–35 min | Guest talk by a local employer about a role with a data component | Make local work tangible |
| 35–55 min | Demonstration: searching vacancies by distance from home and category | Give a practical, repeatable next step |
| 55–75 min | Individual exploration of three local vacancies with the course leader | Personalise the next step |
| 75–90 min | Information on follow-up training and Learning and Work Desk support | Offer routes for those not yet ready to apply |

Participants leave with a short personal list: two vacancies to consider, one follow-up training option and one contact person.

## Questions to Ask Local Employers Before Involving Them

- Which roles in your organisation involve working with data, spreadsheets or digital systems as a significant part of the job?
- Which of those roles could someone with a digital skills course and motivation grow into?
- Do you offer on-the-job training or work-study places for such roles?
- Would you be willing to explain the work in a programme session?
- Can your vacancies be published with an exact location so residents nearby can find them?

Employers who answer these questions positively are the best partners for the programme — and often the first to hire participants.

## Linking the Programme to Regional Labour Market Information

Municipalities can use regional labour market information from UWV and the labour market region to identify occupations with shortages where digital and data skills are increasingly required. Combining that information with visible local vacancies helps programme designers choose which skills to emphasise in the next cohort, and helps participants understand where demand actually exists.

## Privacy and Neutrality

Municipalities should present job resources neutrally, as one of several options, and avoid referring exclusively to one commercial party. Participant data from social domain programmes is sensitive; don't share it with external platforms. Evaluate outcomes with voluntary, anonymous surveys rather than tracking individuals.

## Why This Small Addition Can Improve How the Programme Is Perceived

A digital skills programme that visibly connects participants to concrete local opportunities is easier to justify to councils, funders and stakeholders than one that ends with a certificate and a vague hope. The connection strengthens the case for continued funding by making real-world impact more visible and traceable.

## Measuring Impact Without Tracking Individuals

| Indicator | How to measure |
|---|---|
| Awareness of local data-related roles | One question in the final evaluation |
| Job-search engagement | Voluntary follow-up survey after three months |
| Follow-up training | Number of referrals to Learning and Work Desks or further courses |
| Employer involvement | Number of employers participating in sessions or offering placements |

## A Note on Inclusion

Digital skills programmes often reach residents who face additional barriers to work: limited basic skills, language barriers, health limitations or long periods outside the labour market. For them, a list of data vacancies can feel distant or even discouraging if it is presented without context. Course leaders can prevent that by presenting vacancies as possible destinations over time, showing intermediate steps such as volunteer roles, work placements or further courses, and making clear that each step counts. The goal is to make the connection between learning and work visible for every participant, at whatever distance from the labour market they currently are — not only for those already close to a data role.

## Key Takeaways

- Digital skills programmes often lack a concrete next step linking new skills to local jobs.
- Libraries with Government Information Points, Stichting Lezen & Schrijven, Learning and Work Desks, the SLIM scheme and the Participation Act form the Dutch support landscape.
- Match references to programme level: motivation at basic levels, concrete vacancies at data and retraining levels.
- The AI Act's AI literacy obligation makes basic understanding of AI tools relevant for many residents' future workplaces.
- Involve employers, stay neutral, protect participant data and measure impact anonymously.
