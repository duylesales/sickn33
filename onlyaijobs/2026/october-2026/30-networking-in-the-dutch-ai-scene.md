"Networking" sounds to many technical people like giving a sales pitch at drinks — something that feels uncomfortable and yields little. What works in practice looks very different: consistent, substantive visibility over time, not an evening of handing out business cards.

## What Actually Works

**Sharing something you're working on.** A short explanation of a problem you solved, shared on LinkedIn or in a relevant community, reaches more people than an evening of conversations — and it isn't a sales pitch, it's simply showing your work.

**Recurring presence, not a one-off impression.** Someone who turns up three times at the same meetup or online community is recognised in a way one appearance never achieves.

**Asking questions instead of presenting yourself.** A genuine question to someone with more experience in a specific area opens a conversation more often than a summary of your own CV.

## Why This Matters for Your Job Search

A considerable share of the most interesting roles is filled before a vacancy is ever widely advertised — through existing contacts or referrals. Visibility in the field increases the chance you become part of that informal flow, without having to hunt for it actively.

## A Deeper Look at Why "Giving Before Asking" Works

The principle behind effective networking isn't complicated, but it's easy to forget under time pressure: people respond more positively to someone who first contributes something — an insight, a useful question, a shared article — than to someone who immediately asks for something. This isn't a manipulation technique; it is simply how reciprocity works in professional relationships.

## Comparison Table: Passive vs. Active Visibility

| Approach | Time investment | Effect in the medium term |
|---|---|---|
| Only applying to vacancies | Low per application | Limited to what is publicly advertised |
| Attending one networking event | Moderate | Limited, unless followed up |
| Being consistently, substantively visible over months | Moderate, spread over time | Access to opportunities never widely advertised |

## Why the Dutch AI Scene Is Smaller and More Close-Knit Than You'd Think

The Dutch AI community is relatively small compared with, for example, the United States, which means consistent visibility produces tangible results faster here than in a larger, more anonymous market. Someone who regularly shares relevant content or attends the same meetup for three months is recognised sooner in a market of this size.

## Where the Dutch AI Community Actually Gathers

Advice about networking often stays abstract because it doesn't name where the community is. For the Dutch AI scene, that map can be described fairly concretely.

**Meetups.** Meetups are the most accessible entry point and cluster around the larger cities, with the highest density in Amsterdam and Utrecht and a strong second layer in Eindhoven, Rotterdam and Groningen. **PyData Amsterdam** and **PyData Eindhoven** are well-known examples, bringing together practitioners who work with Python, data and machine learning; PyData Amsterdam also organises a larger annual conference. The **MLOps Community** has local chapters and online events for people working on bringing models into production. Attending the same series two or three times usually yields more than attending five different ones once.

**Conferences.** **World Summit AI**, held in Amsterdam, attracts a large international audience from industry and research. **BNAIC**, the Benelux Conference on Artificial Intelligence, is the long-running academic conference of the Benelux AI research community and a good place to meet researchers and PhD candidates. Specialist conferences in areas such as computer vision, NLP or healthcare AI bring together a considerable share of the relevant community in a single event — particularly valuable for the specialised niches discussed elsewhere on this site.

**Ecosystem organisations.** The **Netherlands AI Coalition (NL AIC)**, a public-private partnership, organises working groups and events on AI adoption across sectors. **ICAI**, the Innovation Center for Artificial Intelligence, is a national network of AI labs in which knowledge institutions, companies and government collaborate — labs that often host events and attract PhD candidates who later move into industry.

**Communities focused on inclusion.** **Women in AI Netherlands**, the Dutch chapter of an international non-profit, runs events and programmes aimed at increasing diversity in AI, and is open to allies as well.

**Online communities.** Online communities are the lowest-threshold entry point for many, especially for people outside the Randstad. **LinkedIn** remains the dominant professional platform in the Netherlands, while open-source platforms such as **Hugging Face** and GitHub let you become visible through contributions rather than posts. The pattern that works is the same: consistent, substantive presence over months easily beats a single introduction post.

## How Visibility Leads to Opportunities in Practice

Visibility rarely leads to a direct job offer. What it does is make sure your name comes up at the moment a concrete need arises — a team lead hearing there's budget for an extra position, a colleague being asked whether they know anyone, a former colleague building a team at a new organisation. In each of these situations, it isn't about who has the best CV, but about who comes to mind first.

That explains why consistency matters more than intensity. Someone who shares something substantive regularly for two years comes to mind in far more of these moments than someone who networks intensively for three months during an active search and then disappears. It's also why networking is most valuable when you don't need it, and least effective when you do.

## What You Can Share When You Don't Have Much Experience Yet

A clear account of something you learned, written for someone who doesn't know it yet, is valuable regardless of experience level — often more valuable when written by someone who recently understood the subject, because that person still remembers the confusion an expert has long forgotten. The same goes for an honest account of something that didn't work and why.

Asking a well-formulated question is also a form of contribution. A specific, thoughtful question that makes others think is more valuable to a community than a generic contribution that adds nothing. What doesn't work is waiting until you feel you have enough authority; for most people that feeling never comes.

## Networking Outside the Randstad: A Different Pattern

For candidates outside the Randstad, networking works differently. The relevant community is smaller and more specialised: in a region such as Twente, Zeeland or the northern Netherlands, the number of people doing comparable work is small enough that you can get to know most of them within a year. Regional knowledge institutions and business associations play a bigger role as hubs, so connecting with a regional technical circle or events at a local university of applied sciences or university often yields more than travelling to an Amsterdam meetup. Online visibility also weighs relatively more heavily where the physical community is thinner.

## What to Do If Networking Genuinely Puts You Off

The most effective route for people who find social interaction draining is to decouple visibility from interaction entirely. Writing and publishing something — an explanation, a write-up, an analysis — requires no conversation but still reaches people and builds recognition.

A second route is contributing to shared work instead of conversations: a contribution to an open-source project, a shared dataset, a tool others can use. Such contributions speak for themselves and require no self-promotion. In both cases, the effect comes more slowly than with direct interaction, but it does come — and a route you actually sustain yields infinitely more than a theoretically more effective route you give up after two attempts.

## What You Build Over the Longer Term

A professional network built over years works as a source of information about the field that no public source matches: you hear which employers are good to work for, which technical approaches work in practice and which mainly sound good at conferences, and which developments have real impact in Dutch organisations.

The same network acts as a safety net you only appreciate when you need it — a reorganisation, an employer going bankrupt, a project unexpectedly stopped. And perhaps most underestimated: it makes the work itself more enjoyable. A field in which you know people and can discuss the problems you run into feels fundamentally different from one in which you work in isolation.

## Using LinkedIn Effectively in the Dutch Market

LinkedIn plays an unusually large role in the Dutch professional world, and a few habits make it far more useful for networking in AI:

- **Write for practitioners, not for recruiters.** A short post explaining how you solved a specific data problem, including what didn't work, attracts the attention of engineers and hiring managers far more than a list of certifications.
- **Comment substantively.** A thoughtful comment on someone else's post — adding a nuance, a question or a related experience — is often the easiest first contact, and it is visible to the author's network too.
- **Personalise connection requests.** One sentence referring to a shared meetup, article or project makes acceptance much more likely than an empty request.
- **Use Dutch or English deliberately.** Many Dutch AI professionals post in English because the field is international; if you target regional employers or the public sector, occasional Dutch posts can widen your reach.
- **Keep your profile concrete.** Replace general claims such as "passionate about AI" with the problems you have worked on, the tools you use and the sectors you know.

## Following Up After a Meetup or Conference

The value of an event is mostly created afterwards. Within a few days, send a short message to one or two people you spoke with, referring to something specific from the conversation. If you promised to share an article or a piece of code, do it. If a talk was useful, a brief note to the speaker about what you took from it is almost always appreciated — speakers rarely hear concrete feedback. These small follow-ups turn a single encounter into the beginning of the recurring contact that makes networking work.

## A Practical Starting Point for the Coming Month

Choose one place where you want to be present in the coming months — a meetup in your region, an online professional community or a specific forum — instead of orienting yourself to several at once. Attend or participate substantively two or three times, with the sole aim of getting to know who comes and what the conversations are about.

In addition, write something once about a problem you recently solved or an insight you gained, and share it where it can reach relevant people — not as promotion, but as a contribution. These two steps cost a few hours spread over a month and set exactly the pattern described in this article in motion.

## Key Takeaways

- Consistent, substantive visibility works better than self-promotion and feels far less forced.
- Many interesting roles are filled through contacts and referrals before they are widely advertised.
- PyData Amsterdam and Eindhoven, the MLOps Community, World Summit AI, BNAIC, NL AIC, ICAI labs and Women in AI Netherlands are concrete places where the Dutch AI community gathers.
- Outside the Randstad, regional institutions and online communities weigh more heavily.
- If networking puts you off, build visibility through writing and open-source contributions instead.

## Where to Start

Choose one place — a meetup, an online community or a specific forum — and be consistently present there for months, instead of a little bit everywhere.
