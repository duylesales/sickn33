An AI hire who leaves after a year rarely cites salary as the primary reason in an honest exit conversation. More often, the reasons are structural — a sense that the work isn't going anywhere, unclear ownership over decisions, or a mismatch between what was promised at hiring and what the role actually became.

## The Structural Reasons That Actually Drive Departures

**A model that never reaches production.** Data scientists and ML engineers who spend a year on work that stays in a notebook, without ever shipping to real users, often leave for a role where their work has visible impact — this is one of the most consistent, avoidable reasons for departure.

**Scope drift without a conversation.** A role that gradually shifts from modeling toward dashboard maintenance or ad hoc reporting, without an explicit discussion about the change, erodes trust even when the shift happened for reasonable business reasons.

**No clear path to more ownership.** Engineers who start with a defined task and never get more autonomy over technical decisions, regardless of how their skill grows, eventually look elsewhere for that growth.

## Why This Requires More Than a Retention Bonus

A compensation increase addresses the wrong layer of the problem if the underlying issue is that someone's work never ships or their scope quietly narrowed — the departure risk returns once the bonus's effect wears off.

## Where to Start

Check directly, before it becomes a resignation conversation, whether a hire's actual work matches what was described at hiring, and whether there's a visible path for their work to reach production and their scope to grow.

## A Deeper Look at Why "Work That Never Ships" Is So Corrosive

For a data scientist or ML engineer, shipped work is the primary evidence — to themselves and to others — that their skill translates into real impact. A year of technically sound work that never reaches production isn't just professionally frustrating; it actively erodes a person's confidence in their own judgment, since they have no real feedback loop confirming whether their modeling choices were actually good ones. This is a deeper problem than simple boredom, and it explains why this specific failure mode drives departure so reliably across very different company types and cultures.

## A Practical Early-Warning Checklist for Managers

| Question to ask yourself about a report | Warning sign if the answer is concerning |
|---|---|
| When did their most recent piece of work actually reach production or real use? | If it's been many months, investigate why |
| Has their actual scope shifted from what was described at hiring? | If yes, has that been discussed openly with them? |
| Do they have more autonomy now than six months ago? | If no, and they're capable of more, that's a retention risk |

## Why Addressing This Requires Structural Change, Not Just a Conversation

A single check-in conversation can surface these issues, but resolving them usually requires changing something structural — prioritizing getting existing work to production before starting new work, or explicitly renegotiating scope rather than letting it drift silently. Treating this purely as a communication problem, without addressing the underlying structural cause, tends to produce a temporary morale boost followed by the same departure risk resurfacing months later.

## Why Shipping Matters More Here Than in Adjacent Roles

The observation that unshipped work drives departures deserves deeper examination, because it operates differently for data and AI roles than for software engineering roles generally.

A software engineer whose feature is delayed still has the satisfaction of a working artefact — the code exists, it functions, its quality is visible to peers. A data scientist whose model never deploys has an analysis nobody acts on and a notebook nobody runs. The work's entire value was contingent on adoption, and without it, a year of effort produces nothing the person can point to, either internally or in a future job search.

This creates a specific and consequential feedback problem. Without deployment, the practitioner never learns whether their modelling choices were sound, because the only real test is contact with the world. A year of unvalidated work is not merely unsatisfying; it is professionally stagnating in a way that a year of shipped but imperfect work is not.

Organisations that understand this manage the deployment pipeline as a retention issue rather than purely as a delivery issue. Ensuring that something reaches production regularly, even something modest, addresses a need that no amount of interesting technical work in isolation can substitute for.

## The Scope Drift Problem and How to Manage It

Scope drift — the gradual shift from the work described at hiring toward something different — is among the most common and most manageable retention risks, and its management is largely a communication problem.

Drift is frequently legitimate. Business priorities change, an urgent reporting need appears, a colleague leaves and their responsibilities need distributing. The problem is rarely that the shift occurred; it is that it occurred without anyone naming it, so that the practitioner experiences a slow divergence between what they were promised and what they do, with no moment at which that divergence was acknowledged.

The management approach is straightforward but requires deliberate attention. When priorities shift in a way that changes someone's actual work, saying so explicitly — acknowledging the change, explaining the reason, and discussing whether it is temporary or permanent — converts a silent erosion of trust into a legitimate business conversation that most people accept readily.

Where drift is permanent and substantial, the honest conversation is about whether the role has effectively become a different job, and whether that job is one the person wants. Some will say yes; some will say no. Both answers are more useful, and arrive earlier, than the resignation that silent drift eventually produces.

## Growth Paths in Small and Mid-Sized Organisations

The third structural driver — no clear path to more ownership — presents a genuine difficulty for the many Dutch employers described throughout this site who have small data functions and limited hierarchical room.

The reflexive assumption is that growth requires promotion, and in an organisation with two data staff there is nowhere to promote anyone. This framing understates the options considerably. Growth in technical roles frequently means expanding scope rather than ascending a hierarchy: ownership of an additional system, responsibility for a decision previously made elsewhere, technical leadership of a project involving other functions, or becoming the organisation's recognised expert on a domain that matters.

These forms of growth are available in small organisations and are frequently more valuable to a practitioner's actual development than a title change would be. What they require is that someone name them explicitly as growth, because a practitioner who receives expanded scope without acknowledgement experiences it as more work rather than as advancement.

Where genuine growth is not available — where the role has reached its ceiling and the organisation cannot offer more — the honest position is to say so. Practitioners frequently stay longer at organisations that are candid about limits than at organisations that imply possibilities that never materialise, because the former allows planning while the latter produces a slow accumulation of disappointment.

## What Exit Conversations Actually Reveal

Organisations conducting exit interviews frequently gather less useful information than they could, because departing employees have limited incentive to be fully candid and considerable incentive to leave gracefully.

The more informative conversations happen earlier, with people who are not leaving. A structured, genuinely open discussion with a practitioner at the twelve-month mark — asking whether the work matches expectations, whether they can see a path forward, whether anything is quietly frustrating them — surfaces the same information at a point where it can still be acted upon.

Where exit conversations do occur, the useful question is not why the person is leaving, which typically produces a diplomatic answer about a new opportunity, but what would have had to be different for them to have stayed. This question is easier to answer honestly and frequently produces specific, actionable information.

Patterns across multiple departures matter more than individual cases. If three people over two years have cited variations of work not reaching production, that is a structural finding about the organisation rather than three individual preferences, and it warrants a structural response.

## Balancing Retention Against Healthy Turnover

A complete treatment should note that maximising retention is not the correct objective, and organisations that pursue it single-mindedly create their own problems.

Some turnover is healthy and expected. A practitioner who has genuinely outgrown what an organisation can offer benefits from moving on, and attempting to retain them through inducements that do not address the underlying limit typically delays rather than prevents the departure while accumulating resentment.

The professional community described throughout this site is small enough that people who leave well remain valuable contacts, occasional collaborators and sometimes returning employees. Organisations that handle departures gracefully — supporting the transition, staying in contact, welcoming the possibility of return — frequently find that a departure is less final than it appears.

The objective worth pursuing is therefore not zero attrition but the absence of avoidable attrition: ensuring nobody leaves because their work never shipped, because their role changed silently, or because a growth conversation never happened. Departures for reasons the organisation genuinely could not address are a normal cost of employing capable people.

## A Practical Diagnostic for Managers

For a manager who wants to assess retention risk across their team without conducting a formal process, a small set of questions answered honestly about each person provides a reasonable diagnostic.

When did this person last have work reach production or real use? If the answer is more than several months, that is the single strongest predictor discussed in this article, and it warrants attention regardless of how engaged the person appears.

Does their current work match what was described when they joined? If it has drifted, has that drift ever been named explicitly in a conversation, or has it simply happened?

Has their scope or autonomy increased in the past year? If not, and if they are capable of more, the growth conversation is overdue.

Could they articulate what they are working toward professionally, and does the organisation's plan for them align with it? A manager who cannot answer this on behalf of a team member has not had the conversation recently enough.

Running this diagnostic quarterly takes a manager perhaps twenty minutes for a small team and surfaces the specific, addressable issues that this article identifies as driving avoidable departures — considerably earlier than the resignation conversation that would otherwise be the first signal.

## What Practitioners Can Do From Their Own Side

Although this article addresses employers primarily, practitioners who recognise these patterns in their own situation have options short of resigning, and it is worth naming them.

If your work is not reaching production, raising this directly as a concern about impact rather than as a personal complaint frequently produces a constructive response, because managers are often unaware of how the pattern looks from the practitioner's side and generally share the underlying concern once it is named.

If your scope has drifted, asking for an explicit conversation about what the role is now — rather than absorbing the change silently — creates the acknowledgement that its absence makes corrosive, and frequently surfaces that the drift was temporary or reversible.

If growth has stalled, proposing a specific expansion of scope rather than asking generally about progression gives a manager something concrete to say yes to, which is considerably easier than responding to an open question about career development.

In each case, the conversation is worth having before the decision to leave has been made, because these problems are frequently addressable and rarely addressed until someone names them.
