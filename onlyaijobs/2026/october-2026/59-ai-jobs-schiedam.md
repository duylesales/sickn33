Schiedam has a long tradition of distilleries and food production, and that tradition creates its own kind of data work — quality control and process optimisation in production environments — which differs in substance from the port-related logistics Rotterdam is known for, despite the short distance between them.

## What Kind of Data Work Actually Happens Here

Quality control in food and beverage production requires statistical modelling with direct consequences for product consistency and food safety — different work from the logistics optimisation that characterises the Port of Rotterdam, even though both are right next to each other.

## Why This Work Is Less Visible

Food and beverage companies rarely compete for visibility on general job platforms, and a candidate searching for "AI Rotterdam" often automatically focuses on the port instead of this adjacent production sector.

## Schiedam's Named Distilling Heritage

Schiedam became world-famous for **jenever**, the Dutch juniper-flavoured spirit, from which gin later developed. At its peak, the city had hundreds of distilleries and malt houses, and the tallest traditional windmills in the world still stand along its canals, originally used to grind grain for distilling. The **National Jenever Museum** in Schiedam tells this history.

Jenever is protected in the EU as a geographical indication under the **EU Spirit Drinks Regulation (EU) 2019/787**, which sets rules on production, labelling and origin. For producers, that means documented processes and traceability — data requirements rooted in centuries-old craft.

**Nolet Distillery.** A family-owned distillery in Schiedam with a history spanning many generations, producing, among others, well-known vodka and gin brands sold internationally.

**De Kuyper Royal Distillers.** A family-owned distiller of liqueurs and spirits with its roots and main production in Schiedam.

Distilling at international scale combines craft with industrial process control: consistent flavour profiles across batches, precise blending, quality testing and supply chain planning for global markets.

## The Wider Employer Base

**Healthcare insurance.** **DSW Zorgverzekeraar**, a Dutch health insurer, has its head office in Schiedam. Health insurance involves claims data, fraud detection, cost forecasting and customer analytics — a completely different data domain within the same city.

**Hospital care.** **Franciscus Vlietland**, a hospital in Schiedam, adds healthcare data work around capacity, quality and patient flows.

**Port and logistics.** Schiedam's own harbour areas along the Nieuwe Maas and its position next to Rotterdam give it logistics and maritime service companies.

**Food production and suppliers.** Beyond distilleries, food producers, ingredient suppliers and packaging companies in the region share quality-control challenges.

## A Deeper Look at What Distinguishes Quality Control in Beverages and Food

Distilleries and food producers work with processes where consistency between batches is essential — a small deviation in a production process can make an entire batch unsaleable. This kind of quality control requires statistical models that don't just measure average performance, but detect outliers and deviations early, often with stricter tolerance than less regulated product categories.

## Where Data and Machine Learning Are Applied

| Application | Typical data | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| Batch consistency monitoring | Alcohol content, temperatures, fermentation and distillation parameters | Statistical process control, anomaly detection |
| Flavour and blend optimisation | Chemical analyses, sensory panel scores, recipes | Regression, mixed models for panel data, optimisation |
| Raw material variability | Grain, botanicals and ingredient quality data | Multivariate analysis, spectroscopy models |

| Packaging line efficiency | Line speeds, stoppages, reject rates | Overall equipment effectiveness analytics, predictive maintenance |

**Sensory data** is a speciality in beverages. Taste panels score aroma, flavour and mouthfeel, but panellists differ in sensitivity and use scales differently. Modelling panel data correctly — accounting for differences between panellists and sessions — is a niche skill that combines statistics with product knowledge.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

- **EU Spirit Drinks Regulation (EU) 2019/787.** Defines categories, production requirements and geographical indications for spirits, including jenever.
- **Excise duties.** Alcoholic beverages are subject to excise duty, administered by the **Dutch Tax Administration**. Producers often store products in bonded tax warehouses, where accurate inventory records are a legal requirement.
- **Dutch Alcohol Act (Alcoholwet).** Regulates sale and marketing of alcohol, including age limits, which affects commercial and marketing analytics.
- **EU Food Hygiene Regulation (EC) No 852/2004.** Requires food businesses to apply HACCP-based food safety procedures.
- **Food information and labelling.** Labelling requirements for ingredients, allergens and alcohol content must remain correct when recipes change.

## A Concrete Scenario: Keeping a Blend Consistent

A distiller sells a spirit whose flavour profile must be identical in every bottle, year after year, while natural raw materials vary from harvest to harvest. A data scientist combines chemical analyses of incoming botanicals and grain spirit with historical sensory panel scores to predict how each batch will taste after blending. The model suggests blend adjustments before the product is bottled, and the master distiller makes the final decision based on tasting. The combination of data and craft reduces the number of batches needing rework and makes the process less dependent on the experience of a few individuals — without replacing their expertise.

## Beyond Distilling: Health Insurance Data in the Same City

DSW Zorgverzekeraar's presence gives Schiedam a data domain that has nothing to do with food or drink. Dutch health insurers operate under the Health Insurance Act, which requires everyone living or working in the Netherlands to have basic health insurance and prohibits insurers from refusing applicants for the basic package. Within that system, insurers work with large volumes of claims data to process declarations, detect incorrect or fraudulent billing, forecast healthcare costs, purchase care from providers and serve customers.

For data scientists, this means work with sensitive health-related data under strict GDPR requirements, where explainability and fairness matter as much as predictive accuracy. Combined with the distilleries, food producers, hospital and logistics companies, it makes Schiedam a surprisingly diverse labour market for a city of its size.

## Why Heritage Brands Invest in Data

Family-owned distillers with centuries of history might seem unlikely data employers, but international competition changes that. Selling premium spirits in dozens of countries requires accurate demand forecasting per market, efficient packaging lines, consistent quality under varying raw materials and compliance with excise and labelling rules in every destination country. Data work doesn't replace the craft that defines these brands — it protects it, by making production more consistent and less dependent on a few individuals' memory.
