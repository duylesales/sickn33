Many AI professionals compare freelancing (in Dutch, working as a *zzp'er*) with a permanent contract purely on a net rate calculation. That's an incomplete comparison — Dutch law and regulation around self-employment, including enforcement against false self-employment, has practical consequences beyond what you can work out on a calculator.

## What We Can and Can't Say Here

🟥 **Important**: this article doesn't give tax or legal advice, and deliberately names no specific amounts, percentages or thresholds — they change regularly and depend on your personal situation. For concrete figures and the current state of the law, an accountant or tax adviser is the right source, not a blog article.

## What Is True, in General Terms

**The nature of the assignment helps determine whether freelancing fits.** Working under authority, fixed working hours and a single client over a long period can be assessed legally differently from an independent assignment with your own control — this touches the core of how false self-employment is assessed.

**Risk and security shift; they don't disappear.** A permanent contract gives dismissal protection and continued pay during illness; freelancing gives flexibility but shifts those risks to you. Neither is "safer" in absolute tax or legal terms — it depends on your situation.

**This choice deserves periodic review.** What is correct and sensible at the start of an assignment can change as the assignment lasts longer or changes in character.

## The Named Legal Framework, Without the Numbers

Although this article gives no figures, it helps to know which rules and organisations are involved, so you can ask your adviser the right questions.

**Deregulation Assessment of Employment Relationships Act (Wet DBA).** The act that replaced the old declaration-of-independence system in 2016. Under it, the **Dutch Tax Administration** assesses whether a working relationship is in fact employment, based on actual circumstances. For years, enforcement was largely suspended; the Tax Administration announced that it would resume enforcement from 1 January 2025, which has made both clients and freelancers more cautious.

**Model agreements.** Under the DBA Act, the Tax Administration has assessed model agreements that parties can use as a starting point. A model agreement only offers certainty if the work is actually carried out as described — paper alone doesn't decide.

**Case law.** In 2023, the **Supreme Court of the Netherlands** ruled in a case about Deliveroo meal delivery riders that they worked under employment contracts, and it set out the factors that must be weighed in assessing a working relationship — including whether the work is embedded in the client's organisation and whether the worker operates as an entrepreneur. This ruling is widely referenced in assessing freelance relationships, including in IT and data.

**Legislative developments.** The Dutch government has been working on legislation to clarify the assessment of working relationships and to introduce a legal presumption of employment below a certain hourly rate, as well as plans for mandatory disability insurance for the self-employed. The timing and content of such proposals can change, which is exactly why current advice matters.

**Registration and tax status.** Freelancers usually register their business with the **Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK)**. Whether you are regarded as an entrepreneur for income tax purposes — which gives access to certain entrepreneur's allowances — is assessed by the Tax Administration based on criteria such as independence, risk and time spent. VAT registration and possible use of the small businesses scheme (KOR) are further topics.

**Social security.** Employees are insured through their employer for unemployment and disability under schemes administered by **UWV**. Freelancers are generally not covered by these employee insurance schemes and must arrange their own provisions.

## Why This Topic Doesn't Lend Itself to a Simple Step-by-Step Plan

The legal assessment of self-employment depends on factual circumstances per assignment — how much control a client has, how the fee is structured, whether there is a lasting, exclusive relationship with one client — and that assessment also shifts with changing regulation. An article written at one point in time can therefore never give a current, reliable answer to a question that inherently depends on your specific, current situation.

## What You Can Prepare Yourself for a Conversation With an Adviser

**An overview of your current and expected assignments**, including how much control the client has over how and when you work.

**A realistic picture of your risk tolerance**, apart from the tax details — how much financial uncertainty can you actually bear without it affecting your work or wellbeing?

**Concrete questions for your accountant or tax adviser**, instead of a vague "what's better" — for example: "does my current assignment meet the criteria for self-employment as they currently apply?"

## Which Questions to Ask an Adviser

First, ask how the nature of your specific assignments is assessed in light of current regulation around self-employment. Describe concretely how you work: who determines your working hours, who determines how you do the work, whether you work for one client or several, whether you use your own equipment, and how long the relationship has lasted.

Next, ask which administrative obligations apply in your situation and which you can handle yourself versus which justify outsourcing. For many new freelancers, this is an underestimated cost that noticeably affects the net outcome of the freelance route.

Third, ask explicitly about the risks you will carry and which provisions exist — disability, pension accrual, buffers for periods without assignments. These topics are often skipped in conversations about rates, while in the longer term they weigh more heavily than the difference between two hourly rates.

## How This Choice Relates to the Type of Work You Do

Work heavily dependent on access to company-specific data and systems — most applied data work — usually requires long, deep involvement with one organisation. That makes short, well-defined assignments less practical and pushes the relationship towards something that, in factual circumstances, resembles employment, with all the assessment questions that come with it.

Work that is more project-based — a defined analysis, setting up a specific system, a second opinion on an existing approach — fits a self-employed arrangement structurally better, because the start and end are clear and the client purchases the result rather than your labour. The arrangement should follow from the work, not the other way round.

## What Changes the Longer You Are Self-Employed

In the early phase, uncertainty about the flow of assignments dominates, and the value of a permanent contract is correspondingly greater. As a freelancer builds a network and reputation and the flow of assignments becomes more predictable, the balance shifts: acquisition effort decreases while flexibility remains.

At the same time, other considerations grow in weight. Pension accrual, which seems easy to postpone in the first years, becomes an increasingly significant issue over time. The same goes for disability risks, which statistically increase with age. This choice therefore deserves periodic reconsideration, and a conversation with an adviser is relevant not only at the start but regularly throughout the years you work independently.

## Why Clients Have Their Own Interests Here

Clients bear risk themselves when a collaboration is later assessed differently than both parties assumed. That makes some organisations reluctant to enter long-term freelance relationships, or leads them to prefer certain arrangements — for example working through intermediaries or preferring employment contracts for long-term roles. When a client proposes a particular form, it isn't necessarily a negotiating position but often a reflection of their own risk policy. Asking how the organisation usually handles this positions you as someone who takes the topic seriously.

## What This Means for How You Evaluate an Assignment

The contract form should be part of evaluating an assignment, not something arranged afterwards. For each assignment, assess how the actual way of working will look: how much autonomy you have over how and when you work, to what extent you work within the client's structures, and how long the expected duration is. It is much easier to get clarity on this beforehand than to restructure an ongoing collaboration afterwards.

## How This Trade-Off Differs by AI Specialisation

For specialisations where work can be defined as projects — an evaluation process, setting up a specific system, a targeted analysis — freelancing is practically feasible, and there is real demand for external expertise on call, for example in AI governance, model evaluation and specialised modelling questions.

For specialisations deeply intertwined with one organisation's operational systems and domain knowledge — industrial process data work, quality control in production, long-running research — freelancing is considerably harder to sustain, because the ramp-up time before you are productive is too long to spread over short assignments.

## Why This Topic Deserves More Attention Than It Usually Gets

Part of the explanation for why this topic stays underexposed is that it seems dull compared with the substantive questions of the field. Another part is that it touches uncertainty and risks people would rather not make explicit. The result is that many professionals make a choice with major financial consequences based on a superficial comparison and don't revisit it for years. For a profession that values careful analysis in its own work, deliberately breaking that pattern — by getting good advice once and reviewing the choice periodically — is one of the higher-return time investments in a career.

## A Practical Checklist Before Signing a Freelance Assignment

Before you accept an assignment as a freelancer, go through these points — and bring the answers to your adviser:

1. **Who decides how the work is done?** If the client determines not only the result but also your methods, working hours and daily priorities, that points towards employment.
2. **Is the work embedded in the client's organisation?** Doing the same work as the client's own employees, in the same team structure, is a relevant factor in the assessment.
3. **Do you operate as an entrepreneur?** Multiple clients, your own website, your own equipment, investment in your business and bearing commercial risk all support independence.
4. **How is the fee structured?** A fixed monthly amount regardless of results resembles salary more than a project fee or a rate for defined deliverables.
5. **What happens at the end?** Clear deliverables and an end date support a project relationship; an open-ended assignment extended repeatedly deserves periodic reassessment.
6. **Which contract is used?** Ask whether the client or intermediary uses a model agreement assessed by the Tax Administration, and check whether the practical situation matches it.
7. **How are your risks covered?** Arrange your own disability insurance, liability insurance and financial buffer before the assignment starts, not after.

## Key Takeaways

- A net rate comparison is not enough; the actual working relationship determines how freelancing is assessed.
- The DBA Act, resumed enforcement by the Tax Administration, model agreements and the Supreme Court's Deliveroo ruling form the current framework.
- Legislative proposals on assessing working relationships and disability insurance for the self-employed may change the picture — get current advice.
- KVK registration, entrepreneur status for income tax, VAT and missing employee insurance through UWV are practical topics to discuss with an adviser.
- Let the type of work determine the arrangement, and review the choice periodically.
