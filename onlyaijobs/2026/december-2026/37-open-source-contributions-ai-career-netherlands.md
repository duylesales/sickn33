Ask a Dutch hiring manager what they actually read when assessing a technical candidate, and a public repository will be near the top of the list — above certificates, above the summary paragraph, often above the degree. Code is evidence in a way that claims are not. But contributing to open source while employed raises questions about ownership, confidentiality and employer policy that many candidates have never thought about until a lawyer asks them.

This article is general information, not legal advice; contracts differ and specific situations need individual review.

## Why Public Code Carries So Much Weight Here

Dutch technical hiring is relatively pragmatic. Interviews tend to probe judgement and collaboration rather than competitive programming, and teams are small enough that a bad hire is felt immediately. In that context, a repository answers questions an interview struggles to reach.

It shows how you structure a project, whether you write tests, whether your commits tell a coherent story, how you respond to review comments, and whether your documentation lets a stranger run your work. Those are precisely the qualities that determine whether you will be pleasant and effective to work with.

It also functions as a portability mechanism. Most of what you do at work is invisible outside your employer. Public contributions are the part of your professional record that belongs to you, which matters in a small market where reputation travels.

## What Dutch Law Says About Code You Write

Two principles matter, and they interact.

**Works created in employment.** Under Dutch copyright law, where creating works of a certain kind is part of the duties an employee is hired for, the employer is generally regarded as the author of works produced in that role. In practice, code you write as part of your job belongs to your employer.

**Contractual clauses.** Employment contracts typically go further, with intellectual property clauses that assign rights in work related to the employer's business, confidentiality obligations that survive the employment, and sometimes non-compete or ancillary activity clauses requiring permission for outside work.

The grey area — and it is genuinely grey — is a side project developed in your own time, on your own equipment, unrelated to your employer's business. A narrowly drafted clause leaves that clearly yours; a broadly drafted one may not. The practical resolutions are the same whichever side of the line you think you are on: read the clause before signing, ask for it to be narrowed if it is sweeping, and get written permission for a specific project rather than relying on interpretation.

## Employer Policies Vary More Than You Expect

**Actively supportive.** Some Dutch employers maintain open source programmes, allow work time for contributions to projects they depend on, and publish their own tools. These employers are usually explicit about it, and it is a genuine benefit worth weighing in an offer.

**Permissive but unstructured.** Most common: nobody objects, but there is no policy, which means approval depends on your manager and could change.

**Restrictive.** Financial institutions, defence contractors, healthcare organisations and some public bodies restrict what may be published, sometimes for good reasons — security, confidentiality, regulatory obligations.

**Silent.** The most dangerous category, because silence is not permission. If a dispute arises, an absent policy leaves you relying on the contract, which was drafted by the employer.

Ask during hiring. "What is your policy on contributing to open source?" is a normal question that also tells you something about the engineering culture.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Library That Came From Work

An engineer builds an internal utility that solves a common problem elegantly. It contains no business logic and no data, and colleagues suggest publishing it.

They ask, and discover the process is not trivial: legal review for third-party licence obligations in dependencies, a security review to confirm no credentials or internal hostnames are embedded, a decision about which licence to publish under, and a question about who maintains it afterwards — because publishing something and abandoning it can embarrass the company.

The approval takes six weeks and is granted. The library gets modest use, and two years later the engineer is contacted about a job because of it.

The instructive part is the process rather than the outcome. Publishing work code is usually possible and almost never spontaneous, and the engineers who succeed are the ones who ask early, anticipate the reviews and offer a maintenance plan.

## Choosing What to Contribute

Not all contributions carry equal weight, and the most common mistake is optimising for volume.

**Highest value:** sustained contributions to a project other people use — bug fixes, documentation improvements, review participation, a feature discussed and merged. This demonstrates collaboration, which is what employers actually worry about.

**High value:** your own project that solves a real problem, with a clear README, tests and an honest statement of limitations. One such project beats ten abandoned repositories.

**Moderate value:** reproductions of papers, well-documented analyses, teaching material. These show understanding and communication.

**Low value:** tutorial follow-alongs, forked repositories with no changes, and streaks of trivial commits. Reviewers recognise these immediately, and they can count against you by implying you do not know what substantive work looks like.

A quiet profile with three real contributions is stronger than a busy one with none.

## Documentation Is the Underrated Signal

Almost every experienced reviewer says the same thing: they look at the README first.

A project whose documentation states what problem it solves, how to run it, what assumptions it makes and what it does not handle tells a reviewer that you think about the person on the other side. That is the single most predictive quality for whether someone will be effective in a team, because most professional work is handing things to other people.

Conversely, a technically impressive project with no documentation reads as someone who builds for themselves. In a Dutch team of six, that is a genuine concern.

If you have limited time to improve your public profile before a job search, improving documentation on what you already have gives a better return than starting something new.

## Contributing Through Work, Research and Community

Open source is not only individual evening work, and in the Netherlands there are several well-trodden routes.

**Research software.** Universities and institutes increasingly employ research software engineers and expect code accompanying publications to be public and reusable. This is a real career path, and contributions made there are unambiguously publishable.

**Public sector code.** Dutch government organisations have moved towards publishing source code for public services under open source principles, which has created work and a growing body of public repositories.

**Company-maintained projects.** Several Dutch employers maintain open tools in data engineering, geospatial and scientific computing. Joining such a team means contributing publicly as part of your job.

**Community involvement.** Meetups, user groups and conferences in the Netherlands are small and accessible. Helping organise, presenting, or maintaining a community resource builds the same reputation with less time pressure than code contribution.

## What Is Changing: Supply Chain Responsibility

A shift worth knowing about for both career and legal reasons: European product legislation is tightening expectations around software supply chains, including obligations on manufacturers relating to the components they incorporate and to vulnerability handling.

The practical consequences reach ordinary developers. Employers increasingly need to know which open source components they depend on, produce bills of materials, and respond to vulnerabilities in a defined way. Contributing to a project that a company depends on becomes more valuable to that company, and maintainers of widely used projects carry expectations they did not sign up for.

For candidates, this creates a marketable competence: understanding licences, dependency risk and supply chain documentation is a scarce skill that sits between engineering and compliance, and it is increasingly asked about in interviews at product companies.

## How Reviewers Actually Read a Repository

It helps to know what happens when a hiring manager opens your profile, because the sequence is fairly consistent and takes about four minutes.

They look first at whether anything is recent, then open the project whose name sounds most relevant to the role. They read the README. If it explains the problem clearly, they scan the file structure to see whether the project is organised or a single enormous script. They open one or two files and read the code the way they would review a colleague's work: naming, structure, comments where the logic is non-obvious.

Then they look at the commit history. Not for volume, but for shape: do commits do one thing, do the messages explain why, is there evidence of iteration and correction. Finally, if the project has issues or pull requests, they read your responses to other people, because that shows how you behave when someone disagrees with you.

Three practical conclusions follow. Put your best work where it will be found — pin it, or name it clearly. Make sure at least one project is recent. And never leave a half-finished experiment as your most prominent repository, because it will be read as representative.

## Licences: The Part Engineers Skip

Licence literacy is a small investment with an outsized professional return, and most engineers avoid the subject until it causes a problem.

The essential distinction is between permissive licences, which broadly allow reuse including in closed products with attribution, and copyleft licences, which require derived works to be distributed under the same terms. Both are legitimate; the consequences differ sharply depending on how the code is used.

The workplace relevance is immediate. Incorporating a copyleft-licensed component into a product you distribute can create obligations your employer never intended. Many companies therefore maintain approved and prohibited licence lists, and an engineer who knows to check before adding a dependency prevents expensive conversations later.

For your own projects, choosing a licence deliberately matters too: publishing without one leaves others legally unable to use your work, which defeats the purpose of publishing it.

Being the person on a team who understands this is a small, cheap differentiator — and with supply chain expectations tightening, it is increasingly asked about in interviews.

## Key Takeaways

- Public code is among the strongest evidence in Dutch technical hiring, above certificates and often above the CV summary.
- Code written as part of your job generally belongs to your employer; side projects depend on how the contract's IP clause is drafted.
- Employer policies range from active programmes to outright restriction — ask during hiring rather than assuming.
- Sustained contributions to a project people use beat volume, and documentation is the most predictive signal reviewers look at.
- Supply chain legislation is making open source dependency knowledge a marketable professional skill.

## Where to Start

Pick one project you actually use, fix one real issue, and write the documentation you wish had existed. That single contribution will do more for your applications than a year of tutorials.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employee with a side project) Does my employer own my evening code?
Work done as part of your job generally belongs to the employer; side projects depend on your contract's IP and ancillary activity clauses, so read them.

### (Scenario: candidate with a sparse profile) Do I need a large GitHub presence to be hired?
No — most Dutch employers do not require it, and three substantive contributions are worth more than many trivial ones.

### (Scenario: engineer wanting to publish work code) Is that usually allowed?
Often yes, after legal and security review and a decision on licensing and maintenance; ask early rather than publishing first.

### (Scenario: candidate in finance or healthcare) Why does my employer restrict contributions?
Confidentiality, security and regulatory obligations; in those sectors restrictions are common and usually non-negotiable.

### (Scenario: candidate short on time) What improves my profile fastest?
Better documentation on projects you already have, which is the first thing reviewers read and the strongest signal of teamwork.
