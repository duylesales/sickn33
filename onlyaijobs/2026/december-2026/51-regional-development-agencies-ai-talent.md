Every Dutch region has an organisation whose job is to strengthen its economy: a regional development company, working alongside the province, the municipalities, the knowledge institutions and the employers. They co-invest, they run innovation programmes, they attract foreign companies, and they convene. What almost none of them own explicitly is the question that determines whether any of it works: can the companies they support actually find the people they need?

## The Gap Nobody Owns

A development agency co-finances a project to develop a new industrial vision system. The technology works. The company then needs two machine learning engineers to take it to market, searches for eight months, and hires a consultancy instead — knowledge that leaves when the contract ends. The project is recorded as a success in the funding report and delivers a fraction of the intended regional benefit.

This pattern repeats across the Netherlands, and it falls between the responsibilities of the organisations involved.

The development agency's mandate is investment and innovation, not recruitment. The municipality's economic affairs department works on business climate, premises and permits. The province funds programmes at a level of abstraction well above individual vacancies. The employment service focuses on people currently out of work rather than on scarce specialists. The universities supply graduates into a national market and do not track where they go.

The result is a regional innovation system in which money, facilities and knowledge are actively coordinated, and labour supply is left to individual employers competing alone against nationally visible brands.

## Why Regional AI Talent Is Not a Free Market

Three market failures make this a legitimate object of regional policy rather than something to leave to employers.

**Information asymmetry.** A data professional in Utrecht does not know that a machine builder in Twente, a food processor in Friesland or a hospital in Zeeland has interesting work. Those employers are invisible in the channels candidates use, and the vacancies are titled in ways that do not match how candidates search.

**Coordination failure.** One company cannot justify building regional visibility alone; twenty companies together easily can. This is a textbook case for a convening organisation.

**Sunk-cost geography.** People do not relocate freely. Housing, partners, schools and family anchor them, which means regional supply and demand can be persistently mismatched even in a country this small. Someone commuting ninety minutes each way is not a functioning market outcome.

Where a market fails for these reasons, the standard regional policy response is to build shared infrastructure. Talent visibility is infrastructure of exactly this kind — and it is considerably cheaper than the physical infrastructure these organisations routinely fund.

## What a Development Agency Can Actually Do

Six interventions, roughly in order of cost.

**Make regional demand visible in one place.** Aggregate and publish the technical vacancies of supported companies, as a regional dataset rather than as individual postings. Candidates cannot evaluate a region they cannot see.

**Improve how vacancies are written.** A large share of regional technical vacancies are invisible to search because they use internal job titles. A short guide and a review service costs almost nothing and materially improves reach.

**Target returnees deliberately.** People who grew up in the region, left for study or work, and would consider coming back are the highest-conversion audience available, and they can be reached through alumni networks, regional media and family connections.

**Build employer collectives.** Twenty companies sharing a recruitment presence, a graduate programme or a traineeship reach candidates none could reach alone, and give a candidate the assurance that a second employer exists if the first does not work out — which is the unspoken concern about regional moves.

**Connect education to employers systematically.** Graduation projects, internships and applied research placements are the most reliable regional talent pipeline and the least systematically organised.

**Include talent in funding conditions.** Where an agency co-finances a project, asking how the company will staff it — and offering support with that — turns an afterthought into a plan.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Programme That Funded Its Own Bottleneck

A region launches a programme to support AI adoption among manufacturing SMEs. Twelve companies participate, each receiving support for a pilot project. Ten pilots succeed technically.

At the eighteen-month evaluation, only three companies have scaled their pilot into production. The interviews reveal a consistent reason: they could not hire anyone to maintain and extend the system, and the external partner who built the pilot was too expensive to retain.

The programme is judged a partial success. A follow-up programme is designed with more funding for pilots.

What the region needed was not more pilots. It was a shared traineeship placing graduates into those twelve companies with a support structure around them, at a fraction of the cost of the pilot funding. The instrument existed; nobody had connected the two problems, because the pilot budget and the labour market budget sat in different organisations with different reporting lines.

## Measuring the Right Things

Regional talent initiatives frequently fail to prove their value because they measure activity rather than outcome. More useful indicators:

**Vacancy duration** for technical roles among supported companies, tracked over time. This is the cleanest available measure of whether the labour market is working.

**Fill rate and source.** How many technical vacancies are filled, and whether the person came from within the region, returned to it, or moved in.

**Retention at twelve months**, which distinguishes filling a vacancy from solving a problem.

**Student destinations.** What share of relevant graduates from regional institutions take their first job in the region.

**Commuting patterns.** The share of regional residents working in technical roles outside the region indicates latent supply that better visibility could capture.

Several of these can be assembled from existing statistics and from the agencies' own company relationships, without a new research programme.

## The Argument for Acting Now

Two trends make this more urgent than it was five years ago.

Hybrid working has permanently changed the geography of employment. A regional employer offering two office days can now recruit from a radius that includes several cities — but only if candidates know the employer exists. The constraint has shifted from physical proximity to visibility, which is precisely the constraint a regional organisation can address.

And the housing situation has made regional living materially more attractive relative to the Randstad. People who would not have considered a move out of the major cities are doing the arithmetic. Regions that are visible when that calculation happens will capture people; regions that are not will watch them move somewhere else.

Both trends favour the regions. Neither delivers anything automatically.

## Working With the Employers You Already Support

Development agencies have an asset that recruitment organisations would pay for: an existing, trusted relationship with hundreds of regional companies. Used deliberately, that relationship solves problems no individual employer can.

**Aggregate the demand picture.** Ask your portfolio companies twice a year which technical roles they are trying to fill and how long those vacancies have been open. Within two cycles you have something no other organisation in the region possesses: a picture of technical labour demand by discipline, by sub-region and over time. That dataset justifies interventions and attracts candidates by demonstrating that a market exists.

**Broker shared arrangements.** Two companies each needing half a data engineer can share one. Three companies can run a joint traineeship. Five can fund a part-time technical coach. These arrangements are obvious once suggested and almost never happen without a convening party, because no individual company will approach a competitor about it.

**Provide the assurance candidates need.** The unspoken objection to a regional move is: what if this job does not work out? A region that can show five other employers with similar work answers that objection in a way that one company never can.

**Help with the unglamorous parts.** Vacancy text review, interview structure, salary benchmarking against national ranges. Regional SMEs are frequently good employers with poor recruitment practice, and the improvement is cheap.

## Sub-Regional Reality: Where the Analysis Usually Goes Wrong

A common error in regional talent policy is treating a province as one labour market. It is not, and the mismatch produces initiatives that fail for geographic reasons nobody examined.

Commuting tolerance in the Netherlands is real but bounded: many people will travel thirty to forty-five minutes and far fewer will travel ninety. That means a province can contain two or three genuinely separate daily labour markets, with different employer mixes, different housing costs and different transport connections.

The practical implications are specific. An initiative based in the provincial capital may be irrelevant to companies an hour away. A traineeship requiring attendance at one location excludes participants from the far side of the province. A regional campaign aimed at returnees should name the towns people actually come from rather than the province, because identity attaches to places, not to administrative units.

The fix is to map the region as candidates experience it: travel times, rail connections, housing costs and where the employers actually sit. Several Dutch regions that did this discovered that their real labour market extends across a provincial border — into Germany, into another province, into a neighbouring region's city — and that some of their coordination effort was being spent on the wrong geography entirely.

## Key Takeaways

- Regional innovation funding routinely produces projects that cannot be staffed, and no organisation owns that gap.
- Information asymmetry, coordination failure and limited mobility make regional talent visibility a legitimate policy object rather than a private problem.
- Practical interventions run from publishing aggregated regional vacancies to employer collectives and talent conditions in funding.
- Measure vacancy duration, fill rate and source, twelve-month retention, graduate destinations and outbound commuting.
- Hybrid working and housing pressure have shifted the constraint from proximity to visibility, which regional organisations can act on.
