Somewhere between the final interview and the contract, a Dutch employer will often ask you for something you may not have encountered before: a certificate of conduct, a set of references with specific permissions, or in some sectors your consent to a full security investigation. Data and AI roles increasingly fall inside these procedures, because access to data is exactly what screening is designed to control.

This article is general information, not legal advice; procedures and criteria change and differ by employer and sector.

## The Levels of Screening You May Encounter

**Basic employment checks.** Verification of identity, right to work, diplomas and previous employment. Standard almost everywhere, and usually unremarkable.

**Certificate of Conduct.** Known as the VOG, this is a statement issued by the Ministry of Justice screening authority confirming that no judicial records were found that present an objection to a specific type of role. It is required by law for some professions and requested by many employers voluntarily, including in healthcare, education, childcare, finance and government.

**Sector integrity screening.** Financial institutions apply integrity and suitability requirements, particularly for roles with influence over risk, capital or customer outcomes. Screening here may include additional declarations and checks.

**Security investigation for a position of confidence.** Where a role gives access to state secrets or critical systems, it may be designated a *vertrouwensfunctie*, and the intelligence and security services conduct an investigation under the Security Screening Act. This is a substantially deeper process at several levels of depth, examining background, associations, financial circumstances and, at higher levels, foreign ties and residence history.

**Client-imposed screening.** Consultants and contractors are often screened by the client as well as the employer. Working for a bank, a ministry or a critical infrastructure operator may require their process regardless of what your own employer has already done.

## Why Data Roles Attract Screening

Access is the reason. A data engineer with production access can typically read more personal data than almost anyone else in an organisation, frequently including data that customer-facing staff see only in fragments. A machine learning engineer may hold copies of datasets in training environments. An analyst can export.

Organisations that have thought about insider risk therefore treat data platform access the way they treat cash handling or system administration. Expect screening to be more likely, not less, as your access grows — and expect it to be repeated when you move into a more privileged role internally.

## What Employers May and May Not Ask

Dutch practice sets real limits, and candidates should know them.

**Proportionality.** Screening must be relevant to the role. An employer cannot demand an unlimited investigation into your private life because you applied for a data job.

**Transparency.** You should be told in advance what will be checked, by whom and why. Screening conducted without your knowledge is a data protection problem for the employer.

**Health questions.** Employers may not ask about your health or medical history during recruitment, and a medical examination is permitted only for specific job requirements under strict conditions.

**Criminal record.** An employer may request a certificate of conduct but generally may not demand a full disclosure of your judicial history directly.

**Social media.** Looking at public profiles is common. Systematic profiling, or asking for access to private accounts, raises legal problems.

**Data protection.** Screening results are personal data, subject to purpose limitation and retention rules. You can ask how long the information will be kept.

The recruitment code used in Dutch practice also expects employers to inform candidates about procedures and to handle their data carefully — a reasonable benchmark to hold an employer against.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Certificate That Arrived Late

A candidate accepts a role at a healthcare organisation with a start date four weeks away. The employer requests a certificate of conduct, the application goes in, and the process takes longer than expected because a check requires additional review.

Because the organisation cannot allow access to patient data without the certificate, the start date is postponed. The candidate has already resigned, and there is a gap with no income and no clarity about when it ends.

Two lessons follow. First, start the process as early as the employer allows, and ask what the typical turnaround is for this employer and role. Second, before resigning, clarify in writing whether the contract is conditional on screening and what happens if the result is delayed rather than negative. A reasonable employer will address this; an employer who will not put it in writing has told you something.

## If You Have Something in Your History

Candidates with a criminal record, a financial history such as a past bankruptcy, or a complicated background sometimes assume that screening is an automatic barrier. It is usually more nuanced.

A certificate of conduct assesses whether a record is relevant to the specific role and its risks, weighing the nature of the offence, how long ago it occurred and what has happened since. A driving offence is unlikely to be relevant to a data engineering job; an offence involving fraud or unauthorised system access may well be. Refusals can be objected to, and there are procedures for appeal.

For deeper security investigations, the assessment is about vulnerability to pressure as much as about wrongdoing, which is why unresolved debt or undisclosed relationships can matter more than an old minor offence.

In all cases, the worst approach is concealment. Being found to have hidden something is generally treated as more serious than the underlying fact, and in security contexts it is disqualifying in itself. If you are uncertain, seek advice before applying rather than after.

## International Candidates and Residence History

Screening was designed around people whose history can be verified in Dutch records, and candidates who have lived abroad face practical complications.

A certificate of conduct covers Dutch judicial records; employers may ask for an equivalent document from countries where you previously lived, and the availability and processing time of such documents varies enormously.

For deeper security investigations, verifiable residence history over a period of years is often required, and gaps that cannot be checked may prevent a positive outcome. This is not a judgement about the individual; it is a limitation of the verification process. Candidates in this situation are generally better served by targeting employers and roles where such investigation is not required, of which there are many.

## Practical Steps for Candidates

- Ask early in the process what screening applies, who conducts it and how long it usually takes.
- Get any conditionality of the offer in writing before you resign from your current job.
- Start applications for certificates as soon as permitted; processing times vary.
- Keep your own records: diplomas, employment dates, addresses and reference contacts, because reconstructing them under time pressure is miserable.
- Be consistent between your CV, your profiles and what you say in interviews — inconsistencies are the most common cause of avoidable screening friction.
- Ask what happens to your screening data afterwards and how long it is retained.

## The Other Side: You Will Also Be Asked to Build Screening Systems

Data professionals encounter screening twice. Once as a candidate, and once as the person asked to build or maintain the systems that perform it.

Organisations increasingly use software to check candidates against sanctions lists, adverse media, company registers and public records. Recruitment platforms rank applicants. Fraud teams score employees and contractors for risk. If you work in a bank, a large employer or a technology supplier to either, you may be asked to work on one of these.

The professional obligations that follow are worth internalising before the request arrives. Screening systems process personal data for a purpose with serious consequences for individuals, which places them squarely inside data protection law and, where they influence employment decisions, potentially inside the high-risk categories of European AI rules.

The practical questions to raise are consistent: Is the screening proportionate to the role? Does the candidate know it is happening? How are false positives handled — is there a human review, and can the person contest a result? Is adverse media matching resolving to the right individual, given how many people share names? How long are results kept? And is the system being used for the purpose it was assessed for, or has it quietly expanded?

Raising these questions is not obstruction; it is the job. Systems that screen people are exactly where a data professional's judgement matters most, because the person affected is rarely in the room.

## Contractors, Consultants and Repeated Screening

Freelancers and consultants in the Dutch market experience screening differently from employees, and the difference is worth planning around.

Each client may run its own process. A consultant working successively for a bank, a ministry and a hospital may complete three separate screenings within a year, each with its own forms, references and waiting time. A certificate of conduct has a purpose-specific character, so one issued for a previous role is not automatically accepted for a new context, and clients frequently require a recent document.

Two practical habits help. First, keep an organised personal file: employment dates, addresses, diplomas, reference contacts and copies of previous certificates with their dates. Reconstructing a decade of history each time is a significant and entirely avoidable cost.

Second, treat screening lead time as part of your availability. A contractor who tells a client they can start in two weeks, when the client's screening takes six, has created a problem for both sides. Being explicit about it early is a mark of professionalism and avoids the awkward alternative of an assignment starting with a gap.

For assignments in defence, critical infrastructure or intelligence-adjacent work, the lead times are longer still, and some clients will not begin screening until a contract is signed — which means accepting a degree of uncertainty as part of the arrangement.

## Access Is the Thing Worth Negotiating

There is a reframing that helps both sides of this conversation: screening exists because of access, so access itself is negotiable.

Many data roles are designed with far more access than the work requires, usually because it was easier to grant broad permissions than to design narrow ones. A machine learning engineer may be given production access to a full customer database when what they actually need is a pseudonymised extract of a subset of fields.

Proposing the narrower arrangement serves everyone. It lowers the screening burden, reduces the organisation's exposure, and makes your own position more comfortable — you cannot accidentally misuse data you cannot reach. It also demonstrates exactly the judgement employers look for in senior data staff.

In practice this means asking, early: what access does this role actually require, can sensitive work be done on pseudonymised or synthetic data, and is there a process for temporary elevated access when it is genuinely needed? Employers who have thought about insider risk will recognise the question immediately. Those who have not may be persuaded to improve their arrangements — which is a good contribution to make in your first months.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch screening ranges from basic verification to a certificate of conduct to a full security investigation for positions of confidence.
- Data and AI roles increasingly attract screening because production data access is a genuine insider risk.
- Employers must keep screening proportionate and transparent, may not ask about health, and must handle results as personal data.
- Timing is the practical risk: clarify conditionality in writing before resigning from your current role.
- Concealment is treated more seriously than the underlying issue; disclosure and advice are the better strategy.

## Where to Start

Ask about screening in the first interview rather than the last. It is a normal question, and the answer tells you how professionally the employer runs its hiring.
