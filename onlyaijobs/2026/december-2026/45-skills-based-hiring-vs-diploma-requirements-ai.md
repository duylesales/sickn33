Almost every Dutch vacancy for a machine learning or data role opens with the same line: a completed university degree in a quantitative field. Some of those roles genuinely require it. Many do not, and the requirement is inherited from a template written years ago. In a market where the same employers complain constantly that they cannot find people, it is worth asking what that line is actually doing.

## What the Degree Requirement Is Standing In For

A degree requirement is a proxy, and it is worth naming what it proxies for.

**Trained mathematical and statistical thinking.** For roles doing genuine modelling under uncertainty, this is real and hard to acquire informally.

**The ability to learn difficult material independently.** A degree is evidence of this, though not the only evidence.

**A shared vocabulary.** Knowing what a confidence interval, a gradient or a distribution is, without having to be taught during the first project.

**A filter for volume.** With two hundred applications, a degree requirement reduces the pile. This is the honest reason in many cases, and it is worth admitting because there are better filters.

The question for any specific vacancy is which of these the work genuinely needs. A role designing evaluation methodology for models influencing clinical decisions needs the first. A role building and operating reliable data pipelines mostly needs engineering discipline, which is not what a statistics degree teaches.

## Where the Requirement Costs You Most

Four groups are excluded by a rigid degree line, and all four contain people Dutch employers say they want.

**Vocationally educated engineers.** The Dutch vocational and applied education system produces excellent practical engineers. In data engineering, platform work, automation and industrial applications, the best candidate for a role is frequently someone from this route with five years of hands-on experience.

**Career changers.** Analysts, developers, actuaries, teachers and researchers who moved into data work through practice. Their degree, if they have one, may be in something unrelated, which a keyword filter treats as no degree at all.

**International candidates with unfamiliar qualifications.** Recruiters who cannot evaluate a degree from another country sometimes default to rejection. Recognition services exist precisely for this, and using them widens the pool considerably.

**People who left education for reasons unrelated to ability.** Illness, family circumstances, money. The Dutch system offers routes back, but a rigid requirement closes the door regardless.

In a labour market where technical vacancies stay open for months, excluding four categories of capable people to save screening time is an expensive economy.

## What to Replace It With

Removing the requirement without replacing it with something rigorous produces worse hiring, not better. The replacement is an explicit definition of capability plus a structured way of assessing it.

**Define the actual requirements.** Not "a master's in a quantitative field" but "can reason about uncertainty in a forecast and explain it to a non-technical stakeholder" or "can build and operate a pipeline that runs unattended and fails safely". Write these before you write the advert.

**Use work samples.** A short, realistic task, assessed against a written rubric, predicts performance better than a conversation about education. Keep it to a few hours, pay for it if it is longer, and evaluate it blind where practical.

**Structure the interview.** The same questions for every candidate, scored against defined criteria. This is the single most effective change most employers can make, and it improves outcomes regardless of the degree question.

**Assess reasoning, not recall.** Ask candidates to talk through a problem, including what they would do when they get stuck. Self-taught candidates often perform strongly here, because they have spent more time getting unstuck.

**Be honest about the floor.** If the role genuinely requires mathematical depth, test for it directly rather than assuming a diploma proves it — plenty of degree holders cannot do it either.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Candidate Who Was Filtered Out Twice

An employer searches for six months for a data engineer. A candidate applies with a vocational qualification, eight years of experience building data infrastructure at two companies, and a public repository demonstrating exactly the skills required.

The applicant tracking system screens for a university qualification and rejects the application automatically. Six weeks later, the same candidate is submitted by an agency, and the hiring manager interviews them because the agency vouched for them. They are hired, perform well, and are promoted within eighteen months.

The employer paid an agency fee for a candidate who had already applied directly and been rejected by their own filter.

This is not unusual, and it is worth auditing: how many applicants are rejected automatically on education, and would any of them have been interviewed had a human read the CV? Employers who run this check are frequently uncomfortable with the answer.

## The Legal and Fairness Dimension

Two considerations deserve attention.

**Equal treatment.** Requirements that are not justified by the role can have indirect effects on groups protected by equal treatment legislation. A requirement that is genuinely necessary is defensible; one inherited from a template is harder to defend and harder to justify to yourself.

**Automated screening.** Where AI tools filter or rank applications, European rules treat recruitment and selection as a high-risk area, with expectations around human oversight, transparency and data governance. An automated rejection on a criterion nobody has examined is precisely the practice these rules are aimed at.

Beyond compliance, there is a practical fairness point. Candidates invest real effort in applications. An employer who advertises a requirement they do not actually need, and rejects people against it automatically, is wasting other people's time at scale.

## What This Does Not Mean

Skills-based hiring is not a lowering of standards, and it is frequently misrepresented as such by both advocates and critics.

Some roles genuinely require deep formal training. Research positions, roles developing new methods, and work requiring rigorous statistical reasoning under uncertainty are better served by candidates with that education, and pretending otherwise produces failure.

Nor does it mean ignoring education entirely. A relevant degree is real evidence, and it should count. The change is that it becomes one signal among several rather than a gate that nothing else can pass.

Finally, it does not reduce screening effort. Assessing capability directly is more work than filtering on a field in a form. The argument for doing it is that it produces better hires from a larger pool, in a market where the pool is the binding constraint.

## Practical Steps for Employers

- Audit your open vacancies: which explicitly require a degree, and for how many is it genuinely necessary?
- Rewrite requirements as capabilities, and separate "must have" from "nice to have" honestly.
- Check whether your applicant tracking system rejects automatically on education, and turn it off if it does.
- Introduce a short, rubric-scored work sample and a structured interview before changing anything else.
- Use diploma recognition services for international qualifications rather than defaulting to rejection.
- Track outcomes: where did your best performers of the last three years come from educationally?

That last step often settles the argument internally faster than any policy discussion.

## Designing a Work Sample That Candidates Will Actually Complete

The most common objection to skills-based hiring is practical: candidates refuse long assignments, and the good ones have other offers. That objection is valid for badly designed assessments and largely disappears for well designed ones.

**Keep it to two or three hours, and say so.** Anything longer should be paid, and paying is cheaper than losing candidates.

**Make it resemble the job.** A realistic small task — debug this pipeline, critique this analysis, design an approach to this problem — tells you far more than an algorithmic puzzle and signals respect for the candidate's time.

**Allow their own tools.** Assessing someone's ability to work the way they normally work is the point.

**Score against a rubric written in advance.** Without one, reviewers reward similarity to themselves, which is both unfair and unhelpful.

**Discuss it afterwards.** The conversation about why they made certain choices is usually more informative than the artefact, and it is where self-taught candidates often shine.

**Give feedback.** Candidates who invested hours deserve more than a template rejection, and in a small market how you treat applicants circulates.

An alternative that works well for experienced candidates is a structured discussion of something they have already built, probed in depth. It costs them nothing and reveals a great deal.

## What the Evidence Suggests About Predictors

It is worth knowing what decades of selection research broadly indicate, because it rarely matches intuition.

Structured interviews — the same questions for every candidate, scored against defined criteria — consistently outperform unstructured conversations, which are closer to a coin flip dressed up as judgement. Work samples that resemble the actual job are among the better predictors available. General reasoning ability predicts performance across roles, though testing for it directly raises fairness considerations that need care.

Years of experience, by contrast, is a weak predictor once past a threshold, and educational attainment predicts less than most hiring managers assume — particularly for roles whose daily work is engineering rather than research.

The practical implication for a Dutch employer is not to abandon judgement but to structure it. Most hiring processes in this market are unstructured conversations plus a degree filter, which is close to the least predictive combination available. Adding a rubric and a work sample costs little and improves outcomes measurably — and it is also the change that makes dropping a degree requirement safe rather than reckless.

## Key Takeaways

- Degree requirements act as a proxy for skills that can be assessed directly, and are frequently inherited rather than chosen.
- The requirement excludes vocationally trained engineers, career changers, international candidates and people who left education for non-ability reasons.
- Replace it with defined capabilities, work samples scored against rubrics, and structured interviews.
- Automated rejection on unexamined criteria raises equal treatment and AI Act oversight questions.
- Some roles genuinely need formal training — test for the underlying capability rather than assuming the diploma proves it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer with a long-open vacancy) Would dropping the degree requirement help?
Often yes, if replaced by direct assessment — it widens the pool to experienced practitioners whose qualifications do not match the template.

### (Scenario: employer worried about standards) Does skills-based hiring mean lower standards?
No — it means assessing capability directly, which is more work than filtering on education and generally more predictive.

### (Scenario: employer using an ATS) What should I check first?
Whether education criteria trigger automatic rejection, and how many otherwise qualified candidates that removes.

### (Scenario: employer hiring for research roles) Are there roles where the degree matters?
Yes — research positions and work developing new methods genuinely benefit from formal training; test for the capability directly.

### (Scenario: employer receiving foreign diplomas) How do I assess unfamiliar qualifications?
Use recognised diploma evaluation services rather than defaulting to rejection, and weight demonstrated work more heavily.
