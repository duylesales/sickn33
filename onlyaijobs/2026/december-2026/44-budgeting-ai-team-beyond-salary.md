A board approves a budget for two data scientists and expects results within a year. Eighteen months later there is a prototype nobody uses, an unexpectedly large cloud bill and a frustrated team. The salaries were right; almost everything around them was missing from the plan. Budgeting an AI capability is not difficult, but it requires accounting for six or seven categories that rarely appear in the original business case.

## The Fully Loaded Cost of a Person

Start with the obvious item, correctly calculated. A Dutch employee costs considerably more than their gross salary: holiday allowance, employer social security and insurance contributions, pension contributions, and any year-end payment. Add workplace costs, equipment, software licences and training, and the loaded cost of an employee is a substantial multiple of the monthly figure on the offer letter.

Then add recruitment. For scarce technical roles, expect either a long internal search consuming management time, or agency fees calculated as a percentage of first-year salary. And account for ramp-up: a new data hire is rarely productive in month one, because they are learning your data, which nobody has documented.

The practical consequence is that the first year of a hire costs meaningfully more than the annual run rate — which is why hiring two people six months apart is often easier to absorb than hiring two at once.

## Compute: The Line That Surprises People

Cloud and compute costs behave differently from other technology spending because they are consumption-based and generated by the team's own decisions.

**Training and experimentation.** Exploratory work is bursty and can be expensive, particularly with accelerated hardware. A single carelessly left-running instance can cost more in a weekend than a month of planned usage.

**Inference in production.** If a model serves live traffic, cost scales with usage. For applications built on large language models, cost per request is a design parameter: a feature that calls an expensive model for every user action can quietly become the largest line in the product's cost base.

**Storage.** Data accumulates and is rarely deleted. Retention policies are a cost control as well as a compliance obligation.

**Data transfer.** Moving data between regions or out of a provider carries charges that surprise teams designing multi-region architectures.

The organisations that manage this well do three things: set a budget and alert on it, attribute cost to teams or products so it is visible to the people generating it, and review it monthly rather than discovering it in the annual accounts. None of this requires sophisticated tooling; it requires somebody being responsible.

## Data: The Cost Nobody Budgets

For most applied machine learning, data is a bigger constraint than modelling, and acquiring usable data costs money.

**Annotation and labelling.** Whether done in-house, through a specialist supplier or by domain experts, labelling a meaningful dataset is a real project with a real budget. Expert labelling in medical, legal or technical domains is expensive and is usually underestimated by a factor of two.

**Purchased data.** Market data, geospatial layers, enrichment services and industry benchmarks carry licence costs, often annually.

**Data preparation.** The engineering effort to get data from source systems into a usable state is the largest single time cost in most first-year AI projects, and it is work, not overhead.

**Quality improvement.** Fixing upstream data quality frequently requires changes in systems owned by other departments, which means negotiation, and sometimes funding their work.

A useful planning heuristic: if your business case allocates most of the budget to modelling and little to data, it is wrong, and the team will discover this in month three.

## Maintenance: The Cost That Compounds

Every system you build must be kept alive. This is the most consistently ignored line in AI budgeting, and the one that eventually stops teams from doing anything new.

A production model requires monitoring, periodic retraining, pipeline maintenance as source systems change, dependency and security updates, incident response, and documentation upkeep. Each individual system is a modest ongoing commitment; five of them together can consume most of a small team's capacity.

The practical implication for planning is that a team's ability to deliver new work declines as its portfolio grows, unless capacity grows with it or systems are deliberately retired. Employers who plan a constant output from a constant headcount are planning for disappointment.

A reasonable rule of thumb used by many teams is to reserve a meaningful share of capacity — often a quarter to a third — for maintenance and reliability once a few systems are in production, and to treat that as a fixed cost of having built them.

## Compliance and Governance

Regulatory obligations have become a budget line rather than a footnote.

Depending on what you build, this can include documentation of data governance and model development, risk assessments, human oversight arrangements, logging and traceability, security review, privacy impact assessments, and in regulated sectors external audit or certification.

For a system falling into a high-risk category under European AI rules, the documentation burden is genuine and should be resourced deliberately rather than absorbed by an engineer at the end of a project. For most ordinary business applications the obligations are lighter, but the assessment of which category you are in is itself work that needs doing.

The cheapest version of compliance is the one designed in from the start; the most expensive is the one retrofitted after a system is in production.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Budget That Was Right and Insufficient

A mid-sized company budgets for two data scientists and a modest cloud allocation to build a demand forecasting capability. The salaries are accurate and the cloud estimate is based on a reasonable extrapolation.

By month four, three things have happened. Getting data out of the ERP system required a supplier change request that was not in the budget. The forecasting work revealed that historical stock-out data was never recorded, so a change to the operational system was needed. And the experimentation phase used more compute than planned because the team ran comparisons properly rather than superficially.

None of these were mistakes. All were foreseeable. The project eventually succeeds, six months later than promised, and the delay damages confidence in a way that a properly scoped plan would have avoided.

The fix is not a bigger budget; it is an honest one, with a contingency for data access work and a phase gate after discovery where the plan is revised with real information.

## Comparison: Where the Money Actually Goes

| Category | Typical share in year one | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| People (loaded cost) | Largest single item | Includes recruitment and ramp-up |
| Data access and preparation | Substantial | Usually underestimated; involves other teams |
| Compute and storage | Moderate, variable | Bursty in experimentation, scales in production |
| Tooling and licences | Modest | Platform, monitoring, annotation, BI |
| Annotation and expert time | Variable, sometimes large | Domain expert labelling is expensive |
| Compliance and documentation | Modest to substantial | Depends heavily on sector and risk category |
| Maintenance | Small in year one, grows | Becomes dominant by year three |

## Tooling: Small Lines That Add Up

Individually modest, collectively significant, and frequently bought twice because nobody keeps a list.

A working data team typically needs: a data platform or warehouse, orchestration, version control and CI, a monitoring and alerting stack, a business intelligence tool, an experiment tracking or model registry function, annotation tooling if relevant, and development environments. Add collaboration tools, security tooling and whatever the rest of the organisation already pays for.

Two failure modes recur. The first is duplication: the analytics team buys a BI tool, another department already had one, and the organisation pays twice while producing two conflicting versions of the same number. The second is abandoned subscriptions — a platform trialled during a project, never cancelled, renewing annually.

The fix is unglamorous and effective: one list, one owner, an annual review, and a rule that new tooling requires naming what it replaces. Employers who do this typically find a meaningful amount of recoverable spend in the first review.

## Phasing: How to Budget When Nobody Knows the Answer Yet

The deepest problem with AI budgeting is that the accurate estimate cannot exist at the moment the budget is approved, because it depends on what the data turns out to be like.

The workable approach is to split funding into phases with a decision point between them.

**Discovery.** A small, time-boxed investment — often a few weeks — to establish what data exists, what state it is in, and whether the problem is tractable. The deliverable is an informed plan, not a model.

**Build.** Funded on the basis of what discovery found, with a scope that reflects reality rather than ambition.

**Productionise and adopt.** Frequently underfunded: the work of integrating a model into an operational process, training users, and changing how decisions are made is often larger than building the model.

**Operate.** The ongoing line, budgeted as a permanent commitment rather than as a project cost.

Organisations that fund in phases stop far fewer projects mid-flight and waste far less money, because the decision to continue is made with information rather than optimism. It also gives the team permission to report that something is not feasible — which is a cheap outcome at the end of discovery and an expensive one at the end of a year.

## Measuring Whether Any of It Paid Off

The final budget question is the one employers ask least rigorously: did the spending produce value?

The honest difficulty is that attribution is hard. A forecasting improvement coincides with a change in purchasing behaviour; a fraud model launches in a quarter when fraud patterns shifted anyway. Claiming the whole effect for the model is the standard temptation and the reason many AI benefit claims are not believed internally.

The practical approach is to decide the measurement before the build. Agree the baseline — what happens if we do nothing — and the metric, and where possible retain a holdout: a set of stores, regions or cases where the old approach continues. A holdout costs a little forgone benefit and buys a credible number, which is worth considerably more the second time you ask for budget.

Also measure the operational effects that do not appear in a business case: hours of manual work removed, decisions made faster, errors avoided. These are frequently larger than the headline financial benefit and easier to substantiate.

Organisations that do this build a track record and find their subsequent proposals approved more easily. Those that do not end up relitigating the value of data work in every budget cycle.

## Key Takeaways

- The loaded cost of a Dutch employee is a substantial multiple of the gross salary, plus recruitment and ramp-up in year one.
- Compute costs are consumption-based and generated by the team's own decisions; attribute, alert and review monthly.
- Data acquisition, preparation and annotation are the most underestimated budget lines in applied AI.
- Maintenance compounds: reserve capacity for it or the team's ability to deliver new work will decline.
- Compliance designed in from the start is far cheaper than compliance retrofitted after deployment.

## Where to Start

Before approving the next AI business case, ask what it allocates to data access, annotation and maintenance. If the answer is nothing, the plan is a modelling plan rather than a project plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: director approving a business case) What is the most commonly missing budget line?
Data access and preparation, followed by annotation and the ongoing maintenance of whatever gets built.

### (Scenario: employer surprised by cloud costs) How do we control compute spending?
Attribute cost to teams, set alerts, review monthly, and make cost per request a design consideration for production features.

### (Scenario: employer planning multiple projects) Why does output fall as we build more?
Each production system carries ongoing maintenance, which consumes capacity unless headcount grows or systems are retired.

### (Scenario: employer in a regulated sector) How much does compliance add?
It varies with risk category, but designing documentation and oversight in from the start is far cheaper than retrofitting.

### (Scenario: SME with a limited budget) Where should limited money go first?
Data access and quality — a capable person with unusable data will produce nothing, while decent data makes modest modelling valuable.
