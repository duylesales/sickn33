A candidate negotiating with a Dutch university medical centre and one negotiating with an Amsterdam scale-up are playing two entirely different games, and the reason has nothing to do with the individuals involved. One employer falls under a collective labour agreement that fixes scales, leave, pension and annual increases; the other has none, and everything is individual. Understanding which situation you are in before you open your mouth is one of the most useful pieces of knowledge in the Dutch job market.

This article is general information, not legal advice; the content of agreements changes with each negotiating round.

## What a CAO Is

A collective labour agreement — in Dutch a *collectieve arbeidsovereenkomst*, universally abbreviated to CAO — is an agreement between employers or an employer association and trade unions, covering pay and employment conditions for a defined group of workers.

It typically sets salary scales and the steps within them, annual increases, working hours, leave entitlements, allowances such as a year-end payment, pension arrangements, provisions on overtime and standby duty, training rights and procedures around reorganisation.

There are two ways you can be covered. Either your employer is bound directly, through membership of an employers' association or by concluding its own company agreement, or the agreement has been declared generally binding for a whole sector by the Ministry of Social Affairs and Employment, in which case employers in that sector must apply it whether they participated or not.

The practical result is that many Dutch employees have their core terms decided collectively, at a table they never sat at — and that this can be to their considerable advantage.

## Which AI and Data Jobs Fall Under Which Agreements

**Universities and research institutes.** Academic staff, research software engineers and data specialists at Dutch universities work under the universities' agreement, with published scales and well-defined progression. Research institutes have their own comparable arrangements.

**University medical centres and hospitals.** Data analysts, information managers and clinical informatics staff fall under healthcare agreements with detailed job families and scales.

**Central and local government.** Data scientists and information advisers at ministries, agencies, provinces, municipalities and water authorities work under public sector agreements with transparent scales.

**Banking, insurance and pensions.** Many financial institutions apply company-level or sector agreements covering a large share of staff, including technical roles.

**Industry and manufacturing.** Technical staff at manufacturing companies often fall under industrial agreements, which cover engineering and IT roles inside those firms.

**Retail, logistics and energy.** Sector agreements exist, and whether a specific data role falls inside them depends on the job structure of the employer.

**Technology companies, consultancies and startups.** Frequently no collective agreement applies. Terms are individual, salary bands are internal and unpublished, and there is no automatic annual increase.

That last category is where most self-described AI jobs sit, which is precisely why so many candidates in this field have never encountered the topic.

## What Coverage Gives You, and What It Costs

**Advantages.** Transparent scales make it possible to know what the job pays before you apply. Progression steps are automatic rather than dependent on advocacy. Leave, year-end payments and pension arrangements are usually stronger than in uncovered sectors. Procedures around reorganisation and dismissal are clearer, and there is a defined route for disputes.

**Disadvantages.** Your ceiling is the top of the scale. Exceptional performance rarely produces exceptional pay. A scarce skill — and machine learning is scarce in public sector organisations — cannot easily be rewarded above the framework, which is one reason such employers struggle to compete for senior technical people. Flexibility on individual arrangements can also be lower.

The honest summary: covered employment is better at the start of a career and for predictability, and worse at the top end for people with in-demand specialisms.

## How Scales Actually Work

Where a CAO applies, your salary is determined in two steps.

First, the job is evaluated and placed in a scale. The evaluation looks at responsibility, complexity, autonomy and knowledge required, using a structured job family description. This is where negotiation genuinely matters — and it is about the job, not about you.

Second, you are placed at a step within that scale, usually based on relevant experience. Some room exists here, particularly if you can evidence directly relevant work.

Two practical consequences follow. If a role is placed one scale too low, no amount of good performance will fix it, so a candidate should read the job family description and, if the work described is genuinely more senior, raise it before accepting. And since progression through steps is often automatic, the long-run difference between candidates comes from moving to higher-graded roles rather than from annual negotiation.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Data Scientist Graded as a Reporting Analyst

A hospital hires a data scientist to build capacity forecasting models. The vacancy was written by a team that previously employed reporting analysts, and the job description was reused with minor edits. The role is graded accordingly.

Eighteen months later the person is running models that influence operating theatre planning, has trained colleagues, and is the only one in the organisation who can maintain the system. Their salary reflects a job description that no longer resembles the work.

The remedy exists — a job re-evaluation, supported by an updated description and evidence of responsibilities — but it takes months and requires a manager who will champion it. The far easier moment to fix this was before signing, by asking which job family the role sits in and whether the description matches the actual work.

Candidates entering scale-based organisations should treat the job description as a financial document, because that is exactly what it is.

## The Works Council: The Other Body That Shapes Your Terms

Alongside collective agreements, Dutch organisations above a certain size have a works council, an elected employee body with legal rights of consultation and, on specific subjects, consent.

For data professionals this matters in a specific way: works councils have a say in arrangements concerning personnel monitoring and systems that process employee data. If your employer wants to introduce productivity monitoring, workforce analytics or an AI-supported HR tool, the works council is usually involved, and its position can determine whether the project proceeds.

That makes the works council relevant to you twice over: as an employee whose data may be processed, and as a professional who may be asked to build such a system. Knowing the process early avoids designing something that will be blocked late.

## A Common Misconception About Collective Agreements

International candidates often assume a CAO means union membership is required. It is not: coverage follows the employer and the sector, not your personal membership, and you receive the terms either way. Membership gives you a voice in negotiations and individual legal support, and a minority of employees are members.

A second misconception, common among Dutch technology workers, is that collective agreements are an anachronism irrelevant to modern work. That belief holds until the first reorganisation, at which point the difference between a defined social plan and an individual settlement becomes extremely concrete.

## Why Public Employers Cannot Pay for Scarcity

There is a structural problem in the Dutch labour market that every data professional should understand, because it explains a great deal about where the work is and is not.

Hospitals, ministries, municipalities, universities and water authorities all need machine learning and data engineering capability. All of them pay according to scales negotiated for an entire workforce, in which a data engineer is graded against a job family that also contains other specialists. Scarcity in one niche cannot easily be reflected without distorting the whole structure.

The consequences are visible. Public organisations lose candidates to commercial employers at the senior end. They compensate by hiring consultants and contractors — sometimes at day rates that dwarf what an equivalent employee would cost — which is expensive, politically awkward and produces knowledge that leaves when the contract ends.

Some organisations have found partial answers: allowances for scarce functions, hiring at a higher step within the scale, investing heavily in training internal staff, or competing on the substance of the work and the conditions rather than on pay.

For candidates, this creates a genuine opportunity. If you value security, transparency, a thirty-six hour week and problems with public significance, you can enter these organisations with considerable leverage — not on the scale, which is fixed, but on the role, the level, the training budget and the freedom to shape the work.

## Reading a CAO Before You Sign

A collective agreement is a long document, but the parts that matter to a new employee can be checked in fifteen minutes. Look for the following.

**The salary table.** Find your scale and step, and check the increase rhythm — usually annual steps until the top of the scale, plus collectively agreed increases.

**Year-end and holiday payments.** Confirm whether a thirteenth month or year-end payment exists and at what percentage.

**Working hours.** A thirty-six, thirty-eight or forty-hour week, and whether reduced-hours arrangements are structurally available.

**Leave.** Statutory and additional days, and whether extra days can be bought or sold.

**Standby and overtime.** Relevant for anyone in an on-call rotation: many agreements specify compensation for standby duty and out-of-hours work, which is frequently forgotten by data teams introducing on-call for the first time.

**Training rights.** Some agreements guarantee a budget or time allocation.

**Reorganisation provisions.** What happens in a restructuring — the social plan framework is worth knowing before you need it.

**Pension.** Which fund or scheme applies and at what contribution split.

Reading these sections is the single most efficient preparation available before a negotiation with a covered employer, because it tells you precisely which levers exist and which do not. Arguing for something the agreement forbids wastes your credibility; asking for a step placement that the agreement allows is a conversation your future manager can actually have.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- Does a collective agreement apply here, and if so which one?
- Which job family and scale is this role placed in, and who decided that?
- What step would I start on, and what evidence would support a higher step?
- Are progression steps automatic, and is there a performance component?
- Is standby or out-of-hours work compensated under the agreement?
- Does a works council exist, and has it been involved in systems this team builds?

These questions are entirely normal in the Dutch context and a recruiter in a covered organisation will answer them without hesitation. If they cannot, ask to speak to someone in HR before you accept — the grading decision is far harder to change afterwards than before.

## Key Takeaways

- A collective labour agreement, where one applies, sets your scale, leave, pension and annual increases — often more decisively than your negotiation.
- Universities, hospitals, government, much of finance and much of industry are covered; most technology companies and startups are not.
- Coverage brings transparency, automatic progression and stronger procedures, at the cost of a hard ceiling for scarce skills.
- In scale-based employers, the job description determines the scale, so read it as a financial document before signing.
- The works council shapes employee monitoring and workforce analytics, which matters to data professionals from both sides.

## Where to Start

Before your next interview, find out whether the employer falls under a collective agreement and, if so, which scale the role sits in. Five minutes of research changes what you should be negotiating for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: international candidate) Do I need to join a union to be covered by a CAO?
No — coverage follows the employer and sector; membership gives you influence in negotiations and individual support, but not the terms themselves.

### (Scenario: candidate at a startup) Does my employer have a CAO?
Most technology companies and startups do not; terms are individual and there is no automatic annual increase.

### (Scenario: candidate joining a hospital or university) What should I negotiate?
Primarily the scale placement of the role and your step within it, since the scale itself is fixed by the agreement.

### (Scenario: candidate with scarce skills) Can I be paid above the scale?
Rarely and usually only through special arrangements; this is why scale-based employers struggle to compete for senior technical specialists.

### (Scenario: data professional building HR tools) Why does the works council matter to me?
Systems that monitor or analyse employees typically require works council involvement, which can determine whether the project proceeds.
