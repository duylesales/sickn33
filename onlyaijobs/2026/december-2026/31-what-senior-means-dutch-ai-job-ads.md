A vacancy asks for a senior machine learning engineer with three years of experience. Another asks for a senior data scientist with ten, leading a team, owning architecture and advising the board. Both are titled the same, both exist in the Dutch market this week, and the salaries differ by a factor that would shock candidates applying to both. Levels in this field are not standardised, and treating a title as information rather than as marketing is one of the more valuable skills a job seeker can develop.

## The Dutch Vocabulary of Levels

The Dutch market uses a three-tier vocabulary that international candidates often meet for the first time here.

**Junior** covers roughly the first two years of professional work. The expectation is that you deliver defined tasks with support and learn quickly.

**Medior** is the term that surprises newcomers, because English-speaking markets rarely use it. It describes the broad middle: someone who works independently on scoped problems, needs guidance on ambiguous ones, and is not yet expected to set direction. Many people spend the majority of their careers at this level, and there is nothing diminishing about it.

**Senior** should mean independence in ambiguity: you take a vague problem, decide what should be built, get it built, and are accountable for the result. In practice the term is applied far more loosely.

Beyond senior, Dutch employers use **lead**, **principal**, **expert**, **staff** and **architect** with no consistency whatsoever. Some are people-management roles, some are technical authority roles, and some are simply salary bands with impressive names.

## Why Titles Have Inflated

Several forces push titles upward, and knowing them helps you read an advert accurately.

**Scarcity.** When employers cannot find senior people, some redefine senior downwards rather than raise pay or train internally.

**Recruitment mechanics.** A vacancy titled senior attracts more applicants and signals a higher salary band. Some employers title up deliberately to widen their pool.

**Flat organisations.** Companies that resist hierarchy often have only two or three levels, so everyone competent ends up senior.

**Retention.** Promoting someone in title is cheaper than promoting them in salary, and it works for about a year.

**Consultancy structures.** Client-facing firms use levels that signal billing rates rather than capability, which leaks into the wider market.

The consequence is that a title tells you about the employer's culture and pay band, and very little about the work — which is precisely why you have to read the rest of the advert.

## How to Read the Advert for the Real Level

Ignore the title and look for four signals.

**Scope of the problem.** "Build and maintain models for our forecasting system" is a defined scope. "Define how our organisation uses machine learning" is not. The second is genuinely senior regardless of what the title says.

**Who you influence.** Working within a team is one level. Working across teams is another. Advising directors and setting standards for others to follow is another again.

**What is already there.** Joining a mature platform with a team of ten is different from being the first data person in a company of two hundred. The second demands seniority the advert may not mention, because the employer does not know what they are asking for.

**Accountability language.** Words like "own", "accountable for", "set the direction", "represent the team to stakeholders" indicate real seniority. Words like "support", "assist", "contribute to" indicate the opposite, however the role is titled.

If the scope and the title disagree, the scope is the truth.

## Comparison: What Levels Usually Mean in Practice

| Level | Typical scope | Typical experience | Independence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Junior | Defined tasks within a project | 0–2 years | Works with regular guidance |
| Medior | Whole features or analyses end to end | 2–5 years | Independent on scoped problems |
| Senior | Ambiguous problems, technical decisions, mentoring | 5+ years | Sets approach, accountable for outcome |
| Lead / Principal | Multiple teams or an organisation-wide domain | 8+ years | Sets direction others follow |

Years of experience are the weakest of these indicators and the one most heavily used in adverts. Someone who has run five different production systems in four years may be more senior in substance than someone who has maintained one for a decade.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Senior Who Was Hired for the Wrong Level

A company hires a senior data scientist to "build our machine learning capability". The candidate has five strong years of modelling experience and expects to work on models.

Reality arrives quickly. There is no data platform, no clear ownership of source systems, no test environment, and no agreement about which business problem matters most. The job is not modelling; it is founding a function — which requires stakeholder management, architecture decisions, hiring input and the confidence to tell executives that their first idea is not feasible yet.

The candidate is good and the employer is reasonable, but the match is wrong. Within a year both are frustrated, and the departure is written up as a hiring mistake when it was a levelling mistake.

The question that would have surfaced it in an interview: what exists today, and what would I be expected to build from nothing? A candidate who asks that learns immediately whether the role is senior in the sense they mean.

## The Levelling Conversation Employers Should Be Having

Internally, the better Dutch employers have moved towards explicit competency frameworks: written descriptions of what each level means in terms of scope, autonomy, influence and impact, used consistently for hiring, promotion and pay.

When such a framework exists, ask to see it. It tells you exactly what would be expected and what the next level requires, and it is a strong signal of a well-run organisation. Its absence is not disqualifying — plenty of good employers manage informally — but in that case you should ask directly how promotion decisions are made and how often they happen.

Pay transparency rules arriving across Europe will push more employers to formalise this, because published ranges require defensible levels behind them. Candidates will benefit: comparing two offers becomes possible when both employers can explain what their levels mean.

## A Common Misconception About Seniority

Many candidates equate seniority with technical depth alone, and are surprised when a promotion goes to someone they consider less technically skilled.

In every levelling framework used in this industry, progression beyond the middle levels is defined by scope of impact rather than by depth of knowledge. A senior person is someone whose work makes other people more effective: through design decisions that outlive them, through mentoring, through choosing the right problem, through preventing expensive mistakes.

That does not mean technical depth stops mattering. It means depth alone plateaus, and the people who continue to progress are the ones who apply it to problems that extend beyond their own keyboard.

The inverse misconception exists too: that seniority requires becoming a manager. In the Dutch market, individual contributor tracks up to principal or architect level are increasingly common, particularly at larger employers, and worth asking about explicitly.

## Practical Advice for Candidates at Each Stage

**If you are junior**, apply to medior roles when the scope described matches what you have already done independently. Do not apply to roles requiring you to define direction; you will be doing it without support.

**If you are medior**, the fastest route to senior is not another year of the same work. It is taking ownership of something ambiguous — a migration, a new system, a problem nobody wants — and seeing it through. That story is what a senior interview is looking for.

**If you are senior**, be sceptical of titles offered at other companies. A "lead" role with no scope beyond your own project is a lateral move dressed up. Ask what decisions you would own and who else would be affected.

**If you are being underlevelled**, gather evidence in terms the framework uses: scope, autonomy, influence, impact. "I work hard and know a lot" does not move a levelling decision; "I own this system, three teams depend on it, and I set the standard others follow" does.

## Career Growth Beyond the Ladder

It is worth remembering that levels are an internal accounting device, not a measure of professional worth. The Dutch market in particular offers routes that the ladder does not describe: moving into a specialist niche where you are one of a handful of practitioners nationally, moving to a smaller organisation where you have far more influence than a title would suggest, going independent, or moving into a domain where your combination of skills is rare rather than competing in a crowded one.

Candidates who optimise exclusively for the next title often find, a decade in, that they have moved up a ladder they never chose. Those who optimise for scope, learning and problems that interest them tend to arrive at the same place with better stories.

## How Levels Differ Between Employer Types

The same word means different things depending on where it is used, and candidates moving between sectors should recalibrate.

**Scale-ups and startups** apply senior early, often to people with three or four years of experience, because the alternative is losing them. Scope is genuinely broad, which partly justifies it, but the depth of mentorship available is limited and the title will not always be recognised elsewhere.

**Large corporates** grade against formal frameworks, so senior generally means something consistent internally and is harder to obtain. It also travels better: a senior title from a large Dutch employer is usually taken at face value by others.

**Consultancies** level by a mixture of billing rate, client-facing capability and tenure. A senior consultant may be extremely capable at scoping and delivering client work while having less depth in a single system than an equivalent in-house engineer.

**Public sector and healthcare** grade against collective agreement scales, where the job family determines the level and the title is often derived from it. Titles are less inflated here, and a senior adviser may have wider responsibility than a senior engineer in a startup.

**Research institutes** use their own academic-adjacent structures, where seniority relates to scientific independence and the ability to attract funding as much as to delivery.

When moving between these worlds, expect to explain your level in terms of scope rather than title, and expect the receiving employer to translate it into their own framework regardless of what your CV says.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- What does this level mean here, and is there a written framework?
- What exists today, and what would I be building from nothing?
- Which decisions would I own without needing approval?
- Who depends on this role's output, and how many teams are affected?
- What would make someone in this role ready for the next level, and how long does that typically take?
- Is there an individual contributor track beyond senior, and who is on it?

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch adverts use junior, medior, senior, lead and principal inconsistently; the title is marketing, the scope is information.
- Read for scope, influence, what already exists and accountability language rather than years of experience.
- Being the first data person in an organisation is a senior role even when advertised otherwise.
- Progression beyond the middle is defined by scope of impact, not by technical depth alone.
- Ask whether a written levelling framework and an individual contributor track exist; both signal a well-run employer.

## Where to Start

Before your next application, rewrite the advert in your own words describing scope, ownership and dependencies. If you cannot, ask — and the answer will tell you whether the level is real.
