Ask a Dutch HR director how their data scientists are graded, and the answer is often that they sit in the IT job family, somewhere between a systems administrator and a software developer, on a framework written before the roles existed. That mismatch is tolerable while nobody compares notes. European pay transparency rules are removing that condition, and organisations that have not thought about their job architecture for technical roles are about to have the conversation whether they planned it or not.

This article is general information, not legal or reward advice; employers should involve their own HR, legal and works council processes.

## Why This Matters Now

Three pressures are converging.

**Pay transparency.** European rules move employers towards disclosing salary ranges in recruitment, giving employees information about pay levels for comparable work, and reporting on gaps. Ranges require defensible levels behind them, and "comparable work" requires a definition of what work is comparable.

**Retention.** Technical staff leave when progression is opaque. A capable engineer who cannot see what the next level requires, or who suspects a peer is paid more for the same work, starts looking — and in this market, looking produces offers.

**Scarcity.** Employers competing for scarce skills need to be able to answer a simple question in interviews: what would I earn here in three years, and what would I have to do to get there. Organisations without an answer lose candidates to those that have one.

## What a Job Architecture Actually Is

Stripped of jargon, it is three things.

**Job families.** Groupings of similar work: data engineering, analysis, machine learning, platform, governance. Enough distinction to be meaningful, not so much that every person has their own family.

**Levels within each family.** Described in terms of scope, autonomy, influence and impact, not years of experience or tool knowledge.

**Bands attached to levels.** Salary ranges that reflect the market for that family at that level, which means accepting that different families may command different market rates for the same internal level.

That last point is where most internal resistance arises, and it is worth addressing directly: paying a scarce specialism more than an abundant one is a market fact, not a judgement of worth. Organisations that refuse to acknowledge it usually end up paying the difference anyway through contractors, at a higher price and with worse continuity.

## Describing Levels Without Pretending

A usable level description answers four questions.

**Scope.** A task, a feature, a system, a domain, an organisation.

**Autonomy.** Works with guidance, works independently on defined problems, defines the approach, sets direction for others.

**Influence.** Own work, own team, multiple teams, the organisation.

**Impact.** Delivers assigned outcomes, owns a measurable outcome, changes how the organisation performs a function.

Keep it to one page per level. Frameworks that run to twenty pages are written to satisfy a consultant rather than to be read by a manager preparing for a promotion conversation, and they are not used.

The test of a good framework: can a manager and an employee, reading it together, agree within ten minutes on where the employee currently sits? If not, it is too vague or too complicated.

## The Individual Contributor Track

The most common structural failure in Dutch data teams is having only one route upward: management.

The consequence is predictable. Your best engineer becomes a mediocre and unhappy manager, you lose your strongest technical contributor, and the person you promoted leaves within two years. Alternatively they refuse the promotion, stay at the same level indefinitely, and eventually leave for an employer with a senior technical role.

A parallel individual contributor track — senior, principal, expert or architect — with equivalent pay bands and genuine status solves this. Two conditions determine whether it works in practice: the senior technical levels must have real decision authority rather than decorative titles, and at least one person must actually be on the track, because an unpopulated ladder is not believed by anyone.

For smaller Dutch organisations, a full dual ladder may be excessive. Even then, it is worth stating explicitly that technical seniority is recognised and how.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Framework That Cost Two Engineers

A company with sixty employees grades its two data engineers in a general IT scale. Both are competent; one has become the person who designs and owns the data platform.

At review time, both receive the same increase, because the scale does not distinguish between them. The platform owner raises it, is told the framework does not allow a different outcome, and leaves three months later for a role at a higher level elsewhere.

The replacement costs an agency fee and four months of vacancy. The remaining engineer, watching this, starts interviewing as well.

The framework cost far more than the salary difference would have. What the company needed was not a complete job architecture project but a modest change: distinguishing the level that designs and owns a system from the level that builds within it, and paying accordingly.

## Implementing This Without a Consultancy Project

For a mid-sized Dutch employer, a workable approach takes weeks rather than months.

**Start from the work.** List what the team actually does, and group it into two or three families. Do not start from a template.

**Define four or five levels**, in one page each, using scope, autonomy, influence and impact.

**Place current people honestly.** This is the uncomfortable step and the informative one: where people sit relative to what they are paid tells you where your problems are.

**Check the market.** Benchmark data for Dutch technical roles is available through salary surveys, recruitment agencies and published ranges; use more than one source.

**Fix the clearest anomalies first.** Correcting two obvious underpayments buys more credibility than a perfect framework announced later.

**Involve the works council** where one exists, and where a collective agreement applies, work within it — job evaluation is usually governed by the agreement rather than by your preference.

**Publish it internally.** A framework nobody can read does nothing. The point is that people can see what the next level requires.

## What Changes in Recruitment

Once levels exist, several recruitment problems become easier.

You can publish a range in the advert, which is where pay transparency rules are heading and which improves application quality immediately — candidates who would have withdrawn at the offer stage self-select out at the start.

You can answer the progression question in interviews concretely, which is a differentiator against competitors who cannot.

You can level candidates consistently, rather than paying whatever the last negotiation produced, which is the mechanism by which pay inequities accumulate in the first place.

And you can explain to an internal candidate why an external hire came in at a particular level — a conversation that otherwise corrodes trust quietly.

## The Equity Dimension

Pay gaps rarely arise from deliberate decisions. They accumulate through individual negotiations, where outcomes correlate with confidence and with what someone earned previously rather than with the work performed.

A defined architecture reduces that mechanism by anchoring pay to the level of the work. Reporting obligations under European rules will make residual gaps visible, and organisations that examine their own data first are in a considerably better position than those that discover a pattern when they are required to publish it.

The practical first step costs almost nothing: compare pay within each level across the team, and look at the outliers. If the explanation for a difference is "that is what they negotiated", the organisation has a problem it can still fix quietly.

## The Families Worth Distinguishing in a Data Team

Employers frequently ask how granular to be. For a Dutch organisation with a data team of five to fifty, a small number of families covers the ground.

**Data engineering and platform.** Building and operating the infrastructure, pipelines and tooling that everything else depends on. Market rates here are high and rising, and this is the family most often mis-graded against a general IT scale.

**Analysis and analytics engineering.** Turning data into decisions, building the modelled layers that business users consume, and working directly with stakeholders. Broader stakeholder skill, somewhat lower market rate than platform engineering at equivalent levels.

**Machine learning and data science.** Modelling, experimentation and the production systems that follow. Wide internal variation: a research-oriented role and a production ML engineering role are genuinely different jobs, and some employers split them.

**Governance, quality and privacy.** Data governance, model validation, data protection. A growing family driven by regulation, with a different skill profile and often an easier recruitment market.

**Leadership.** Team leads, product owners and managers, which should be a separate family rather than the top of a technical one.

Resist the temptation to create a family per person. Five families with five levels gives twenty-five defined positions, which is ample for almost any Dutch organisation and simple enough that managers can actually apply it.

## Handling the Transition Fairly

Introducing a framework where none existed exposes problems that were previously invisible, and how you handle the first few months determines whether people trust it.

Expect to find people who are paid above their level and people paid below. The second group is straightforward and should be corrected promptly; delay signals that the framework exists to constrain rather than to fix.

The first group is harder. The standard approach is to protect current salary rather than reduce it, while limiting future increases until the level and the pay align. Communicating that honestly is better than pretending the anomaly does not exist, because the person usually knows.

Two further principles help. Apply the framework to new hires immediately, so that the anomalies stop growing. And be explicit that the placement of a role reflects the work rather than a judgement of the person — a distinction that sounds pedantic and turns out to matter enormously in how the conversation lands.

## Key Takeaways

- Pay transparency rules require defensible levels and comparable-work definitions, which most Dutch data teams do not yet have.
- A usable architecture is job families, one-page level descriptions based on scope and impact, and bands attached to levels.
- Different families may command different market rates for the same internal level; refusing to acknowledge this pushes spending into contractors.
- A genuine individual contributor track prevents losing strong engineers to unwanted management roles.
- Publishing ranges improves application quality and makes internal levelling conversations possible rather than adversarial.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer without a framework) Do we need a full job architecture project?
No — for a mid-sized employer, two or three families and four or five one-page level descriptions can be built in weeks.

### (Scenario: employer under a collective agreement) Can we create our own levels?
Job evaluation is usually governed by the agreement; work within it and use the scale placement and job family description as your levers.

### (Scenario: employer losing senior engineers) Why do technical staff leave after promotion?
Often because the only route upward was management; a genuine individual contributor track with real authority prevents this.

### (Scenario: employer preparing for transparency rules) What should we do first?
Compare pay within each level across the team and examine the outliers, before ranges have to be published.

### (Scenario: employer worried about internal reactions) Should we publish the framework internally?
Yes — a framework people cannot read achieves nothing, and visibility is what makes progression conversations constructive.
