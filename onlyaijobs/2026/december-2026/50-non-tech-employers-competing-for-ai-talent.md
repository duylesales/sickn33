A wholesaler in Brabant, a hospital in Gelderland and a machine builder in Twente are all trying to hire the same data engineer, and so is a well-funded technology company in Amsterdam that can pay more and whose name the candidate recognises. The traditional employers usually respond in one of two ways: by imitating the technology company badly, with a vacancy full of borrowed vocabulary, or by giving up and hiring a consultancy. Both are avoidable, because the actual competition is less lopsided than it appears.

## Why You Are More Competitive Than You Think

Candidates leave technology companies for ordinary organisations constantly, and the reasons are consistent.

**The problems are concrete.** Optimising a production line, reducing waste in a fresh supply chain, improving patient flow — these are more tangible than incremental improvements to a conversion funnel, and a large share of experienced data professionals say so explicitly.

**The data is unexploited.** A company with twenty years of production data and no analysis offers a first-year impact that a mature technology company cannot: the low-hanging fruit is still on the tree.

**Ownership is broader.** In a team of three, a good engineer touches everything and sees the results. In a team of eighty, they own a component.

**The pace is sustainable.** Fewer reorganisations, less chasing of trends, more predictable weeks. For experienced people with families this is a genuine draw rather than a consolation.

**Domain depth is interesting.** People who become the person who understands both the models and the manufacturing process build a rare and durable profile.

The task is not to invent advantages. It is to state the ones you have, honestly and specifically.

## The Mistakes That Lose Candidates

**Imitating technology company language.** A vacancy full of borrowed terminology from a company whose business is machines or care reads as inauthentic, and candidates who want that environment will apply to the real thing anyway.

**Vagueness about the work.** "Working with big data and AI to drive innovation" tells a candidate nothing. "Predicting quality deviations on a production line using ten years of sensor data" tells them everything.

**Hiding the maturity level.** If there is no data platform, no version control and no one else technical, saying so attracts the specific candidate who wants to build it and repels the one who would have resigned in month three. That filtering is desirable.

**Slow processes.** Scarce candidates hold multiple conversations. A process with four rounds spread over six weeks loses them to an employer who decided in ten days.

**Salary secrecy.** Candidates assume the worst when no range is given, and pay transparency rules are moving the market towards publication regardless.

**Underestimating the advert.** For a non-obvious employer, the vacancy is the entire first impression. A specific, well-written advert from an unfamiliar company outperforms a generic one from a known name more often than employers expect.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Machine Builder That Got It Right

A machine builder needs someone to work on vision-based quality inspection. They cannot match Randstad salaries and their brand means nothing to a data scientist.

Their advert does four things. It describes the actual problem, including why the current approach fails. It states plainly that they have good data and no data team yet, and that the person will build both. It gives the salary range. And it says that the factory is a fifteen-minute cycle from a town where a family house costs a third of an Amsterdam apartment.

They receive eleven applications rather than eighty, and four are strong. The person they hire had two other offers, both better paid, and chose this one because they wanted to own something and because the commute would give them back ninety minutes a day.

Nothing in that outcome required a bigger budget. It required describing the job accurately and knowing which candidate it would appeal to.

## Build the Capability, Not Just the Vacancy

The most common failure among non-technology employers is hiring one data person into an organisation that cannot support them, then concluding after a year that AI does not work here.

Four preconditions matter more than the calibre of the hire.

**A sponsor.** Someone senior who wants the outcome and will clear obstacles. Without this, the hire spends a year requesting access.

**Access to data.** If getting a data extract requires a supplier change request and three months, solve that before the person starts.

**A defined first problem.** Not "explore what's possible" but a specific, valuable, feasible question with a person who will use the answer.

**Realistic time horizons.** The first useful result typically takes six to nine months in an organisation starting from zero, most of it spent on data access and quality.

Employers who get these right can hire a good mid-level person and succeed. Employers who get them wrong will fail with anyone.

## The Alternatives to Hiring First

Several routes build capability with less risk than an immediate senior hire.

**Graduation projects and internships.** Dutch universities and universities of applied sciences place students on real problems. It costs little, produces a genuine proof of concept, and is the most common way regional employers meet their first data hire.

**Develop someone internal.** The process engineer, the planner or the business analyst who already understands your operation and enjoys analysis is often a better long-term investment than an external hire, and training them costs less than a recruitment fee.

**A bounded consultancy engagement** with knowledge transfer written into the contract, used to build the first system while you recruit.

**Collaborative projects** with applied research institutes or regional development organisations, which sometimes carry funding and always carry expertise.

**Part-time and shared arrangements.** Several experienced specialists work three or four days a week, and a part-time senior person can be more valuable than a full-time junior.

## Where to Find the Candidates

Non-technology employers often advertise in the wrong places and conclude that nobody is available.

Candidates for these roles are frequently not in Amsterdam. They live in the region already, sometimes commuting an hour and a half each way, and would prefer not to. Regional visibility, specific job boards, university and applied university networks, and local professional communities reach them better than a generic national posting.

Referrals matter disproportionately. The first technical person you hire knows others, and in a small market a personal recommendation from someone who took a chance on an unfamiliar employer carries weight that no advert does.

And there is a timing advantage: people reassess their commute when their circumstances change — a child starting school, a partner's job moving, a house purchase. A regional employer with a steady presence in the local market catches those moments; one that advertises only when desperate does not.

## Writing the Advert: A Worked Structure

For employers whose name means nothing to a data professional, the vacancy carries the entire argument. A structure that works:

**Open with the problem, not the company.** Two sentences describing what needs solving and why it matters operationally. A candidate should know by the end of the second line whether this is interesting.

**Describe the data honestly.** What exists, in what state, how much history. "Fifteen years of process data in a historian, never analysed" is a more compelling sentence than any adjective.

**State the situation.** Team size, what exists technically, what does not, who the person would work with. Candidates are trying to assess whether they will be effective, and vagueness reads as concealment.

**Name the first project.** Specificity here is the strongest signal available that the employer has thought about the role.

**Give the range.** And the contract type, hours and office days in numbers.

**Explain the practicalities.** Location, commute, what living nearby costs compared with the Randstad, relocation support if any.

**Keep the requirements short and real.** Five genuine requirements outperform fifteen aspirational ones, which mostly deter good candidates who match twelve.

An advert of this kind takes an afternoon to write and outperforms months of generic posting. It also forces the internal clarity that makes the eventual hire succeed.

## The Long Game: Becoming Known Regionally

Employers outside the technology sector tend to recruit reactively — a vacancy appears, a search begins, six months pass. The organisations that eventually recruit easily have done something different over several years, and none of it is expensive.

They host or sponsor a local meetup, or simply offer their canteen as a venue. They send an engineer to speak about a real problem they solved, which is far more persuasive than any employer branding. They take students on graduation projects every year, which means twenty young professionals have seen the inside of the company by the time they are hiring. They maintain a relationship with the nearest university of applied sciences. And when someone leaves on good terms, they stay in touch.

Over three or four years this produces a situation in which the region's data professionals know who you are and what you work on. Vacancies then attract applications from people who already understand the context, and referrals arrive without being asked for.

None of this helps with the vacancy you are trying to fill this quarter. All of it helps with the one you will have next year, and the year after — which, for organisations that expect to need these skills permanently, is the more important problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Non-technology employers offer concrete problems, unexploited data, broader ownership and a sustainable pace, which candidates value more than employers assume.
- The most common mistakes are imitating technology company language, vagueness about the work, hiding maturity level and slow processes.
- A specific, honest advert with a salary range outperforms a generic one from a better-known employer.
- Preconditions — a sponsor, data access, a defined first problem and realistic timelines — matter more than the calibre of the hire.
- Graduation projects, internal development, bounded consultancy and part-time seniors are lower-risk routes to a first capability.

## Where to Start

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer competing on salary) Can we win against technology companies?
Frequently, on problem concreteness, ownership, pace and commute — but not by imitating their language or pretending to be one.

### (Scenario: employer with no data team) Should we say we are starting from zero?
Yes — it attracts the candidate who wants to build and repels the one who would leave in month three, which is the outcome you want.

### (Scenario: employer whose first hire failed) What usually goes wrong?
Missing preconditions: no senior sponsor, no practical data access, no defined first problem, or unrealistic expectations of speed.

### (Scenario: employer unsure about hiring) What is the lowest-risk first step?
A graduation project or internship with a Dutch university, which produces a proof of concept and often a future hire.

### (Scenario: regional employer) Where do we find candidates?
In your own region — many already commute long distances and would prefer not to; local networks and referrals reach them better than national adverts.
