Every Dutch organisation that has tried to hire a machine learning engineer knows the temptation. The permanent search has run for five months. A capable independent contractor is available next week at a day rate that is expensive but finite. The work gets done. The question nobody in the room asks is whether the arrangement, once it has run for two years with the contractor sitting in the team's daily stand-up, is still legally a contract for services.

This article is general information, not legal or tax advice. Classification is fact-specific and enforcement practice changes; take advice for your own situation.

## Why This Became a Live Issue

The Netherlands has one of Europe's largest populations of self-employed professionals, and technology is one of its densest concentrations. For years, the framework for assessing whether an engagement was genuine self-employment or a disguised employment relationship existed but was enforced with restraint.

That restraint has been wound back. Enforcement on the classification of working relationships has resumed, which means the tax authority can act where it concludes that an arrangement is in substance an employment relationship. The consequence is not primarily a problem for the contractor; it is a problem for the client, who may face assessments for wage tax and social insurance contributions.

Case law has also sharpened the picture, with courts emphasising that the question is decided by how the relationship actually works rather than by what the contract says. A well-drafted agreement that does not match daily reality offers little protection.

## The Criteria That Actually Matter

Classification turns on the substance of the relationship, and three themes recur in assessments.

**Authority and direction.** Does the client direct how the work is performed, when and where? Instructions about the result are normal in any commercial engagement; instructions about method, working hours and presence look like employment.

**Embedding in the organisation.** Is the person performing work that is part of the organisation's regular activities, alongside employees doing the same thing, with an internal email address, a place in the team structure and attendance at internal meetings? Deep embedding points towards employment.

**Entrepreneurship.** Does the individual bear commercial risk, have multiple clients, invest in their own tools and training, carry their own liability insurance, and market themselves? A contractor with one client for three years looks different from one with four clients a year.

Additional signals are weighed: whether the person can be replaced by someone else from their own business, how the rate compares with an employee's, whether they receive holiday arrangements or training like staff, and how long the engagement has run.

No single factor decides. The overall picture does.

## What This Means for How You Engage Contractors

Practical arrangements that keep a genuine contracting relationship genuine:

**Contract for a defined result or project**, not for open-ended capacity. "Deliver a working forecasting pipeline meeting agreed acceptance criteria" is different from "work on our data team for a year".

**Avoid integrating them into internal structures** more than necessary: no line management, no performance reviews, no inclusion in staff appraisal cycles or internal promotion conversations.

**Do not set working hours or require presence** beyond what the work genuinely needs.

**Allow substitution** where the contractor operates a business with staff, and do not insist that one named individual must personally perform every task unless the nature of the work requires it.

**Keep the engagement bounded.** Long, rolling extensions are the single most common pattern that turns a defensible arrangement into an indefensible one.

**Document the reality**, not just the contract: the statement of work, the deliverables, the acceptance of results.

If what you actually need is a person in a team for the foreseeable future, doing the same work as your employees, under your direction, then what you need is an employee — and pretending otherwise creates risk without solving the underlying problem.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Contractor Who Became the Team

A company engages a freelance data engineer for a three-month project. The project goes well, so the engagement is extended. Two years later, the contractor attends the daily stand-up, is listed on the team page, has a company laptop and email address, takes direction from the team lead, and is the only person who understands three production pipelines.

Their invoices have been paid monthly at a consistent number of days. They have had no other client in eighteen months.

Nothing about this arrangement looks like an independent business providing a service to a client. It looks like an employee with an unusual payment method — and that is the assessment a tax inspector would make.

The organisation faces three problems simultaneously: a classification risk, a knowledge concentration risk in one person who can leave at a week's notice, and a fairness question from employees doing similar work for less money with more obligations.

The remedy is usually the obvious one: offer employment. Many contractors in this position decline; some accept; a few become the strongest employees the company has. But the conversation should happen deliberately, not after an inspection.

## When Contracting Is Genuinely the Right Answer

None of this means contractors are a problem to be avoided. There are situations where independent engagement is exactly right.

**A specific expertise you need once.** Setting up an MLOps platform, an architecture review, a specialist optimisation model — a genuine project with a beginning and an end.

**A peak you cannot staff permanently.** A migration or an implementation with a defined scope.

**Expertise you cannot employ.** Someone with a national reputation in a niche, whose services you want for eight days, and who would never take a permanent role.

**Bridging while you recruit**, with an explicit end date and, importantly, a plan for knowledge transfer.

In all four cases the marker of a healthy arrangement is the same: you can describe the deliverable, and you can describe how the engagement ends.

## The Alternatives Worth Considering

**Secondment and staffing agencies.** The professional is employed by the agency, which carries the employment obligations, and you contract with the agency. This is the standard route for filling a seat in a team for an extended period without classification risk, at a higher cost than a direct contract.

**Fixed-term employment.** For a defined project of a year or more, a fixed-term contract may be simpler, cheaper and more attractive to the candidate than either alternative.

**Consultancy engagement.** Buying a defined outcome from a firm, which supplies its own people and manages them.

**Part-time permanent.** Frequently overlooked: several experienced specialists prefer three or four days a week, and a part-time permanent role competes surprisingly well against contracting for people who value security.

## The Business Case Beyond Compliance

Set the legal question aside for a moment: there is an operational argument for being deliberate about this.

Contractors rarely document as thoroughly as employees, because their incentives and tenure differ. Knowledge concentrates in people who leave when the engagement ends, which for machine learning systems — heavily dependent on undocumented context — is a serious continuity risk.

There is also a team dynamics dimension. Employees who see a contractor at a substantially higher rate doing similar work, without the obligations of employment, will notice, and the resentment expresses itself indirectly.

The organisations that manage this well are explicit about the difference: contractors work on bounded deliverables, transfer knowledge as a contractual obligation, and are not embedded in team rituals. Everyone understands the arrangement, and nobody is surprised when it ends.

## Writing an Engagement That Works in Practice

If contracting is the right answer, the engagement itself can be structured so that it serves the business and stands up to scrutiny. A few elements do most of the work.

**A statement of work with acceptance criteria.** What will exist at the end, and how will you know it is finished? Vague scope is both a commercial problem and a classification signal.

**A knowledge transfer obligation.** Written documentation, a walkthrough session, and a named internal owner who receives it. Make this a deliverable, not a courtesy, and hold back a portion of the engagement's value against it if necessary.

**Explicit intellectual property terms.** Who owns the code and models produced, and what may the contractor reuse for other clients? Ambiguity here causes disputes later, especially when the contractor's approach is genuinely reusable.

**Data protection arrangements.** If the contractor processes personal data, a processing agreement is required, along with clarity about where data may be stored and what happens to copies at the end.

**Confidentiality and security terms** proportionate to the access being granted, including what happens to laptops, credentials and repositories at the end of the engagement.

**A defined end, with an option rather than an assumption about extension.** Extensions should be a decision, taken deliberately, not the default outcome of nobody raising the question.

An engagement written this way tends to deliver better outcomes regardless of the legal dimension, because both sides know what success looks like.

## What Contractors Themselves Should Know

Although this guide is written for employers, the classification question cuts both ways, and clients benefit from working with contractors who understand it.

A genuine independent business protects itself by maintaining several clients over time, investing in its own equipment and professional development, carrying appropriate insurance, presenting itself commercially, and being willing to decline arrangements that look like employment in all but name.

Contractors who have thought this through are easier clients to work with, because they will push back on arrangements that create risk — asking for a scoped deliverable rather than open-ended capacity, declining to be included in performance review cycles, keeping their own equipment.

Employers occasionally experience this as inflexibility. It is the opposite: it is a sign of a professional who understands the framework both parties operate in, and it is a considerably better position than working with someone who will agree to anything and leave the client carrying the exposure.

## Key Takeaways

- Enforcement on false self-employment in the Netherlands has tightened, and the financial risk sits primarily with the client.
- Classification turns on substance — direction, embedding and genuine entrepreneurship — not on the wording of the contract.
- Bounded, deliverable-based engagements with clear endings are defensible; rolling extensions into team membership are not.
- Where you genuinely need a person in a team indefinitely, employment, secondment or a fixed-term contract are the appropriate routes.
- Beyond compliance, contractors concentrate undocumented knowledge, which is a real continuity risk for machine learning systems.

## Where to Start

Look at your longest-running contractor engagement and ask two questions: can you describe the deliverable, and can you describe how it ends? If not, that is the arrangement to address first.
