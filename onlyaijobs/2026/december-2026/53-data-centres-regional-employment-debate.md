Few Dutch spatial planning debates are as polarised as the one about data centres. Proponents cite digital sovereignty, connectivity and employment. Opponents cite land, electricity, water and the visual impact of very large windowless buildings on open landscape. Both sides reach for employment figures, and both tend to use them badly. For a municipality or province weighing an application, it is worth being precise about what the jobs argument does and does not support.

## The Employment Reality, Stated Plainly

A large data centre is a capital-intensive, highly automated facility. Its direct permanent employment is modest relative to its footprint, its investment and its electricity consumption. This is not a criticism; it is what the facility is.

Direct roles are real and skilled: critical facility engineers, electrical and mechanical technicians, security staff, and monitoring and automation specialists. These are decent, well-paid technical jobs, and in regions with a vocational training tradition they are a genuinely good fit.

Construction employment during the build phase is substantial and temporary, often drawing on specialised contractors from outside the region.

Indirect employment — maintenance contractors, catering, security services, local suppliers — exists but is dispersed and difficult to attribute confidently.

What a data centre does not typically bring is software engineering or machine learning employment. Those functions sit with the companies whose workloads run in the facility, and those companies are usually located elsewhere, frequently in another country.

That last point is the one most often blurred in local debates, in both directions: proponents imply a technology cluster will follow, and opponents dismiss the skilled facility roles that genuinely do arrive.

## Why the Honest Figure Still Matters

None of this means the employment argument is worthless. It means it should be made accurately.

For a rural municipality, twenty to fifty permanent skilled technical jobs is not trivial. These roles pay well, suit people trained in the regional vocational system, and offer career paths in critical infrastructure that transfer to other industries. In areas losing young technical workers, that is a real contribution.

What the figure does not support is a claim that a data centre transforms a regional labour market or creates a technology cluster. Regions that granted permissions on that expectation have generally been disappointed, and the disappointment damages trust in the next planning debate.

The policy conclusion is not to oppose or to approve but to negotiate on the basis of what the facility actually delivers.

## The Constraint That Dominates Everything: Electricity

In much of the Netherlands the binding constraint on new economic activity is grid capacity. Where congestion exists, a large connection granted to one user is not available to another.

This changes the nature of the decision. The question is not only whether a data centre is desirable in itself, but what else the same capacity could support: a manufacturer expanding, a housing development electrifying, a horticultural cluster converting away from gas, an electrolyser, a hospital.

For a province or municipality, this makes grid capacity an explicit planning variable rather than a technical detail handled by the network operator. Regions that have started treating it that way — mapping available capacity against economic priorities — are making better decisions than those treating each application in isolation.

It also creates a negotiating position. A facility that can offer flexibility — reducing demand at peak times, providing local grid services, coordinating its build with network reinforcement — is a materially different proposition from one that simply requests a large firm connection.

## What to Negotiate, Beyond Jobs

Municipalities and provinces have more leverage in these conversations than they often use. Conditions worth pursuing:

**Residual heat.** A data centre produces low-temperature heat continuously. Connecting it to a district heating network, a greenhouse cluster or a public building is technically feasible and increasingly expected. Getting this into the agreement at the planning stage is far easier than retrofitting it.

**Local training commitments.** Partnership with regional vocational institutions, guaranteed apprenticeship places, and a commitment to recruit locally for facility roles. These are low-cost for the operator and directly address the employment question.

**Grid flexibility arrangements**, as described above.

**Water and cooling transparency**, particularly where cooling affects local water resources.

**Landscape and reuse.** Design quality, screening, and what happens to the site if the facility becomes obsolete — a genuine question given the pace of technological change.

**Local energy participation.** Where renewable generation is developed alongside, community participation structures used for Dutch wind projects can apply.

Each of these converts an abstract debate about desirability into a concrete negotiation about terms, which is a more productive conversation for everyone involved.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Permission Granted on the Wrong Argument

A municipality approves a large facility partly on the basis of a presentation emphasising high-skilled digital employment. Local expectations form accordingly.

Three years later the facility is operating with a permanent staff considerably smaller than residents expected, most of it technical facility work rather than the digital jobs that were imagined. The local newspaper runs a critical piece. The residual heat, discussed during the process, was never connected because no network existed nearby and nobody was obliged to build one.

Nothing improper occurred. The operator delivered what it always intended. The municipality negotiated on an argument that the facility could never have satisfied, and neglected the conditions it could have obtained.

The next application in that region faces an electorate that no longer believes anything it is told — which is the real cost, and it is paid by the next project rather than by this one.

## What Regional Policy Should Actually Optimise For

If the goal is regional technical employment, data centres are not the most efficient instrument available, and it is worth saying so.

The same policy attention directed at the region's existing manufacturers, food processors, logistics operators, hospitals and energy companies — helping them adopt data-driven methods and find the people to run them — produces more technical jobs per unit of land, electricity and political capital.

That does not mean refusing digital infrastructure. It means being clear about which objective each instrument serves. Digital infrastructure serves connectivity, digital sovereignty and specific industrial needs; technical employment is served better by supporting the companies already in the region.

Regions that separate these two conversations make better decisions in both. Those that merge them tend to approve facilities for reasons that do not hold, and then under-invest in the capability building that would have delivered what they actually wanted.

## Key Takeaways

- Large data centres bring modest but genuine direct employment, skilled and suited to vocationally trained technicians.
- They do not typically bring software or machine learning employment, which sits with the companies whose workloads run there.
- Grid capacity is the dominant constraint; a connection granted to one user is unavailable to another, making it a planning variable.
- Negotiate residual heat, local training commitments, grid flexibility, water transparency and site reuse rather than debating desirability alone.
- For regional technical employment, supporting existing companies to adopt data-driven methods is a more efficient instrument.
