The standard advice circulating in technology careers is that switching employers is the fastest route to higher pay, and it is broadly true. The standard counter-advice is that constant movement produces shallow experience, and that is broadly true too. In the Dutch market both effects are real but weaker than in larger economies, and a third factor — that people talk — changes the calculation in ways the international advice does not capture.

## What Switching Actually Buys

**A step change in salary.** Internal increases in the Netherlands are typically modest, whether set by a collective agreement's annual step or by a company's review cycle. An external move resets the negotiation entirely, and the difference is frequently larger than several years of internal progression.

**A level correction.** Organisations are slow to re-level people internally: you carry the reputation of the person who arrived as a junior three years ago. A new employer sees only your current capability, which is why moving is often the fastest route from medior to senior.

**New context.** Seeing a second, third and fourth way of doing things is genuinely educational. People who have only worked in one organisation frequently mistake local conventions for universal truths.

**An exit from a dead end.** Some situations do not improve. A manager who will not change, a company whose data strategy is decorative, a team with no path forward — recognising these early and leaving is not disloyalty, it is judgement.

## What Staying Actually Buys

**Depth and consequence.** You see your own decisions age. The architecture you chose two years ago either holds up or does not, and living with that is how engineers develop judgement. Those who move every eighteen months rarely experience the consequences of their own work, which is a significant gap at senior levels.

**Trust and scope.** Influence accumulates. The person who has been there three years is asked about the roadmap; the person who arrived last month is asked to deliver a ticket. In the Netherlands, where teams are small and hierarchies flat, trust converts into autonomy quickly.

**Institutional knowledge.** Knowing why a decision was made, which stakeholder cares about which number, and where the bodies are buried is real professional capital, even though it does not transfer.

**Employment security.** The chain rule means tenure converts temporary contracts into permanent ones. Leaving resets that clock, and the value of a permanent contract is easy to underestimate until you need it.

**Pension continuity.** Long tenure in a strong scheme accrues meaningfully, and moving between employers with different arrangements introduces administrative complexity and sometimes loss.

## The Dutch Specifics That Change the Sums

Three national characteristics alter the standard advice.

**Compressed salary ranges.** The gap between the bottom and the top of the market for a given role is narrower than in the US or UK. Mobility therefore delivers real but bounded gains, and after a few moves the returns diminish noticeably.

**A small, talkative market.** Hiring managers ask around. In specialised niches, everyone knows everyone within two degrees. A pattern of leaving abruptly, or a reputation for over-promising, circulates informally in a way that simply does not happen in a market of millions.

**Employment protection.** The permanent contract has genuine value here, which raises the cost of restarting a probationary relationship with a new employer, particularly for people with mortgages or families.

The net effect is that the optimal Dutch pattern involves fewer moves than the international advice suggests, but not none.

## Comparison: The Patterns and Their Outcomes

| Pattern | Typical salary outcome | Depth developed | Reputation risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move every 12–18 months | Fast early gains, plateaus | Limited; rarely sees consequences | Rises with each short stint |
| Move every 3–4 years | Strong overall trajectory | Good; sees work mature | Low |
| Stay 6–10 years, then move | Slower interim, strong senior profile | Deep, sometimes narrow | Low, but re-entry can be harder |
| Stay 15+ years | Depends entirely on the employer | Very deep, potentially insular | Risk of appearing immobile |

The three-to-four-year rhythm appears repeatedly among people who end up in strong senior positions in the Dutch market, for a simple reason: it is roughly how long it takes to build something, see it operate, and learn from what it became.

## A Concrete Scenario: The CV That Raised Questions

A candidate with seven years of experience has five employers on their CV, each stay between twelve and eighteen months, all with impressive-sounding titles.

The technical interview goes well. The concerns emerge in the discussion afterwards, and they are not about loyalty. The panel notices that in every role the candidate describes building something, and in none of them do they describe what happened to it afterwards, what broke, or what they would do differently. They have five first years of experience.

The candidate is not rejected for moving. They are rejected because the panel cannot find evidence of the judgement that comes from living with your own decisions.

The lesson is not "do not move". It is that you must be able to describe consequence. A candidate with five short stays who can say "I left after the system had been running for six months and here is what we learned in that time" answers the concern entirely.

## When Leaving Is Clearly Right

Some signals are unambiguous, and people tend to ignore them for far too long.

You have stopped learning anything and have said so twice with no change. The organisation's stated commitment to data work is not reflected in any decision or budget. Your manager actively blocks development or takes credit. The team is structurally under-resourced and the response to raising it is that everyone is busy. You have been passed over for a level you can evidence, with no explanation. Or the work has become maintenance of one system with no path onward.

None of these improve through patience alone. If a concrete conversation about change does not produce a concrete response within a few months, the decision has effectively been made for you.

## When Staying Is Clearly Right

Equally, some circumstances justify staying longer than the average advice suggests.

You are building something whose outcome you have not yet seen. You have influence you would have to rebuild elsewhere. You are learning from someone genuinely better than you. The organisation is investing in you in ways that are visible in your calendar and budget rather than in a performance review. Your life outside work needs stability at this particular moment — and that is a legitimate professional consideration rather than an admission of weakness.

The Dutch labour market accommodates this better than most. Part-time arrangements, reduced hours during a difficult period and internal moves are all more available here than in most countries, and using them is often better than an external move made for the wrong reason.

## Internal Moves: The Underused Middle Path

The forgotten option between staying and leaving is changing roles within the same organisation. In larger Dutch employers this is frequently possible and frequently overlooked: a move from analytics to platform engineering, from a business unit to a central team, from delivery to architecture.

An internal move preserves tenure, contract status, pension continuity and your accumulated trust, while providing the novelty and learning that a change of employer would deliver. It is usually easier to arrange than people expect, because a manager who cannot keep you in their team would still rather keep you in the company.

The practical approach is to raise it early with your own manager rather than applying quietly elsewhere internally, which is generally regarded as poor form in Dutch organisations and will be found out anyway.

## How to Explain Your History in an Interview

Whatever pattern your CV shows, you will be asked about it, and the answer is a preparable artefact rather than an improvisation.

**If you have moved often**, give each move a reason that is about the work rather than about grievance: a project completed, a capability built, a domain you wanted to learn. Then volunteer the consequence evidence unprompted — what happened to the systems you built after you left, what you learned from seeing them run, what you would design differently. That single addition converts the concern into a strength.

**If you have stayed a long time**, the concern is the opposite: whether you have seen anything else and whether you can adapt. Answer it with variety inside the tenure — different teams, different technologies, different problem types, internal moves, external community involvement. Demonstrating that you kept learning without changing employer is the whole task.

**If you left something quickly**, address it briefly and without drama. A role that was misrepresented, a reorganisation, a company that ran out of funding — these are common and understood. A long defensive explanation reads far worse than a single calm sentence.

**If there is a gap**, say what it was. Illness, caring responsibilities, burnout recovery, study, a deliberate break. Dutch employers are considerably more relaxed about gaps than candidates fear, and evasiveness is what raises concern rather than the gap itself.

The underlying principle is the same in all four cases: interviewers are not scoring your history, they are assessing whether you have thought about it clearly.

## Key Takeaways

- Switching raises salary faster and corrects levelling, but Dutch salary ranges are compressed so the returns diminish after a few moves.
- Staying builds the judgement that comes from living with your own decisions, which senior interviews probe directly.
- In a small, talkative market, reputation circulates informally in ways it does not in larger economies.
- A three-to-four-year rhythm recurs among people who reach strong senior positions in the Netherlands.
- Internal moves preserve tenure, contract status and trust while providing the change you might be seeking externally.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate weighing an offer) Is switching employers the fastest way to a higher salary?
Usually yes, because internal increases are modest — but Dutch ranges are compressed, so the gains diminish after several moves.

### (Scenario: candidate with short stays) Will frequent moves count against me?
Not automatically; what concerns interviewers is the absence of evidence that you lived with the consequences of your own work.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure whether to leave) What are the clear signals to go?
No learning after raising it, blocked development, structural under-resourcing with no response, or being passed over without explanation.

### (Scenario: candidate who wants change but likes the company) Is an internal move realistic?
Often yes, and it preserves tenure, contract status and trust; raise it with your own manager rather than applying quietly elsewhere internally.

### (Scenario: candidate on a temporary contract) Does leaving affect my job security?
Yes — tenure converts temporary contracts into permanent ones under the chain rule, and moving resets that clock.
