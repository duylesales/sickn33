For years, the first question an international candidate asked about a Dutch job offer was whether it came with the thirty per cent ruling. That question is still asked, but the answer has become more complicated: the facility has been narrowed, capped and reformed in successive political rounds, and anyone relying on numbers from an article written a few years ago will plan badly.

This article is general information, not tax or immigration advice. Percentages, thresholds and durations change, and the current rules should always be checked with the tax administration, the immigration service or a qualified adviser before you make a decision.

## What the Facility Is

The expat facility allows employers to pay a portion of the salary of an employee recruited from abroad free of wage tax, as a compensation for the extra costs of living outside one's home country. Historically this was thirty per cent of gross salary, which is why it became known by that number.

Several things about it have changed in recent years, and these are the points to verify rather than assume:

**The percentage.** Successive reforms have reduced the tax-free proportion, and the structure has shifted between tiered and flat approaches. Transitional arrangements have generally protected people already using the facility, but the details matter and depend on when your first grant was made.

**The duration.** The maximum period has been shortened compared with earlier years.

**The salary threshold.** A minimum taxable salary applies, with a lower threshold for younger employees holding a recent master's degree. The amounts are indexed annually.

**The cap.** The facility is limited above a certain salary level.

**Related provisions.** Options that previously accompanied the ruling, such as certain non-resident treatment for other income, have been abolished or phased out.

The practical advice is uniform among advisers: get the calculation for your own situation, in the year in which you would start, in writing.

## The Immigration Route Matters as Much as the Tax

For non-EU nationals, the tax facility is secondary to the question of whether you can work here at all. The dominant route into Dutch technology jobs is the highly skilled migrant scheme, which requires the employer to hold recognised sponsor status with the immigration service and to pay at least a defined minimum salary, differentiated by age and indexed each year.

Three practical implications follow.

**Not every employer can hire you.** Smaller companies are often not recognised sponsors, and becoming one takes time. When applying, ask directly whether the employer is a recognised sponsor.

**Salary thresholds interact with job level.** A junior role may not meet the threshold, particularly for candidates over thirty, which is one reason international juniors find the Dutch market harder to enter than seniors.

**Alternatives exist.** The EU Blue Card, the orientation year for recent graduates of Dutch and certain international universities, and intra-company transfers all have their own conditions, and the best route is not always the obvious one.

EU and EEA nationals have free access to the labour market and are not affected by sponsorship, though the tax facility still requires meeting its own conditions.

## The Costs Nobody Mentions in the Offer Letter

**Housing.** This is the single largest practical obstacle for people moving to the Netherlands today. Supply in and around the major cities is severely constrained, rents are high, and competition for rental properties is intense. Buying is difficult in the first year without a permanent contract and local financial history. Candidates should budget more than they expect and consider regions outside the Randstad seriously, which is exactly where many strong employers are.

**Health insurance.** Residents must take out private basic health insurance, with a monthly premium per adult and an annual own-risk amount. This is a real monthly cost that surprises people from systems funded through taxation.

**Registration and administration.** Registering with the municipality, obtaining a citizen service number, opening a bank account and arranging a rental contract form a chain where each step depends on another. Employers with experienced relocation support make this dramatically easier, and it is worth asking what support is offered.

**Childcare and schooling.** Dutch childcare is subsidised but not free, and international schools are expensive with waiting lists. Families should treat this as a major line item rather than a detail.

**Partner employment.** A partner's ability to work depends on their own permit situation, and their job search may be harder than yours if they do not speak Dutch.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Offer That Looked Generous

An engineer receives an offer that, with the expat facility applied, appears to deliver a substantially higher net income than their current job elsewhere in Europe.

Once the full picture is assembled, the difference narrows. Rent for a family-sized apartment within reasonable commuting distance consumes a large share of the gain. Health insurance premiums for two adults are a new monthly cost. Childcare, even subsidised, is significant. The facility applies for a limited period, after which net income drops noticeably unless salary has grown to compensate.

The move may still be worth making — for the work, the quality of life, the cycling infrastructure, or the fact that the Dutch labour market treats employees well — but it should be evaluated on the post-facility steady state, not on the first-year figure. A useful rule of thumb is to ask yourself whether the job would still be attractive if the facility did not exist at all, because eventually it will not.

## A Common Misconception Among International Candidates

Many assume the ruling is an entitlement attached to being foreign. It is not: it is a facility with conditions, applied for by the employer, which requires the employee to have been recruited from abroad and to have lived at a sufficient distance from the Dutch border for a defined period beforehand. Someone who has already been living in the Netherlands, or nearby, may not qualify.

A second misconception is that the facility can be arranged later if it is forgotten at the start. Application deadlines apply, and a missed deadline can cost months of benefit. Confirm with the employer before your first payslip who is arranging it and when.

## What to Ask an Employer Before Accepting

- Are you a recognised sponsor with the immigration service, and have you hired internationally before?
- Will you apply for the expat facility, and who prepares the application?
- What relocation support is provided — housing search, registration, administration, partner support?
- Is there a repayment clause if I leave within a defined period?
- What is the salary progression path, given that the facility will end?

Employers who answer these clearly have done it before, and that experience will save you months of friction.

## The Non-Financial Calculation

Beyond the arithmetic, the Netherlands offers international professionals a labour market where English is widely spoken in technology and research, working hours are genuinely respected, part-time and flexible arrangements are normal, and employment protection is strong by international standards.

The counterweights are worth stating honestly: housing is difficult, top-end salaries are compressed relative to the US or Switzerland, learning Dutch matters for many non-technology roles and for social integration, and bureaucracy rewards patience.

For most AI professionals who move here, the decisive factors turn out not to be tax at all, but the quality of the work, the commute, and whether their family settled well.

## Where International Candidates Actually Get Hired

Knowing which parts of the Dutch market are genuinely open to international candidates saves months of wasted applications.

**Most open.** High-tech industry and semiconductors, research institutes and universities, international technology companies, the space and aerospace cluster, and specialised engineering firms. These environments work in English, hire internationally as a matter of course, and are experienced with sponsorship.

**Moderately open.** Financial services, consultancies with international clients, larger logistics and manufacturing employers, and product teams at Dutch platforms. Dutch is often preferred rather than required, and the answer depends on the specific team.

**Largely closed without Dutch.** Healthcare, government at every level, education, most SMEs, and any role facing Dutch customers or citizens. This is not prejudice; the work is conducted in Dutch and the stakeholders are Dutch-speaking.

There is also a seniority effect worth naming. Senior specialists are recruited internationally because they are scarce; junior international candidates face far more competition, because Dutch graduates are abundant and cheaper to hire without sponsorship. International juniors therefore succeed most often through Dutch universities, the orientation year that follows graduation, or internships that convert.

Targeting the open segment and being realistic about the closed one is the single most effective strategy adjustment most international candidates can make.

## Learning Dutch: The Honest Calculation

Every international professional in the Netherlands eventually has this argument with themselves, usually around the eighteen-month mark.

The case for not learning: you can work in English in much of the technology sector, everyone speaks English well, courses take time, and progress is slow because Dutch people switch to English the moment they detect an accent.

The case for learning is stronger than it first appears. It roughly doubles the number of employers available to you, particularly in healthcare, government and regional companies where interesting problems sit. It changes your position in meetings, because informal discussion — the part where decisions actually form — often happens in Dutch. It affects how permanent your life here feels, which in turn affects whether you stay. And it matters for a partner's employment prospects, which is frequently the real determinant of whether a family settles.

A pragmatic target is conversational competence within two years, with a realistic acceptance that professional fluency takes longer. Many employers fund courses; asking for this during negotiation is normal and rarely refused.

The decision is ultimately about time horizon. For a two-year assignment, English is sufficient. For a life, it is not — and most people who move here for two years are still here after five.

## Regional Netherlands Is the Underused Answer to the Housing Problem

Almost every international candidate begins their search in Amsterdam, Utrecht, Rotterdam and The Hague, which is precisely where housing competition is worst and where their salary buys least.

The alternative deserves serious consideration. Eindhoven and the surrounding high-tech region is the most internationally oriented labour market in the country outside Amsterdam, with English widely used in engineering teams. Groningen, Enschede, Nijmegen, Wageningen, Delft and Maastricht are university cities with international communities and substantially lower rents. Smaller industrial and research towns across the country host employers that are global leaders in their niche and that struggle to attract candidates purely because nobody has heard of the place.

The country is also small and the rail network is dense: living in a regional city does not isolate you the way it might elsewhere in Europe. A Saturday in Amsterdam is a train ride, not an expedition.

The practical suggestion is straightforward. Run your job search on the work rather than the postcode, then evaluate housing, commute and community for the specific location. Candidates who do this frequently find both a better role and a better standard of living than those competing for the same Randstad vacancies as everyone else.

## Key Takeaways

- The expat tax facility has been repeatedly reduced in percentage, duration and scope; verify current rules for your start year.
- For non-EU candidates, sponsorship and salary thresholds matter more than tax — confirm the employer is a recognised sponsor.
- Housing is the largest practical obstacle to moving to the Netherlands today, especially in the Randstad.
- Health insurance, childcare and international schooling are real costs that offers rarely mention.
- Evaluate the role on the steady state after the facility ends, not on the first-year net figure.

## Where to Start

Ask the employer for a net calculation in writing, with and without the facility, and for a clear statement of sponsorship and relocation support. Then decide on the job, not on the tax.
