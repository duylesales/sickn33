A province launches a campaign to attract technical talent. The advertisements are good, the message is honest, and the jobs are real. A candidate responds, interviews, receives an offer — and then spends six weeks discovering that within thirty minutes of the workplace there is nothing available that a household of their size can afford or find. They decline, and the vacancy reopens.

This sequence plays out continuously across the Netherlands. In most regions, the campaign is run by one department and the housing file by another, and nobody owns the point where they meet.

## The Specific Shape of the Problem

The Dutch housing shortage is not uniform, and the parts that affect technical recruitment are quite particular.

**The middle rental segment is thinnest.** People arriving for a job usually rent first: they do not yet have the employment history or the local financial position to buy. The segment between social housing eligibility and expensive free-sector rental is precisely where supply is scarcest.

**Newcomers compete badly.** Without a permanent contract, a Dutch rental history or local references, an applicant loses to those who have them, regardless of income.

**Speed matters more than price.** In tight markets, properties go to whoever can respond within hours. Someone coordinating a move from another city or country is structurally disadvantaged.

**Family housing near employment is scarcest of all.** A single professional can find a room; a household with children needing three bedrooms within commuting distance of an industrial site faces a much harder search.

**Temporary accommodation is the missing rung.** Without a place to stay for the first two to three months, the whole sequence — registration, bank, rental application — cannot begin.

## Why This Is a Regional Economic Issue, Not Only a Social One

Housing policy in the Netherlands is discussed primarily in terms of affordability, waiting lists and social equity, which is appropriate. What is discussed less is the economic consequence for regions.

When housing near employment is unavailable, three things follow.

Employers cannot fill skilled vacancies, which constrains investment decisions and, over time, influences where companies choose to expand.

Employees commute further, which costs them time and health, costs the region congestion and emissions, and weakens the connection between where people work and where they participate in community life.

And regions lose their own young people permanently: a graduate who cannot find housing at home does not return, and by the time housing is available their life is established elsewhere.

None of these appear in a housing waiting list statistic, and all of them affect regional prosperity directly.

## What Municipalities and Provinces Can Actually Influence

Housing supply cannot be conjured quickly, but several interventions are available on shorter timescales.

**Temporary and flexible accommodation.** Short-stay units, converted buildings and flexible housing serve newcomers, students, people between homes and those in crisis. They are faster to deliver than permanent housing and address the missing first rung directly.

**Coordination with major employers.** Where a large employer or a cluster is expanding, planning capacity in advance of the recruitment is straightforward and rarely done. Employers will often participate financially.

**Middle-segment rental in planning conditions.** Municipalities have instruments to require a proportion of mid-market rental in new development, and the segment matters disproportionately for labour mobility.

**Repurposing.** Vacant offices, retail and institutional buildings can be converted, and regional centres with declining retail often have more stock than they realise.

**Information as a service.** A regional housing information point for newcomers — what areas exist, what they cost, how the rental process works, which agencies operate — costs little and improves conversion of job offers into accepted jobs.

**Student and starter housing.** Regions with a university of applied sciences that produces graduates and then cannot house them are training people for other regions.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Employer Who Built Housing

An industrial employer in a region with a tight housing market repeatedly loses candidates over accommodation. Working with the municipality and a housing provider, they arrange a small number of units for new employees, available for the first year of employment.

The effect is disproportionate to the number of units. Candidates who would have declined now accept, because the impossible first step is solved and they can search for permanent housing with a local address, a local income and time. Most move on within a year, freeing the units for the next arrivals.

The municipality's contribution was planning cooperation and speed rather than money. The employer's contribution was modest against the cost of a permanent vacancy.

This model — a small pool of transitional housing linked to employment — is used in several Dutch regions and remains surprisingly rare given how well it works.

## The Trade-Off Nobody Wants to State

There is an uncomfortable tension in regional housing debates that is worth naming, because avoiding it produces incoherent policy.

Building housing to attract workers is frequently unpopular with existing residents, particularly where local young people are already on waiting lists. A municipality that visibly prioritises incoming knowledge workers over its own residents creates a legitimate grievance.

The honest framing is that these are not necessarily competing objectives. The same shortage affects the local nurse, teacher, technician and the incoming data engineer, and the segment that serves all of them — mid-market rental and affordable starter homes — is the one in shortest supply. Policy that addresses that segment serves both groups.

What does create genuine conflict is dedicating scarce existing stock to newcomers rather than adding supply. Regions that expand the pool can serve both objectives; regions that reallocate it will face opposition they cannot argue their way out of, and should expect to.

## Connecting the Files

The practical recommendation for a province or region is organisational rather than financial.

Put the economic development officer and the housing officer in the same conversation about the same specific problem: how many skilled vacancies are open, where, and what housing exists within a realistic commute. That analysis takes weeks and is almost never done, because the two files have separate budgets, separate reporting lines and separate policy vocabularies.

Where it has been done, the conclusions tend to be concrete and unglamorous: a particular business park needs transitional accommodation, a particular town has vacant stock and no conversion pipeline, a particular employer's recruitment radius is limited by one missing rail connection.

Those findings are more useful than another talent attraction campaign, and considerably cheaper.

## Key Takeaways

- Regional talent campaigns fail when housing within a realistic commute is unavailable, and the two policy files are rarely connected.
- The binding shortages are mid-market rental, family housing near employment, and temporary accommodation for the first months.
- Newcomers compete badly regardless of income, because they lack local history, references and the ability to respond within hours.
- Faster interventions exist: flexible accommodation, planning coordination with employers, conversion of vacant buildings, information services.
- Address the segment that serves both residents and newcomers; reallocating existing stock to incomers creates justified opposition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: region running a talent campaign) Why are candidates declining offers?
Frequently housing: without available mid-market rental within a realistic commute, an attractive job is not actually accessible.

### (Scenario: municipality with limited land) What can we do in the short term?
Flexible and temporary accommodation, conversion of vacant buildings, and a newcomer housing information service.

### (Scenario: council facing local opposition) How do we justify housing for incoming workers?
By addressing the segment that is scarce for everyone — mid-market rental and starter homes — rather than reallocating existing stock.

### (Scenario: employer struggling to recruit) Can employers help?
Yes — transitional housing linked to employment solves the impossible first step and is cheap relative to a permanent vacancy.

A joint view of open technical vacancies by location against housing availability within a realistic commute of each.
