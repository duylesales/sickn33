A bank, a hospital and a municipality all need machine learning capability, and all three will find that hiring an excellent engineer from a product company does not automatically give them one. The technical work is similar. What differs is everything around it: what must be documented, who must approve, what happens when a supervisor asks a question, and how long it takes to change something that is already running. Hiring for these environments requires selecting for a different profile, and being honest with candidates about what the job actually involves.

## What "Regulated" Actually Changes

Four things, consistently.

**Approval precedes deployment.** A model that affects customers, patients or citizens goes through review — internal validation, risk assessment, sometimes external audit — before it runs. The engineer does not decide alone when something is ready.

**Documentation is a deliverable.** Data lineage, design rationale, validation evidence, monitoring plans and change history are not overhead; they are what allows the organisation to defend a decision two years later.

**Change is controlled.** Retraining a model, updating a threshold or swapping a data source is a change with a procedure attached. Continuous deployment in the product-company sense is rarely available.

**Explainability is a requirement, not a preference.** If a decision affects someone materially, the organisation must be able to explain it — to the individual, to a supervisor, sometimes to a court.

Candidates who find these constraints intolerable exist, and they self-select out quickly. Candidates who find them interesting become extremely valuable, because relatively few people can do both the modelling and the governance well.

## Sector-Specific Realities

**Financial services.** Supervision by the central bank and the markets authority brings expectations around model risk management, outsourcing, operational resilience and consumer outcomes. Larger institutions run a formal model validation function independent of model development — which is itself a career path, and one that is easier to hire into than model development because fewer candidates know it exists.

**Healthcare.** Clinical decision support may fall under medical device regulation, with conformity assessment, clinical evaluation and post-market surveillance obligations. Data is special-category personal data, research use requires an appropriate basis, and clinicians must retain meaningful oversight of decisions affecting patients.

**Government.** Transparency expectations are high and rising: Dutch public bodies publish information about algorithms in a public register, decisions affecting citizens must be explainable and contestable, and procurement rules shape what can be bought and from whom.

**Energy, water and critical infrastructure.** Operational safety rules, resilience requirements and security obligations constrain what may be automated and how systems must be protected.

Across all four, European AI rules add a further layer where a system falls into a high-risk category, with obligations on risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight and documentation.

## The Profile You Are Actually Hiring For

Technical competence is table stakes. The differentiators in a regulated environment are these.

**Validation instinct.** Does the candidate, unprompted, ask how a model would be tested, what could go wrong, and how failure would be detected? This is the single strongest predictor of success in these environments.

**Documentation discipline.** Can they write clearly for a reader who is not an engineer? Ask for a writing sample or a description of documentation they have produced.

**Comfort with oversight.** Some engineers experience review as an obstacle; others as a useful second opinion. Both attitudes are legitimate, but only one is workable here.

**Domain curiosity.** Someone who wants to understand how a mortgage decision, a care pathway or a benefits process actually works will produce far better systems than someone who treats the domain as a data source.

**Stakeholder patience.** Progress requires convincing risk officers, clinicians, legal advisers and works councils. That is the job, not a distraction from it.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Hire Who Left in Four Months

A hospital hires a strong machine learning engineer from a technology company to build predictive models for capacity planning. The offer is competitive and the person is capable.

Within four months they resign. Nothing went wrong technically. What happened is that their first model took eleven weeks to reach a pilot because of privacy review, clinical validation and an information security assessment, and during that period they were told twice that a simpler approach would be preferred because it could be explained to a clinical board.

The hospital concludes that technology company people do not fit. The more accurate conclusion is that the role was described as model development when it was substantially about navigating a governance process, and the candidate was never given the chance to decide whether they wanted that.

The fix is entirely in the hiring conversation: describe the actual sequence of a project, including the review steps and the timelines, and ask the candidate how that sounds. The ones who are honest about disliking it save everyone months.

## Writing Vacancies That Attract the Right People

Several practical adjustments make a measurable difference.

**Describe the constraints as substance, not as bureaucracy.** "You will build models that must withstand supervisory scrutiny, which means your validation work is part of the product" attracts a different and better candidate than a vacancy that omits the topic.

**State the timeline honestly.** If a model takes six months to reach production, say so. Candidates who need faster feedback will not apply, which is the desired outcome.

**Name the mission.** Regulated organisations have one genuine advantage over product companies: the work matters in a way that is easy to articulate. Patient flow, fraud affecting real customers, benefits reaching the right people. Use it.

**Be realistic about pay.** Public sector and healthcare salary scales cannot match commercial technology pay at senior levels. Compete on the other dimensions — hours, security, pension, the problem itself — rather than pretending the gap does not exist.

**Offer the governance career.** Model validation, algorithm governance and data protection roles are growing, are easier to fill than model development, and are attractive to experienced people who want influence without on-call duty.

## Building Capability When You Cannot Compete on Salary

Three strategies work in practice for regulated Dutch organisations.

**Grow from adjacent internal talent.** Clinical data analysts, actuarial staff, policy analysts and information managers often have deep domain knowledge and can be developed technically. This is slower than hiring and produces better-fitting people.

**Partner rather than build everything.** Collaborations with universities, applied research institutes and university medical centres give access to expertise that no single organisation can employ, and Dutch research institutions are generally willing.

**Make the work genuinely interesting.** The problems in healthcare capacity, energy grid management and public service delivery are more interesting than most commercial machine learning. Organisations that give people real ownership and publish their work attract candidates who would otherwise never consider them.

The one strategy that consistently fails is hiring a single senior person and expecting them to build a capability alone inside a governance structure they do not yet understand.

## Onboarding for a Governance Environment

Hiring the right person is half the task; the first three months determine whether they stay. Regulated organisations that onboard well do a few specific things.

**Explain the machinery early.** Who approves what, in what order, and why. A new engineer who understands that the privacy officer, the information security assessment and the clinical or risk committee each answer a different question stops experiencing them as obstacles.

**Introduce them to the reviewers as people.** A relationship with the risk officer or data protection officer, formed before the first submission, changes a review from an adversarial process into a collaborative one.

**Give them one small end-to-end project first.** Something genuinely useful but low-risk, taken all the way through the governance process. They learn the route in miniature, and the organisation learns whether the person can navigate it.

**Pair them with domain expertise.** A clinician, an actuary, a policy specialist. In regulated environments the domain constrains the solution more than the technology does.

**Show them the archive.** Previous validation reports, model documentation and audit responses teach the expected standard faster than any explanation.

Organisations that skip this and hand a new hire a modelling task on day one typically discover the problem three months later, when the first submission is returned for the third time.

## The Advantage Regulated Employers Undersell

There is a recruitment argument that regulated organisations almost never make, and it is a strong one.

In a commercial technology company, a data professional's work is often measured by engagement metrics or revenue attribution, and a substantial share of projects are cancelled before anyone uses them. In a hospital, a grid operator, a supervisor or a public agency, the work is consequential by construction: a model that improves theatre scheduling releases operating capacity, a fraud model protects real customers, a benefits model affects whether people receive what they are entitled to.

Alongside that, these employers typically offer terms that commercial technology companies do not match: shorter contractual weeks, stronger pension arrangements, genuine acceptance of part-time work, more leave, and job security that has real value at certain stages of life.

Candidates increasingly weigh these dimensions, particularly experienced professionals with families who have already discovered that a higher salary does not compensate for a poor week. The mistake regulated employers make is competing on the axis where they are weakest — salary — instead of the axes where they are strongest. Writing vacancies that lead with the problem, the hours and the security, rather than apologising for the scale, changes who applies.

## Key Takeaways

- Regulated environments change approval, documentation, change control and explainability — not the underlying technical work.
- Hire for validation instinct, documentation discipline, comfort with oversight and domain curiosity, alongside technical skill.
- Describe review steps and realistic timelines in the vacancy; candidates who dislike them should self-select out.
- Model validation and algorithm governance are growing career paths and are easier to recruit for than model development.
- Where salary cannot compete, grow internal talent, partner with research institutions, and compete on the substance of the work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: regulated employer losing hires quickly) Why do commercial hires leave?
Usually because the governance reality was not described in hiring, not because they lacked ability; describe review steps and timelines explicitly.

### (Scenario: employer competing on salary) How do we compete with technology companies?
On the substance of the problem, hours, security and pension — and by growing internal domain experts technically rather than only hiring externally.

### (Scenario: employer unsure what to screen for) What predicts success in a supervised environment?
Whether a candidate asks unprompted how a model would be validated, monitored and explained.

### (Scenario: employer with few applicants) Are there easier roles to fill?
Yes — model validation, algorithm governance and data protection roles attract experienced candidates and are less competitive than model development.

### (Scenario: employer planning a first hire) Should we hire one senior person to build the capability?
Rarely successful alone; pair the hire with internal domain expertise and, where possible, an external research or consultancy partner.
