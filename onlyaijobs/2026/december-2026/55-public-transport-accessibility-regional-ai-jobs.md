Every vacancy has a catchment area, and it is smaller than the map suggests. Research on commuting behaviour consistently shows that willingness to travel falls sharply beyond roughly forty-five minutes each way, and that the decline is steeper for lower-paid roles and for people with caring responsibilities. In a country as compact as the Netherlands, that threshold should include a great deal. In practice, the last kilometre between a railway station and a business park often decides whether it does.

## Why Accessibility Determines the Candidate Pool

A regional employer competing for scarce technical staff has three levers: pay, the work itself, and how easy it is to get there. The third is the one most often ignored and the cheapest to improve.

Consider two employers in the same province. One sits beside a station with four trains an hour. The other sits on a business park six kilometres from the nearest station, served by a bus every half hour that does not run in the evening. The first can recruit from every city on the line. The second can recruit only from people with a car, which excludes a meaningful share of younger candidates, some international arrivals who have not yet obtained a licence or vehicle, and anyone whose household has one car and two jobs.

The difference is not marginal. It can halve the effective candidate pool for a role that is already hard to fill.

## The Last Kilometre Problem

Dutch rail connectivity between cities is genuinely good. The weakness is the final stretch, and it is concentrated exactly where employment sits.

Industrial estates, logistics parks and manufacturing sites were located for land, road access and freight logic rather than for passenger accessibility. Many are two to eight kilometres from a station — too far to walk, awkward by bus, entirely reasonable by bicycle in good weather and unpleasant in a Dutch January.

Several practical instruments address this, and none require new infrastructure.

**Shared bicycles at stations**, which in the Netherlands is a well-established and heavily used solution, provided enough are available at peak times at the relevant station.

**Employer-provided bicycles** kept at the station, a simple arrangement several regional employers use.

**Business park shuttle services**, jointly funded by the companies on the park. Individually unaffordable, collectively cheap.

**Bus timing aligned with shift patterns** rather than with school hours, which requires the transport authority to know what those patterns are.

**Safe and lit cycle routes** between station and park, which is a municipal responsibility and frequently the missing piece.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Vacancy That Was a Transport Problem

A manufacturer on a business park struggles for eight months to fill a data analyst role. They raise the salary twice without effect.

An analysis of the applications shows a pattern: candidates within fifteen minutes by car apply and are largely unsuitable; suitable candidates live in two cities on the rail line, and all of them withdraw after asking about the commute. From the station it is a twenty-five minute walk or an unreliable bus, adding a full hour to the day in each direction.

The company buys six bicycles, keeps them in a locked shelter at the station, and states in the vacancy that the workplace is a seven-minute cycle from the station with a bicycle provided.

They fill the role in seven weeks. The intervention cost less than one month of the salary increase they had already granted.

This is not an unusual story. It is the most common form of hidden recruitment constraint among Dutch regional employers, and it is invisible unless somebody looks at where applicants live.

## What Regional Policy Can Do

**Map accessibility properly.** Travel-time analysis from residential areas to employment locations, by public transport and by bicycle, at the times people actually travel. This is standard analysis and rarely applied to employment sites specifically.

**Prioritise the last kilometre in mobility planning.** A modest investment in a cycle route or a shuttle can unlock a large employment area, and it competes well against larger road projects on cost per job made accessible.

**Coordinate transport concessions with employment patterns.** Bus timetables built around school and shopping trips serve employment poorly. Employers and transport authorities rarely speak directly; convening them is cheap.

**Include accessibility in business park development.** New employment areas without a plan for public transport and cycle access create a permanent recruitment handicap for every company that locates there.

**Recognise hybrid working.** If most office workers attend two or three days a week, peak demand patterns change, and services can be planned accordingly rather than assuming five-day flows.

**Consider it an equity issue.** Access to skilled employment without a car is not evenly distributed. Younger people, lower-income households and some international arrivals are affected disproportionately.

## What Employers Can Do Without Waiting

Regions move slowly; employers do not have to.

Provide bicycles at the station, or fund a shared arrangement with neighbouring companies. Offer a full public transport subscription rather than a per-kilometre allowance, which changes the calculation for someone deciding whether to own a car. Align start times with the actual train timetable rather than with tradition. Use hybrid policy deliberately, since two office days makes a longer commute acceptable that five days would not. And state the commute honestly in the vacancy, including the cycle time from the station — candidates who discover it later withdraw later, which wastes everyone's time.

There is also a reporting dimension: employers of a certain size in the Netherlands face obligations around reporting work-related mobility, and sustainability reporting increasingly covers commuting emissions. The measures that improve accessibility generally also improve those numbers, which makes the internal business case easier.

## The Wider Argument

Accessibility policy is usually justified in terms of congestion, emissions or equity. The labour market argument deserves equal weight, particularly in regions worried about skills shortages.

A region that makes its employment locations reachable within forty-five minutes by public transport and bicycle expands the effective labour pool of every employer within it, without building anything new or spending money on recruitment. It also retains people who would otherwise move closer to a more accessible job elsewhere, and it makes the regional labour market function as one market rather than several disconnected ones.

For a province weighing a mobility investment against a talent attraction campaign, the accessibility measure is frequently the better purchase — and unlike a campaign, it keeps working after the budget ends.

## Key Takeaways

- A vacancy's real candidate pool is everyone who can reach it in about forty-five minutes, and the last kilometre often decides that.
- Business parks distant from stations with poor bus service effectively restrict hiring to people with cars, halving some candidate pools.
- Cheap interventions work: station bicycles, shared shuttles, timetable alignment with shifts, safe cycle routes.
- Employers can act immediately with bicycles, transport subscriptions, start-time alignment and honest commute descriptions.
- Accessibility investment expands every regional employer's candidate pool permanently, unlike a recruitment campaign.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer with a long-open vacancy) Could the commute be the problem?
Frequently yes — check where applicants live and where suitable candidates withdraw; the last kilometre from the station is a common cause.

### (Scenario: municipality planning mobility) What is the cheapest effective measure?
Improving the station-to-business-park link with cycle infrastructure, shared bicycles or a jointly funded shuttle.

### (Scenario: transport authority) Why do bus timetables fail commuters?
They are often built around school and shopping patterns rather than shift and office start times, which employers are rarely asked about.

### (Scenario: employer unable to wait for policy) What can we do ourselves?
Provide bicycles at the station, offer full public transport subscriptions, align start times with trains, and describe the commute honestly.

### (Scenario: province comparing investments) Is accessibility better value than a talent campaign?
Often — it permanently expands every local employer's candidate pool, while a campaign stops working when the budget ends.
