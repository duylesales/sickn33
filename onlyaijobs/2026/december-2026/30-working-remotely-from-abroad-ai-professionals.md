"Can I work from my parents' place in Spain for a month?" is one of the most common questions in Dutch technology companies, and one of the most inconsistently answered. Some employers say yes without blinking. Others allow a strict maximum of twenty days a year. A few refuse entirely. The variation is not arbitrary — it reflects genuinely different risk exposures, and understanding them will help you ask for what you want in a way an employer can actually approve.

This article is general information, not legal or tax advice; rules differ by country and change.

## The Four Risks Your Employer Is Managing

**Social security.** Within the EU, EEA and Switzerland, you are normally covered by the social security system of one country at a time. Working substantially from another member state can shift that coverage, with consequences for contributions, pensions and benefits. An A1 certificate documents which system applies, and a European framework agreement provides a route for cross-border teleworkers to remain covered in the employer's country within defined limits — but it must be applied for, and it does not cover every situation.

**Personal income tax.** Where you pay tax depends on residence and on where work is physically performed, subject to tax treaties. Short stays usually change nothing; extended stays can create a tax liability in the other country, and in some cases an obligation for the employer to operate payroll there.

**Permanent establishment.** This is the risk employers fear most and candidates understand least. If an employee's activity in another country is deemed to create a taxable presence for the company — particularly for roles involving contracts, sales or management — the company may face corporate tax obligations in that country. The exposure is disproportionate to any benefit from letting one engineer work abroad for a month, which is why policies are often conservative.

**Security, data protection and export control.** Access to production data and code from another country raises questions about lawful transfer outside the EU, about physical security of the working environment, and in some sectors about export-controlled technology. Defence, critical infrastructure and regulated financial work often prohibit foreign access outright.

## Why Policies Differ So Much

A startup with fifteen employees may have no policy and permit anything until an accountant intervenes. A multinational usually has a formal policy with an approval workflow, country lists and day limits, because it has been advised on exactly the risks above. A bank, hospital or defence contractor may forbid working from abroad for most roles.

Sector and role matter as much as employer size. An engineer with no customer contact working on internal systems presents a different profile from someone negotiating contracts. Within the same company, two people may legitimately receive different answers.

What is unreasonable is an employer that refuses without explanation, or one that quietly tolerates the practice without a policy — the latter is worse for you, because if something goes wrong, the absence of approval falls on the employee.

## Full Remote From Another Country Is a Different Question

There is a categorical difference between working from abroad temporarily and being permanently employed in another country.

If you want to live in Portugal and work for a Dutch employer indefinitely, the realistic options are: the employer has or establishes a legal entity there, the employer uses an employer-of-record service, or you become self-employed and invoice. Each has consequences for cost, employment protection, pension accrual and your own administration, and the employer's costs may exceed what your Dutch contract implies.

Many Dutch employers say no to this for entirely rational reasons. Candidates who want to work abroad long term are usually better served by targeting employers that are already distributed and have solved these problems, rather than persuading one that has not.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Month That Became a Tax Question

An engineer works from another EU country for six weeks over the summer, arranged informally with their manager. The following spring, the employer's payroll adviser reviews travel patterns and raises a question, because the stay combined with earlier visits approached thresholds that could trigger obligations abroad.

Nothing dramatic happens: the periods are documented, advice is taken, and the outcome is a policy with clear limits. But the episode consumes weeks of internal attention and the engineer's request the following year is refused pending a formal framework.

The avoidable part was the informality. A request submitted in advance, with dates, country and a note that the person will not conclude contracts or represent the company there, is straightforward for an employer to assess. The same request made retroactively is a compliance incident.

## What a Good Request Looks Like

Employers approve what they can assess. A request that includes the following is dramatically more likely to succeed:

- The country, exact dates and total days abroad this calendar year.
- Confirmation that you will remain contactable during Dutch working hours, or a proposal for overlapping hours.
- Confirmation that you will not meet clients, sign anything, or represent the company in that country.
- A statement about your working environment: secure network, no shared workspace where screens are visible, company device only.
- A note on data: whether your role requires access to personal data or restricted systems, and whether any access restrictions apply from outside the Netherlands.
- Willingness to complete any A1 or equivalent formalities the employer requires.

This turns a vague favour into a documented, low-risk decision. It also signals professional judgement, which tends to make future requests easier.

## The Practical Side Nobody Plans For

**Time zones.** A two-hour difference is manageable; a seven-hour difference changes your job. Deep work may improve while collaboration suffers, and colleagues quietly notice who is available.

**Working conditions.** Dutch occupational health rules concern themselves with your workstation. A kitchen table for six weeks is a genuine physical risk, and employers increasingly ask about it.

**Insurance and healthcare.** Coverage abroad may be limited, and the European health insurance card covers necessary care during temporary stays rather than everything.

**Equipment and connectivity.** Losing a laptop abroad, or working over an unfamiliar network, is an information security event, not an inconvenience.

**Team fairness.** Colleagues who cannot work abroad — because of children, care responsibilities or role constraints — notice when others routinely do. Good policies apply consistently rather than on the basis of who asks.

## Cross-Border Commuters Are a Separate Category

A sizeable group of people working in the Netherlands live in Germany or Belgium, or the reverse, and their situation is governed by specific arrangements rather than by ad hoc policy.

Border regions — around Maastricht and Aachen, Enschede and Münster, Zeeland and Flanders, Venlo and the Niederrhein — have long-standing cross-border labour markets. Tax treaties determine where employment income is taxed, social security coordination determines which system covers you, and dedicated advisory bodies exist in these regions to help people navigate the combination.

The complication that surprised many people in recent years is home working. A cross-border commuter who previously worked five days at the Dutch office now may work two days at home across the border, which can change the social security picture because of thresholds on the proportion of work performed in the country of residence. Frameworks have been developed to allow a degree of cross-border telework without shifting coverage, but they require action rather than assumption.

For a data professional considering a job in a border region — and there are excellent employers in all of them — the practical advice is to raise the topic explicitly during negotiation. Employers in these regions are generally experienced with it; employers elsewhere hiring someone who happens to live across the border may not be.

## How to Evaluate an Employer's Remote Policy Before You Join

If flexibility matters to you, evaluate it during the hiring process rather than discovering the reality afterwards. Useful questions, in order of how much they reveal:

**Is the policy written down?** An unwritten policy is a manager's discretion, which changes when your manager does.

**How many days abroad per year are permitted, and in which countries?** Specific numbers indicate the employer has taken advice. Vagueness indicates they have not.

**Who approves, and how long does it take?** A workflow that requires three levels of approval six weeks in advance is a different proposition from a manager's sign-off.

**Has anyone actually done it?** Ask for an example. Policies that exist on paper but have never been used are common.

**What about the rest of the team?** If some roles can and others cannot, how is that handled? Unequal flexibility within a team is a persistent source of resentment.

**What is expected on availability?** Some employers care only about output; others expect overlap with Dutch office hours. Both are reasonable, but they are different jobs.

A candidate who asks these questions calmly comes across as someone who understands employment reality, not as someone planning to disappear. And the answers tell you a great deal about how the organisation treats its people generally — because remote policy is where stated values and actual management practice tend to diverge most visibly.

## Hybrid Within the Netherlands Is the Bigger Lever

While working from abroad attracts the most attention, the arrangement that changes most people's lives is far more mundane: how many days per week you must be in a Dutch office, and where that office is.

The country's geography makes this decisive. A two-day-per-week office requirement transforms which employers are realistic for you. Someone living in Zwolle can reasonably work for an employer in Amsterdam two days a week and would struggle five. That single policy difference opens or closes half the national labour market, and it costs the employer nothing.

It also interacts with housing. Many people who cannot afford a Randstad rent can afford a house in a regional city, and a hybrid arrangement is what makes the combination viable. This is one of the quiet structural shifts in the Dutch labour market since remote work normalised, and it benefits regional employers as much as employees.

Before accepting a role, get the actual expectation in writing: number of days, whether they are fixed or flexible, whether team-wide anchor days exist, and whether the policy is set centrally or by your manager. Policies stated as "hybrid" mean anything from one day a month to four days a week, and the difference determines where you can live.

## Key Takeaways

- Employers restrict working from abroad because of social security, income tax, permanent establishment and security risks, not to be difficult.
- Within the EU, A1 certificates and the cross-border telework framework provide routes, but they must be applied for in advance.
- Permanent relocation while keeping a Dutch contract is a different question, usually requiring an entity, an employer-of-record or self-employment.
- A specific, documented request — country, dates, no client contact, secure environment — is far more likely to be approved.
- Informal arrangements shift the risk onto the employee if something later goes wrong.

## Where to Start

Ask for your employer's written policy before booking anything. If none exists, propose a specific plan rather than a general question — that is how policies get written in your favour.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employee planning a summer abroad) How long can I work from another EU country?
It depends on your employer's policy and on social security and tax thresholds; many employers set limits of a few weeks per year and require advance approval.

### (Scenario: employee who did not ask permission) What is the risk of going informally?
If an issue arises later, the lack of approval falls on you, and it can jeopardise future requests and in some cases the employment relationship.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting to relocate permanently) Can I keep my Dutch contract and live abroad?
Usually not without an entity, an employer-of-record arrangement or self-employment; target already-distributed employers instead.

### (Scenario: candidate in regulated work) Why is my employer stricter than others?
Financial, healthcare, defence and critical infrastructure employers often restrict foreign access for data protection, supervisory and export control reasons.

### (Scenario: employee writing a request) What makes approval likely?
Specific dates and country, no client contact or contract signing, a secure working environment, and willingness to complete A1 formalities.
