Dutch regional policy has a well-developed instrument set. Regional agreements between national government and regions fund projects around economic strength and quality of life, provinces run their own digitalisation and innovation agendas, and municipalities add local programmes. Substantial sums move through these channels every year, a meaningful share of it aimed at digitalisation and the adoption of new technology by regional businesses. What these programmes rarely address directly is whether the jobs they create can be filled.

## What These Instruments Are Good At

Regional funding programmes in the Netherlands do several things well, and it is worth being specific before criticising the gap.

They convene. Getting a province, several municipalities, a development agency, a university of applied sciences and twenty employers into a shared programme is genuinely difficult, and these instruments do it.

They fund experimentation that would not otherwise happen — field labs, pilot projects, shared facilities that no single SME could finance.

They think in terms of broad prosperity rather than only economic output, which brings housing, education, accessibility and quality of life into the same conversation as business support. That framing is unusually well suited to the talent question, though it is seldom applied to it.

And they operate on multi-year horizons, which is what labour market change actually requires.

## The Assumption That Quietly Fails

Most digitalisation programmes rest on an implicit chain of reasoning: fund the technology, the companies adopt it, adoption creates skilled jobs, skilled jobs raise regional prosperity.

Each link is plausible. The third is where the chain breaks.

A regional SME that adopts a data-driven approach needs someone to run it. If that person cannot be found, one of three things happens. The company reverts to its previous way of working and the investment is written off. It outsources permanently, in which case the value accrues largely outside the region. Or it recruits successfully at a price that only the larger companies can afford, leaving the smaller ones — the intended beneficiaries — behind.

None of these outcomes appear clearly in programme evaluations, which typically count projects completed and companies reached rather than capability retained.

## What to Add, Without a New Programme

The practical recommendation is not to create another instrument. It is to add a talent dimension to the ones that exist.

**Ask the staffing question at application.** Any funded digitalisation project should answer: who will operate this afterwards, do they already work here, and if not, how will they be found? This alone improves project design.

**Fund people alongside technology.** A share of programme budget allocated to traineeships, retraining or shared staff across several SMEs frequently produces more durable capability than the same money spent on pilots.

**Make regional demand visible as a public good.** A programme that publishes the aggregated technical vacancies of its participating companies gives candidates something a single SME cannot: evidence that the region has a market, not one job.

**Invest in vocational and applied education, not only universities.** Regional digitalisation depends disproportionately on practically trained people, and the vocational and applied institutions are usually closer to regional SMEs than research universities are.

**Support lifelong learning concretely.** Many of the people who will operate these systems already work in the region, in adjacent roles. Retraining an experienced planner or process engineer is often faster and more reliable than recruiting externally.

**Measure capability retained, not projects completed.** Twelve months after a project ends, is the capability still in the company, and who operates it?

## A Concrete Scenario: Two Regions, Two Outcomes

Two regions run comparable SME digitalisation programmes with comparable budgets.

The first funds twenty pilot projects. Eighteen are completed. At evaluation, seven companies still use what was built; the others lack anyone to maintain it.

The second funds fifteen pilots and uses the remaining budget for a shared traineeship: twelve graduates placed in participating companies for two years, with a part-time technical coach supporting them collectively and a peer network so the trainees are not isolated as the only technical person in their organisation.

At evaluation, thirteen companies still use what was built, nine trainees have been offered permanent positions, and the region has a cohort of young professionals with local ties who now know each other.

The second programme spent the same money and produced durable capability rather than a set of completed projects. The difference was treating people as infrastructure rather than as a company-level input.

## Broad Prosperity Makes This Easier to Justify

The shift in Dutch regional policy towards broad prosperity — considering health, housing, education, environment and social cohesion alongside economic indicators — provides a natural framework for the talent question.

A person who can find skilled work near where they live, without a ninety-minute commute, experiences better health, more time with family and a lower environmental footprint. Their employer retains knowledge locally. The region retains a resident who might otherwise have moved to the Randstad.

Framed this way, making regional skilled work visible is not an economic development activity competing for budget against social objectives. It contributes to several of them simultaneously, which is precisely the argument these programmes are designed to reward.

The practical implication for programme managers is that the talent dimension can be written into a broad prosperity framework without inventing a new justification — the justification is already there.

## Honest Limitations

Three caveats, because regional policy is full of overclaiming.

Visibility is not supply. If a region genuinely has too few people with a skill, making vacancies visible does not create them. It captures latent supply — people already there, people commuting out, people who would return — which is usually larger than assumed, but it is not infinite.

Education pipelines take years. Investment in vocational and applied programmes pays off over a five-to-ten-year horizon, which exceeds most political cycles and must be defended accordingly.

And regions compete with each other. A successful regional talent initiative partly redistributes people from elsewhere in the Netherlands. That is a legitimate regional objective, but it should not be presented as national value creation.

Being honest about these limits makes the case for the achievable parts considerably stronger.

## Designing a Shared Traineeship That Works

Because the traineeship model recurs as the highest-return intervention, it is worth setting out what distinguishes the ones that succeed from the ones that quietly disband.

**Cohort, not placements.** Trainees placed individually in SMEs become the only technical person in their organisation, which is isolating and a common reason they leave. A cohort that meets regularly — a day a month, a shared channel, joint training — gives them peers and makes the experience survivable.

**A technical coach.** One experienced part-time professional supporting the whole cohort answers the questions that a trainee's own employer cannot, and prevents the situation where a twenty-four-year-old is the most senior data person in a company of eighty.

**Employer commitment beyond money.** Participating companies should name a sponsor, commit to a defined first project, and agree that the trainee is not to be used as general IT support. Without this, some placements will drift into whatever work is urgent.

**A realistic duration.** Two years is the common shape: long enough to deliver something real and to build a relationship, short enough for a graduate to commit to.

**An explicit path afterwards.** Both the trainee and the employer should know before it starts whether a permanent role is the expected outcome. Ambiguity at the end wastes the relationship built.

**Selection for fit rather than prestige.** Trainees with local roots, or a reason to want to live in the region, stay. Recruiting nationally for the strongest CVs and placing them in a region they have no connection to produces high-quality, short-lived placements.

## The Reporting Problem, and How to Solve It

Programme managers face a practical obstacle: talent outcomes are harder to report than project outputs. A completed pilot is a photograph and a paragraph; a labour market improvement is a trend over years.

Three approaches make this tractable.

**Use existing statistics.** National statistics provide regional employment, commuting and educational data that can be tracked over the programme's life without commissioning new research. The change will be slow, and that should be stated openly rather than hidden.

**Collect a small panel.** Twenty participating companies, asked the same three questions twice a year — open technical vacancies, average time to fill, whether capability from previous projects is still operating — produce a defensible indicator set at almost no cost.

**Report intermediate outcomes honestly.** A traineeship that placed twelve people, of whom nine received permanent offers, is a result worth reporting even before regional statistics move. Overclaiming a causal effect on provincial employment figures is what damages credibility, not modest reporting.

The broader point is that these programmes are usually evaluated against what is easy to count. Programme managers who choose better indicators at the design stage, rather than at the evaluation stage, get credit for the work that actually mattered.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch regional funding instruments are good at convening, experimentation and long horizons, and rarely address staffing directly.
- The implicit chain from technology funding to skilled regional jobs breaks at the point where a company cannot find anyone.
- Add a talent dimension to existing instruments rather than creating new ones: staffing questions at application, funded traineeships, visible regional demand.
- Vocational and applied education and retraining of existing workers matter more than university pipelines for regional SMEs.
- Broad prosperity framing already contains the justification; visibility captures latent supply but does not create people.
