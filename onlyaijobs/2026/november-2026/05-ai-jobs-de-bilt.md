Every Dutch weather forecast, every official heat warning and every climate scenario used by water boards and municipalities can be traced back to a single location on the eastern edge of Utrecht. De Bilt is where the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI) is based, and neighbouring Bilthoven has for decades been the home of the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM). Together they make this quiet, green municipality one of the most important places in the country for scientific data work — and a place where machine learning is currently changing long-established methods.

## Why Weather Forecasting Is Having a Machine Learning Moment

For decades, weather forecasting relied on numerical weather prediction: physical equations of the atmosphere solved on supercomputers. Machine learning played a supporting role, for example in post-processing model output. That balance is shifting. In recent years, data-driven weather models trained on decades of reanalysis data have shown forecast skill comparable to physics-based models for many variables, at a fraction of the computational cost. The **European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)**, of which the Netherlands is a member state, has developed its own machine-learning-based forecasting system alongside its physics-based models.

For national meteorological services such as KNMI, this raises fundamental questions. How reliable are data-driven models in extreme events that are rare in historical data? How should physical models and machine learning be combined? How do you communicate uncertainty in a warning when two types of model disagree? These are open scientific and operational questions, and they create demand for people who understand both atmospheric science and modern machine learning.

## The Named Organisations Behind De Bilt's Scientific Data Market

**Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI).** KNMI is the national institute for weather, climate and seismology. It issues weather forecasts and warnings, conducts climate research, produces the national climate scenarios and monitors earthquakes, including induced earthquakes in the Groningen gas field region. KNMI also operates observation networks and makes a large amount of data available through its open data platform.

**Cabauw Experimental Site for Atmospheric Research (CESAR).** Near Cabauw, in the municipality of Lopik, KNMI operates a tall measurement tower and an observatory where atmospheric processes are measured in detail, in collaboration with other research institutes. It is one of the most important atmospheric research sites in Europe.

**National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM).** RIVM supports the government on public health, infectious disease control, vaccination programmes, environmental quality and safety. It operates monitoring networks such as the national air quality monitoring network and produces models and analyses used in national policy. RIVM has long been associated with Bilthoven and has been moving to a new building at Utrecht Science Park, so check the work location stated in each vacancy.

**European partners.** KNMI participates in **EUMETSAT**, the European organisation for meteorological satellites, and in ECMWF. That means much of the data and model infrastructure used in De Bilt is European in scale.

**Utrecht University and Utrecht Science Park.** Utrecht University has strong research in climate physics, earth sciences, epidemiology and data science, and Utrecht Science Park hosts university, medical centre and institute activities within a short distance of De Bilt.

## The National Climate Scenarios as a Data Product

The **KNMI'23 climate scenarios** describe possible futures for the Dutch climate — temperature, precipitation, sea level rise and extremes — under different emission pathways. They are used by governments, water boards, insurers and infrastructure managers to plan adaptation.

Producing such scenarios is a data-intensive process: global climate model output must be translated to the scale of a small, low-lying country, and statistical methods determine how rare extreme events are represented. Machine learning is increasingly explored for emulating expensive model runs and for downscaling. The high stakes — dike heights and drainage capacity depend on these numbers — make methodological transparency essential.

## Regulation and Data Policy

**EU Open Data Directive (EU) 2019/1024.** Meteorological data is designated as a category of high-value datasets that public bodies should make available free of charge in machine-readable form. This supports a broad ecosystem of companies building services on public weather data, and it means much of KNMI's work is published openly.

**General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).** Weather and climate data are rarely personal data, but public health data often is — and health data is a special category. Work at a public health institute involves strict rules on pseudonymisation, access and purpose limitation.

**Public Health Act (Wet publieke gezondheid).** This act provides the framework for infectious disease control in the Netherlands, including notification of certain diseases, which shapes the data available for surveillance.

**Government standards.** As public bodies, KNMI and RIVM follow government information security baselines and expectations on algorithm transparency, including the Dutch Algorithm Register for relevant applications.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Scientific research is partly exempt, but AI systems used in public services, particularly those affecting people's access to services or safety decisions, must be assessed against the AI Act's requirements.

## A Concrete Scenario: Nowcasting a Summer Downpour

On a warm summer afternoon, thunderstorms develop quickly over the south of the country. Traditional nowcasting extrapolates the movement of precipitation observed on radar, but it struggles to predict where new cells will form. A deep learning model trained on years of radar images predicts the next ninety minutes with visibly sharper patterns.

The operational meteorologist, however, asks whether the model systematically underestimates the most intense rainfall — precisely the events that flood tunnels and streets. Evaluation shows the model performs well on average but smooths out peaks. The team combines it with probabilistic post-processing and presents both to forecasters. The lesson is typical for this field: average skill is not enough when the purpose of a warning is to catch the rare, dangerous event.

## A Common Misconception About Scientific Institutes

Some candidates assume that working at a national institute means pure academic research with no operational pressure. At KNMI and RIVM, the opposite is often true: weather warnings must go out on time, surveillance figures inform ministers, and models run in operational chains every day. The work combines scientific depth with production responsibility — closer to operating critical infrastructure than to writing papers alone, although publishing research is also part of the job.

## Skills and Tools That Stand Out

- **Scientific computing.** Python with NumPy, xarray and Dask; familiarity with NetCDF, GRIB and high-performance computing environments.
- **Statistics for rare events.** Extreme value theory, probabilistic forecasting and proper scoring rules for evaluating uncertain predictions.
- **Spatio-temporal machine learning.** Models for gridded data, radar sequences and irregular station networks.
- **Physical understanding.** Enough atmospheric, climate or epidemiological knowledge to recognise when a model violates physical or biological plausibility.
- **Operational software engineering.** Reproducible pipelines, testing and monitoring for models that run every day.
- **Communication.** Explaining uncertainty to forecasters, policymakers and the public.

## Why the Location Works for Utrecht-Based Professionals

De Bilt borders the city of Utrecht, and many people working at KNMI cycle or take the bus from Utrecht. Utrecht Science Park is nearby, and Utrecht Central Station connects the area to the rest of the country. For professionals who live in Utrecht, Amersfoort or Zeist and assume that scientific data work requires working in Amsterdam or Delft, De Bilt is a short commute away. The broader Utrecht region also hosts private weather companies, climate risk consultancies and insurers that use weather data, giving candidates options beyond the public sector.

## Career Growth in Weather, Climate and Public Health Data

A career in this field often combines scientific credibility with operational skills. Some professionals grow into senior researcher or model development lead roles; others move into private weather and climate risk companies, insurers, energy traders or infrastructure managers who increasingly need people who understand weather and climate data. Public health data experience likewise transfers to hospitals, health insurers and research organisations. Because climate adaptation and energy transition will drive demand for years, weather and climate data skills are among the more future-proof specialisations in the Dutch AI market.

## Open Data You Can Use to Demonstrate Relevant Skills

Few fields make it as easy to build a credible portfolio as weather and climate. KNMI publishes a large amount of observational and model data through its open data platform, including station observations and radar-based precipitation products. The Copernicus Climate Change Service provides the ERA5 reanalysis, a consistent historical reconstruction of global weather that is widely used for training and evaluating data-driven models. Public health and environmental data, such as air quality measurements, are also published by Dutch public bodies.

A strong portfolio project for this market is modest in scope but rigorous in method. Examples include a precipitation nowcasting model evaluated with proper probabilistic scores, a comparison of statistical and machine learning post-processing for temperature forecasts at Dutch stations, or an analysis of how well a model captures extreme rainfall rather than average conditions. What distinguishes a strong project is not the size of the model but whether you evaluated it the way a meteorologist would.

## Working Conditions at National Institutes

KNMI and RIVM are agencies of Dutch ministries, and staff generally fall under the collective labour agreement for central government (CAO Rijk). That has practical consequences candidates should understand. Salary scales are published, which makes pay transparent before you apply. Working hours, leave, pension through ABP and a personal development budget are regulated centrally. Salaries for senior technical specialists may be lower than at commercial tech companies, but job security, work-life balance and the societal relevance of the work are frequently mentioned as reasons people stay for many years.

Some roles involve operational duties, such as participation in weather service rotations or crisis response during infectious disease outbreaks. Ask about this explicitly if it matters to you.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **Does the model I would work on run operationally, or is it in a research phase?** The answer determines how much production engineering the role involves.
- **How are machine learning methods evaluated against the current physics-based or statistical approach?** Mature teams have clear verification standards.
- **Who are the users of the output — forecasters, policymakers, the public?** Different users require different communication of uncertainty.
- **What computing infrastructure is available?** Access to high-performance computing or European infrastructure affects what is feasible.
- **How is scientific publication balanced with operational delivery?** Institutes differ in how much time is reserved for research.
