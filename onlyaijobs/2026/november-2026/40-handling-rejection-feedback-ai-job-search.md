A graduate applies to thirty data science roles, hears nothing from half of them, receives fifteen polite rejections and gets two interviews that end in a "we have chosen another candidate". It is easy to conclude that something is fundamentally wrong: the degree is not good enough, the market is closed, the dream of working in AI was unrealistic. In most cases, none of that is true. Rejection is a normal, statistical part of any job search, particularly for junior roles that attract many applicants. What matters is how you interpret rejections, what information you extract from them and how you adjust your approach while keeping momentum.

## Why Rejections Happen — Often for Reasons You Cannot See

Many rejections have little to do with the candidate's quality.

- **Volume.** Popular junior roles can attract very large numbers of applications, and employers can invite only a few.
- **Internal candidates.** A position may be filled by an internal applicant or someone already known to the team.
- **Changed plans.** Budgets are frozen, priorities change or the role is redefined after publication.
- **Specific requirements.** The team may need a very specific skill or domain background not emphasised in the vacancy.
- **Timing.** Another candidate was further along in the process or available sooner.
- **Screening filters.** Applicant tracking systems or screening questions filter out applications on criteria such as language, location or availability.

Other rejections do reflect gaps in skills, experience or presentation — but it is often impossible to know which type of rejection you received without asking.

## Understanding Where in the Process You Are Rejected

| Stage of rejection | What it may indicate | What to adjust |
|---|---|---|
| No response or rejection after applying | CV not matching the role, strong competition or screening filters | CV tailoring, choice of roles, networking, application channels |
| Rejection after first conversation | Communication, motivation or fit concerns | Preparation, explaining projects, research on the organisation |
| Rejection after technical assessment | Specific skill gaps or approach | Targeted practice, evaluation methods, code quality |
| Rejection after final round | Close competition, team fit or specific experience | Often little to change; ask for feedback and stay in contact |

Tracking at which stage rejections occur is more informative than counting rejections overall. Many rejections at the first stage point to a CV or targeting problem; rejections after assessments point to skill development.

## How to Ask for Feedback Effectively

Many employers do not give detailed feedback automatically, especially after early stages, but candidates who ask politely often receive something useful — particularly after interviews.

**Ask soon, briefly and specifically.** Send a short message within a few days of the rejection. Thank the interviewer, express continued interest in the organisation and ask one or two specific questions, for example: "Which part of my technical assessment could have been stronger?" or "Was there a specific skill or experience that made the difference for the selected candidate?"

**Make it easy to answer.** A short call of ten minutes or two sentences by email is more likely to be granted than a request for extensive feedback.

**Do not argue.** The purpose is learning, not reversing the decision. Arguing closes the door to future opportunities.

**Stay in touch when appropriate.** If feedback suggests you were close, ask whether you may apply again in the future or be considered for other roles.

## Your Rights as a Candidate in the Netherlands

**NVP Recruitment Code.** Many Dutch employers follow this code, which includes principles such as informing candidates about the procedure, handling applications within reasonable timeframes and providing reasons for rejection upon request.

**Right of access under the GDPR.** Under Article 15 of the GDPR, you can request access to personal data an employer processes about you, which can include assessment results and interview notes, subject to limitations such as protecting others' rights.

**Automated decisions.** If a rejection is based solely on automated processing that significantly affects you, Article 22 of the GDPR gives you rights, including to obtain human intervention and contest the decision. Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems used in recruitment to filter or evaluate candidates are high-risk, with requirements for human oversight and transparency.

**Equal treatment.** If you believe you were rejected because of a protected characteristic, such as religion, race, sex, nationality, sexual orientation, disability or age, you can seek advice and submit a request for an opinion to the **Netherlands Institute for Human Rights**.

**Data retention.** Employers should not retain application data longer than necessary; candidates can ask for their data to be deleted or consent to longer retention for future vacancies.

## Reading Feedback Correctly

Feedback is valuable but imperfect. Interviewers may give vague answers, avoid sensitive topics or describe the decision in general terms.

**Look for patterns.** One piece of feedback may reflect one interviewer's opinion; similar feedback from several employers indicates a real area for improvement.

**Separate skill feedback from fit feedback.** "You need more experience with deployment" is actionable; "we chose someone with more domain knowledge" may not require changing anything.

**Translate vague feedback into questions.** If feedback says "communication could be clearer", ask yourself whether you explained projects too technically, too long or without clear conclusions — and practise specifically.

**Do not overcorrect.** Changing your entire profile after one rejection can make your applications less focused.

## When You Hear Nothing at All

Silence after applying is frustrating and, unfortunately, common. It does not necessarily mean the application was not read. If a vacancy mentions a timeline, follow up politely once after that period has passed. If there is no timeline, one follow-up after two to three weeks is reasonable. After that, treat the application as unsuccessful and focus your energy elsewhere.

## Rejections After the Final Round: Staying on the Radar

A rejection after the final interview is often the most painful, because you invested the most time and came closest. It is also the rejection with the most potential for future opportunities. When several candidates reach the final round, the decision can depend on small differences, and employers often remember strong runners-up.

Respond graciously. Thank the team for the process, mention what you appreciated about the organisation and state that you would welcome being considered for future roles. Ask whether you may connect with the hiring manager on a professional network.

Keep in touch lightly. If the organisation publishes a new vacancy months later, mention your previous application and what you have learned since. In some cases, a selected candidate declines an offer or leaves during probation, and employers contact the runner-up directly.

Finally, reflect on the process itself. Reaching the final round means your profile and skills were competitive. Instead of changing everything, look for small improvements — perhaps in explaining business impact or asking sharper questions — and apply that confidence to your next applications.

## Adjusting Your Search Strategy

**Improve targeting.** Apply to fewer roles that fit well rather than many that fit partly. Consider employers in your region, sectors where your background is relevant and roles with hybrid titles, such as data analyst with modelling tasks.

**Use multiple channels.** Combine vacancies, specialist job boards, networking, university career services, meetups and open applications. Many smaller employers with data roles are not visible on large generalist platforms.

**Strengthen one weak point at a time.** If assessments are the problem, practise evaluation and code quality. If interviews are the problem, rehearse explaining projects. If the CV is the problem, rewrite it with results and tailor it.

**Consider stepping stones.** Internships, traineeships, analyst roles or data roles in adjacent sectors can build experience that opens doors later.

## Using Rejections to Refine Your Target List

Rejections also contain information about where you apply, not only how. If you apply mainly to large technology companies and scale-ups with hundreds of applicants per junior role, rejection rates will be high regardless of your quality. Many graduates overlook employers with less competition and genuine data needs: regional manufacturers, logistics companies, energy grid operators, water boards, hospitals, municipalities, insurers outside the Randstad and SMEs hiring their first data professional.

Review your log and ask a few questions. Which types of employers invited you to interviews? Which sectors responded positively to your background? Were you more successful with roles that combined analysis and modelling than with pure machine learning roles? Did applications to employers closer to your location perform better?

Adjusting your target list based on these patterns is often more effective than improving your CV further. A data science graduate from Eindhoven, for example, may find that applications to high-tech manufacturers and logistics companies in Brabant lead to more interviews than applications to Amsterdam scale-ups — and that the resulting roles offer more responsibility early on.

## Taking Care of Motivation

A long job search affects confidence and energy. Some practical habits help:

- **Set process goals** rather than outcome goals, such as a number of tailored applications or conversations per week.
- **Keep a log** of applications, stages and feedback to see progress beyond offers.
- **Continue a small project** that builds skills and gives you something new to discuss.
- **Talk to peers** who are also searching; shared experiences reduce isolation.
- **Use available support**, such as university career services for recent graduates or job search resources from the **UWV** for people receiving unemployment benefits.

## A Concrete Scenario: Twelve Rejections and One Pattern

A recent graduate with a master's degree in data science receives twelve rejections in two months. She creates a simple log and notices that she reaches the technical assessment stage in most cases but is rejected afterwards.

She asks three employers for feedback. Two mention that her models were strong but her evaluation lacked a baseline and discussion of limitations; one mentions that her code was difficult to follow. She spends three weeks improving her approach: structuring code, adding baselines and writing concise summaries. In her next four assessments, she reaches the final round three times and receives an offer from a regional energy company. The rejections contained the information she needed — once she collected and compared it.

## A Common Misconception About Rejection

The most common misconception is that rejection reflects personal worth or overall ability. In reality, many rejections are driven by factors outside the candidate's control, and even strong candidates receive many. A second misconception is that asking for feedback is inappropriate or annoying. When done politely and briefly, it is professional and often appreciated — and it sometimes leads to future opportunities.

## Key Takeaways

- Rejections in AI job searches are common and often caused by volume, internal candidates, changed plans or specific requirements.
- Track at which stage rejections occur to identify whether the issue lies in targeting, CV, interviews or assessments.
- Ask for feedback briefly and specifically, look for patterns and avoid overcorrecting.
- In the Netherlands, the NVP Recruitment Code, GDPR access and automated decision rights, the AI Act and equal treatment law protect candidates.
- Improve one weak point at a time, broaden channels, consider stepping stones and protect motivation with process goals and support.
