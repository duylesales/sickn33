In large parts of the Netherlands, companies that want a new or heavier connection to the electricity grid are placed on a waiting list. Housing developments, factories switching from gas to electricity and charging hubs for electric trucks all compete for limited grid capacity. At the same time, solar parks sometimes cannot feed in their full output on sunny days. Grid congestion has become one of the most pressing economic issues in the country — and one of the clearest places where data and AI can make an immediate difference. For data professionals who want work with visible societal impact, the energy transition is one of the strongest career choices in the Dutch market.

## Why the Grid Became a Data Problem

The traditional electricity system was relatively predictable: large power stations produced electricity, and consumption followed familiar daily and seasonal patterns. The energy transition changes both sides. Supply increasingly comes from solar and wind, which depend on weather. Demand grows and changes shape as heat pumps, electric vehicles, electric boilers and industrial electrification are added. And many of these new assets can, in principle, shift when they use or produce electricity.

Expanding grid capacity takes years, because cables, substations and transformers must be designed, permitted and built, and technical staff are scarce. In the meantime, operators must use existing capacity more intelligently. That requires knowing where and when congestion occurs, forecasting it, and organising flexibility from grid users to relieve it. Each of these steps is fundamentally a data problem.

## The Named Organisations in the Dutch Energy Data Landscape

**TenneT.** The national transmission system operator manages the high-voltage grid and is responsible for balancing supply and demand across the country. Its work includes forecasting, balancing markets, offshore grid connections and long-term grid planning.

**Regional grid operators.** **Alliander** (including Liander), **Stedin** and **Enexis** are the largest distribution system operators, managing medium- and low-voltage grids in most of the country, alongside several smaller operators. They face congestion most directly and invest heavily in data, digitalisation and grid monitoring.

**Netbeheer Nederland.** The association of Dutch grid operators coordinates sector-wide topics and publishes information about grid capacity, including maps showing where transport capacity is limited.

**GOPACS.** A platform established by Dutch grid operators to manage congestion by letting market parties offer flexibility — for example, by increasing production or reducing consumption in one area and compensating elsewhere.

**ElaadNL.** The knowledge and innovation centre for smart charging, established by Dutch grid operators, researches how electric vehicle charging can be integrated into the grid without overloading it.

**Energy suppliers and traders.** Companies such as Eneco, Vattenfall and Essent, along with many smaller suppliers, traders and aggregators, forecast demand, trade on electricity markets and increasingly offer flexibility services.

**Authority for Consumers and Markets (ACM).** The regulator sets rules for grid operators and energy markets, including rules on how grid capacity is allocated and how congestion management works.

## Core Technical Problems in Energy AI

**Forecasting.** Short-term forecasts of load, solar and wind production and electricity prices underpin almost everything: balancing, trading, congestion management and battery operation. Forecasts must be probabilistic, because decisions depend on the risk of extreme situations.

**Grid state estimation.** Low-voltage grids historically had few measurements. Operators estimate loads on cables and transformers from smart meter data, connection data and a limited number of sensors. Improving these estimates tells operators where they can safely connect more users.

**Flexibility optimisation.** Batteries, electric vehicles, heat pumps and industrial processes can shift consumption. Optimising when they charge or run — considering prices, grid constraints and user needs — is a classic optimisation problem with real-time and uncertainty components.

**Asset management.** Grid operators manage enormous numbers of cables, transformers and other assets. Predicting failures and prioritising replacement or reinforcement investments is a data-driven task with long time horizons.

**Grid planning.** Deciding where to reinforce the grid requires scenarios of future demand from electrification, housing and industry — combining spatial data, adoption forecasts and power flow analysis.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**Dutch Energy Act (Energiewet).** The new Energy Act replaces the Electricity Act 1998 and the Gas Act, modernising the legal framework for energy markets and grid operators, including rules on data management and the role of market participants.

**EU electricity market design reform.** Adopted in 2024, the reform strengthens rules on flexibility, long-term contracts and consumer protection in the EU electricity market.

**ACM codes and decisions.** The ACM sets technical and market codes, including rules for congestion management and prioritisation of grid connections, which directly affect the algorithms and processes grid operators use.

**GDPR.** Smart meter data can reveal household behaviour and is personal data. Access for analysis is regulated, and aggregation and privacy protection are essential.

**NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555.** Energy companies fall within the scope of stricter cybersecurity obligations, which affects how operational data is accessed and shared.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems used as safety components in the management and operation of critical infrastructure, including electricity supply, can be classified as high-risk, requiring risk management, documentation and human oversight.

## A Concrete Scenario: Connecting More Users to a Congested Area

A regional grid operator has declared an industrial area congested: no new large connections are possible for years. Several companies want to electrify. A data team analyses measured loads on the relevant substations over several years and discovers that peak loads occur during a limited number of hours per year.

With probabilistic forecasts and agreements with companies willing to reduce consumption during expected peaks, the operator can offer some companies a connection with conditions — for example, a non-firm or time-dependent capacity arrangement. The model is only part of the solution: contracts, regulatory rules, operational procedures and trust between companies and the operator are equally important. But without the data analysis, the capacity would have remained invisible.

## Batteries and the Growing Flexibility Market

Large batteries have become one of the fastest-growing assets in the Dutch electricity system. They can charge when electricity is abundant and cheap, discharge when prices are high, provide balancing services to the transmission operator and, in some cases, help relieve local congestion. Behind every battery is an optimisation problem: when to charge and discharge, in which market to offer capacity, and how to manage battery degradation over time.

This has created demand for optimisation engineers and quantitative analysts at battery developers, energy traders and aggregators. The work combines price forecasting under uncertainty, stochastic optimisation and detailed understanding of market rules. It is also an area where rules change frequently — for example, how grid tariffs apply to batteries or how batteries can participate in congestion management — so models must be adaptable.

For candidates, battery optimisation is an attractive entry point into energy because it uses familiar mathematical tools while teaching the fundamentals of electricity markets quickly.

## Working at a Grid Operator

Dutch grid operators are regulated companies owned by public shareholders, typically provinces and municipalities. Employment conditions are set by the collective labour agreement for grid companies, with published salary scales and generally good secondary conditions. Grid operators are also among the organisations hiring most actively in the country, because the energy transition requires both technical field staff and office-based specialists.

The working culture combines technical engineering traditions with a growing digital workforce. Data professionals often work closely with grid planners, asset managers and field engineers, and projects are linked to very concrete outcomes: which neighbourhood can be connected, which transformer must be replaced first, which industrial customer can electrify.

## A Portfolio Project That Speaks to Energy Employers

A strong portfolio project for this sector does not need proprietary data. Public electricity price data, weather data and published information about solar production allow projects such as a probabilistic day-ahead price forecast, a battery dispatch optimisation with realistic constraints, or a model estimating household load profiles with and without heat pumps and electric vehicles. What makes such a project convincing is evaluation: showing how forecasts perform during extreme situations, and how optimisation results change when forecasts are uncertain.

## A Common Misconception About Energy Data Work

Many candidates imagine energy data work as mainly about trading algorithms or renewable forecasting at startups. Those roles exist, but the largest demand is at grid operators, which are regulated, public-interest organisations with large technical workforces. Their data work is less glamorous in public perception but arguably more impactful: every improvement in grid utilisation can allow more homes, businesses and charging points to connect sooner.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Probabilistic forecasting.** Quantile regression, ensemble methods and evaluation with proper scoring rules.
- **Optimisation.** Linear, mixed-integer and stochastic optimisation for flexibility and planning problems.
- **Power systems basics.** Understanding voltage, capacity, power flow and grid topology — enough to talk with grid engineers.
- **Geospatial analysis.** Grid assets and future demand are strongly spatial.
- **Time series data engineering.** Handling large volumes of meter and sensor data reliably.
- **Energy market knowledge.** Day-ahead, intraday and balancing markets and how prices form.

## How to Transition Into Energy From Another Sector

Energy companies actively recruit data professionals from finance, logistics and technology, because optimisation and forecasting skills transfer well. The main learning curve is domain knowledge: grid physics, market rules and regulation. Candidates can prepare by studying publicly available grid capacity information, electricity market price data and open energy datasets, and by building a small forecasting or battery optimisation project. In interviews, showing curiosity about how the grid actually works is often more convincing than listing algorithms.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **How do data products influence operational or investment decisions?** The answer shows whether analytics reaches practice.
- **What measurement data is available for the low-voltage grid?** Data availability varies significantly.
- **How are regulatory changes reflected in the team's roadmap?** Energy regulation evolves rapidly.
- **How does the team work with grid engineers and operators?** Collaboration determines impact.
- **Which parts of the work count as critical infrastructure, and what does that mean for security?** This affects tooling and data access.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate unsure where energy data jobs are) Which employers hire data professionals for the energy transition?
Grid operators, energy suppliers, traders and aggregators, charging companies, industrial companies, consultancies and research institutes.

### (Scenario: data scientist new to energy) Do I need an electrical engineering background?
Not necessarily — forecasting and optimisation skills transfer well, but you must learn grid basics and market rules.

### (Scenario: candidate curious about grid congestion) How can data help with grid congestion?
By estimating actual grid loads, forecasting peaks, identifying spare capacity and organising flexibility through platforms such as GOPACS.

Yes — AI used as a safety component in critical infrastructure can be high-risk under the AI Act, and NIS2, GDPR and energy regulation also apply.

Often yes — they offer large-scale, impactful work, stable employment and substantial investment in data and digitalisation.
