National investment in artificial intelligence in the Netherlands has grown substantially over the past years. Programmes fund research, public-private labs, human capital initiatives and support for businesses adopting AI. Yet for many regional employers and job seekers, these investments remain abstract. A manufacturer in Twente or a logistics company in Limburg may never connect a national AI programme with its own difficulty in hiring a data engineer. A graduate in Groningen may not know that a lab involving local companies is working on exactly the kind of AI they studied. For regional AI coalitions, innovation hubs and knowledge networks, the question is how national investment translates into regional employment — and what is missing in between.

## The National AI Landscape: Named Programmes and Organisations

**AiNed.** AiNed is a multi-year national programme to strengthen the Dutch AI ecosystem, funded through the **National Growth Fund (Nationaal Groeifonds)**. It covers themes such as research and innovation, human capital, application of AI in sectors and responsible AI, with the aim of ensuring the Netherlands benefits economically and societally from AI.

**Dutch AI Coalition (NL AIC).** The coalition brings together companies, government, knowledge institutions and civil society organisations to accelerate AI development and application. It has worked with regional structures — often referred to as AI hubs — to connect national activities with regional ecosystems.

**ELSA Labs.** ELSA Labs focus on the ethical, legal and societal aspects of AI, developing human-centred AI in practice together with partners from government, business and society. Several labs are funded through national programmes in which the **Dutch Research Council (NWO)** plays a role.

**Innovation Center for Artificial Intelligence (ICAI).** ICAI is a national network of labs in which knowledge institutions collaborate with companies, government organisations and other partners on AI research, often involving PhD candidates working on problems from partner organisations.

**TNO.** The applied research organisation works on AI applications and responsible AI with public and private partners.

**European Digital Innovation Hubs (EDIHs).** Funded under the EU's **Digital Europe Programme** and national co-funding, EDIHs help SMEs and public organisations adopt digital technologies including AI, through services such as test-before-invest, training and support in finding investment. Several EDIHs operate in the Netherlands with regional focuses.

## How National Investment Creates Regional Employment

| Mechanism | How it creates jobs or talent | Typical regional effect |
|---|---|---|
| Research funding and labs | PhD, postdoc and research engineer positions | Research talent in university cities |
| Public-private labs | Collaboration with companies on AI problems | Company-specific expertise and potential hires |
| Human capital programmes | Education, training and upskilling initiatives | Broader AI skills in the regional workforce |
| SME adoption support | Companies start AI projects and need skills | New data and AI roles at regional SMEs |
| Responsible AI programmes | Governance, ethics and compliance expertise | Roles in public organisations and regulated sectors |
| Ecosystem building | Events, networks and regional hubs | Stronger connections between talent and employers |

## The Missing Link: Visibility of Jobs, Internships and Talent

National programmes invest in knowledge and adoption, but they rarely address the practical question of how a trained person finds the regional employer that needs them, or how an SME that has just started an AI project finds its first data professional.

**PhD candidates and researchers** in public-private labs gain valuable expertise, but may not know which regional companies outside their lab could employ them after their contract ends.

**SMEs supported by innovation hubs** may complete an AI pilot but struggle to hire people to continue the work.

**Students trained in AI** in regional programmes may be unaware of regional employers involved in national initiatives.

**Upskilled professionals** may not find roles that use their new skills.

Without visibility, investment in skills and adoption leaks away: talent moves to employers they already know, often in the Randstad or abroad, and SMEs fall back to outsourcing or abandon projects.

## Why Regions Are the Right Level to Close the Gap

National programmes set direction and fund activities, but labour markets are regional: people choose jobs based on travel time, family and housing. Regional AI hubs, development agencies, campuses and knowledge institutions know local employers and talent. They are therefore best placed to connect national investments with regional employment — for example by showing which companies in the region are hiring for AI roles, which internships are available and which labs involve regional partners.

## Practical Actions for Regional AI Coalitions and Hubs

**1. Map regional AI employers.** Identify companies and organisations in the region participating in national programmes, receiving innovation hub support or developing AI capabilities.

**2. Make AI vacancies and internships visible.** Encourage these organisations to publish AI and data roles where talent searches, showing jobs at exact location, including internships and graduation projects.

**3. Connect labs to regional employers.** Organise meetings between researchers in public-private labs and regional companies, so that expertise can flow into regional jobs.

**4. Follow SMEs after pilots.** When innovation hubs support AI pilots, help companies take the next step: defining roles, recruiting talent or arranging internships.

**5. Align training with demand.** Use regional vacancy data to inform education and upskilling programmes.

**6. Use responsible AI as a regional strength.** With the **EU AI Act** requiring AI literacy and imposing obligations for high-risk systems, expertise from ELSA Labs and responsible AI programmes can be translated into regional services and jobs in governance and compliance.

**7. Measure regional outcomes.** Track not only research outputs and pilots, but also AI vacancies, hires, internships and graduates staying in the region.

## European Instruments That Complement National Programmes

Regional AI ecosystems in the Netherlands can also draw on European instruments. The **Digital Europe Programme** funds European Digital Innovation Hubs and capacity-building in digital skills. Horizon Europe funds research and innovation projects, including collaborations involving Dutch universities, research institutes and companies. The EU has also launched initiatives to give researchers, startups and SMEs access to computing infrastructure for AI, and Testing and Experimentation Facilities where companies can test AI solutions in areas such as manufacturing, healthcare, agrifood and smart cities.

These instruments strengthen knowledge and adoption, but they share the same missing link as national programmes: they do not automatically connect the people trained or the companies supported with regional jobs. Regional AI coalitions that combine European and national support with visible regional vacancies and internships ensure that investments from all levels lead to lasting employment and capability.

## Indicators That Show Regional Impact

- **Number of AI and data vacancies** at regional employers over time.
- **Hires of graduates and researchers** from regional institutions and labs by regional employers.
- **Internships and graduation projects** related to AI at regional companies.
- **SMEs that hire data or AI staff** after receiving adoption support.
- **Participation in regional AI events** by employers and job seekers.

These indicators complement traditional programme metrics such as publications, projects and funding volumes.

## A Concrete Scenario: From Pilot to Hire

A regional innovation hub supports a food producer in testing a computer vision system for quality inspection, using test-before-invest services. The pilot shows promising results, but the company has no employees who can maintain and improve the system. The hub has completed its support, and the project risks stalling.

The regional AI coalition steps in. It helps the company define a data engineering role combined with machine vision tasks, connects it with a nearby university of applied sciences for a graduation project and publishes the vacancy on a specialist platform showing jobs at exact location. A graduate who completed a project in a related lab applies and is hired. The national investment in AI adoption and education thus results in a concrete regional job and a sustained AI capability.

## Human Capital: The Least Visible Part of AI Investment

National AI programmes typically distinguish between research, innovation, application and human capital. Human capital is arguably the most important and the least visible: without skilled people, research results remain in papers and pilots remain pilots. Human capital initiatives include new education programmes, professional training, PhD positions, AI literacy efforts and programmes to attract and retain talent.

The challenge is that human capital outcomes materialise in labour markets, not in programme reports. A trained person only creates value when employed in a role that uses their skills. Programmes can train thousands of people, but if employers and talent do not find each other, the investment's impact is diluted. This is why the connection between training and visible job opportunities — especially at regional employers — deserves as much attention as the training itself.

## What Regional Employers Say They Need

Regional employers, particularly SMEs, often describe a similar set of needs when asked about AI:

- **Practical help defining use cases** that fit their business and data.
- **Access to people** who can implement and maintain solutions after a pilot.
- **Affordable training** for existing employees.
- **Guidance on regulation,** such as the AI Act and GDPR, in practical terms.
- **Examples from comparable companies** in their sector and region.

National programmes and innovation hubs address several of these needs, but the second — access to people — often remains unresolved. Regional coalitions can bridge this by combining adoption support with recruitment support, internships and visibility of vacancies.

## Connecting Responsible AI Expertise to Jobs

Programmes focused on ethical, legal and societal aspects of AI generate expertise that is increasingly relevant to employers. With AI Act obligations for AI literacy, high-risk systems and transparency, organisations need people who understand governance, risk assessment, documentation and human oversight. Researchers and professionals involved in responsible AI programmes are well placed to fill such roles, but these positions are new and titles vary — AI governance specialist, AI compliance officer, responsible AI lead, algorithm auditor. Regional networks can help by making these roles visible and connecting organisations that need governance expertise with people who have developed it.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Measuring success only by projects and pilots,** not by regional employment and capability.
- **Assuming trained talent will find employers automatically.**
- **Focusing on large companies** already connected to national networks, while SMEs remain disconnected.
- **Duplicating platforms and portals** instead of using existing channels.
- **Separating responsible AI activities** from labour market needs, missing opportunities for governance roles.

## Key Takeaways

- National AI investment in the Netherlands includes AiNed, the Dutch AI Coalition, ELSA Labs, ICAI labs, TNO and European Digital Innovation Hubs.
- These initiatives create research positions, public-private expertise, human capital and SME adoption — but rarely address visibility of jobs and internships.
- Without visibility, talent and investment leak away from regions and SME pilots stall.
- Regional AI coalitions and hubs can map employers, make AI vacancies and internships visible, connect labs to companies, follow SMEs after pilots and measure regional outcomes.
- Responsible AI expertise, reinforced by AI Act obligations, can become a regional employment strength.
