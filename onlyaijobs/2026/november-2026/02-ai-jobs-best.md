Best has around thirty thousand inhabitants and a train station most travellers pass without getting off. Yet the town has been home to one of the most important healthcare technology sites in the Netherlands for decades, where medical imaging systems and the software around them are developed. For machine learning engineers interested in image analysis with real clinical consequences, Best is one of the most concentrated places to look in the country — and one of the least searched.

## Why a Small Town Has a Large Healthtech Footprint

**Philips** has a large healthcare site in Best, historically associated with the development and production of medical imaging systems, including magnetic resonance and image-guided therapy equipment. A site like this brings together hardware engineering, physics, software development, clinical science and regulatory affairs in one location.

That combination is what makes the AI work here distinctive. Image reconstruction, image quality improvement, workflow automation and decision support are not developed as isolated software products but as part of devices that must be safe, validated and approved before they reach a hospital.

## The Wider Ecosystem Around Best

**Eindhoven University of Technology (TU/e).** TU/e has strong groups in biomedical engineering, medical image analysis and electrical engineering. Researchers and graduates move between the university and industry in the region.

**Eindhoven MedTech Innovation Center (e/MTIC).** This collaboration brings together TU/e, Philips and regional hospitals such as **Catharina Hospital Eindhoven**, Máxima Medical Center and Kempenhaeghe to develop and test medical technology in a clinical context. It is a clear example of how industry, university and hospitals in the region work together.

**Brainport Development.** The regional development organisation supports the Brainport high-tech ecosystem, of which healthtech is one pillar alongside semiconductors, mobility and high-tech manufacturing.

**Suppliers and engineering service companies.** A large healthtech site attracts specialised suppliers, software consultancies and detachment firms that place engineers on projects. Many AI and software engineers working on imaging projects are formally employed by such companies rather than by the device manufacturer itself.

## What Kind of AI Work Actually Happens Here

| Area | What it involves | Typical techniques |
|---|---|---|
| Image reconstruction | Turning raw scanner data into images, faster or with less signal | Deep learning reconstruction, compressed sensing, physics-informed models |
| Image quality and denoising | Improving images while preserving clinically relevant detail | Convolutional networks, generative models, perceptual metrics |
| Workflow automation | Automatic positioning, planning and protocol selection | Detection, segmentation, landmark localisation |
| Clinical decision support | Measurements, segmentation and findings support for clinicians | Segmentation networks, classification, uncertainty estimation |
| Device intelligence | Predictive maintenance and remote service for installed systems | Time series analysis, anomaly detection |
| Verification and validation | Proving that software performs as intended across populations | Test dataset curation, statistical validation, bias analysis |

The last row is easy to overlook. In medical devices, a substantial share of the work is not training models but designing and executing the evidence that shows a model is safe and effective.

## Regulation: The Defining Feature of This Work

**EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR, EU 2017/745).** Software intended for medical purposes can itself be a medical device. The MDR sets requirements for clinical evaluation, post-market surveillance and technical documentation, and most AI-based medical software requires assessment by a notified body.

**IEC 62304.** This standard defines the software development lifecycle for medical device software, including requirements, architecture, verification and risk-based classification of software items.

**ISO 13485 and ISO 14971.** Quality management and risk management for medical devices. Every design decision must be traceable, and every identified risk must be analysed and controlled.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems that are safety components of products covered by EU product legislation, including medical devices, are classified as high-risk. For medical AI, the AI Act's requirements are designed to work together with the MDR conformity assessment.

**U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).** Imaging systems are sold worldwide, so American regulatory pathways are often part of the same development effort.

For an ML engineer, this means documentation, traceability and validation are part of the job description. Engineers who see this as bureaucracy tend to struggle; engineers who see it as the reason their work can be trusted in a hospital tend to thrive.

## A Concrete Scenario: Faster Scans Without Losing Diagnostic Value

A team develops a deep learning reconstruction method that allows a scan to be acquired faster. On internal test data, images look sharper than conventional reconstruction. Then the questions begin. Does the network hallucinate structures that are not there? Does it perform equally well on scanners of different field strengths, on children and on patients with implants? How will radiologists be involved in reader studies? What happens when a hospital uses an acquisition protocol the training data never covered?

Answering these questions takes longer than building the first model. It involves physicists, clinical scientists, radiologists and regulatory specialists — and it is exactly the kind of multidisciplinary work that defines imaging AI in Best.

## A Common Misconception About Medical AI Jobs

Many candidates assume medical AI means research positions at academic hospitals. That work exists, but a large share of medical AI that actually reaches patients is built inside device companies and their suppliers, where it is embedded in scanners, workstations and clinical software. These roles combine the rigour of research with the discipline of product development, and they are often titled as software, imaging or systems engineering roles rather than "AI researcher".

## Skills and Tools That Stand Out

- **Image processing fundamentals.** Understanding of signal processing, Fourier domain concepts and how images are formed — not only how to classify them.
- **Deep learning for imaging.** Segmentation, detection and reconstruction architectures, with frameworks such as PyTorch.
- **Performance-aware programming.** C++ and GPU programming are common, because algorithms must run on clinical hardware within time limits.
- **Validation discipline.** Designing test sets that represent patient populations, analysing subgroup performance and quantifying uncertainty.
- **Regulated development.** Familiarity with requirements traceability, risk analysis and design controls under IEC 62304 and ISO 13485.
- **Collaboration with clinicians.** Being able to discuss results with radiologists and clinical scientists in their language.

## How to Present Experience From Other Domains

Engineers from industrial vision, semiconductor inspection, remote sensing or automotive perception often have directly transferable skills. When applying, emphasise how you handled distribution shift between devices or sites, how you validated models beyond a single test score, and any work in a formal quality or safety process. Domain knowledge about anatomy and clinical workflows can be learned; a disciplined validation mindset is what hiring managers find hardest to teach.

## Why Healthtech Work Has Long Project Horizons

A medical imaging product can take years from concept to clinical use, and it remains on the market for many years after launch. That has consequences for a career: you may work on one product generation for a long time, and your work may be used in hospitals long after you have moved on. Some engineers find this slower rhythm frustrating; others value the depth and the tangible impact on patient care. Knowing which type you are is the most useful question to answer before applying.

## Clinical Data Access: The Constraint Nobody Mentions in the Job Ad

Medical imaging AI depends on clinical data, and clinical data is among the most protected information in Europe. Under the GDPR, health data is a special category of personal data, which means processing requires a specific legal basis and additional safeguards. In practice, a device company rarely has free access to hospital images. Data is obtained through research collaborations, data use agreements with hospitals, clinical studies with informed consent, or datasets that have been de-identified under strict procedures.

This has direct consequences for daily work. Datasets are smaller and more fragmented than candidates from consumer AI expect. Access requests can take months. Some data may only be analysed inside a hospital's own environment, which is why techniques such as federated learning and privacy-preserving evaluation receive serious attention in healthtech. The European Health Data Space Regulation, adopted at EU level, is intended to make secondary use of health data more structured over the coming years, but its practical effects will phase in gradually.

For a candidate, understanding these constraints is a genuine advantage. An engineer who can design a useful model from a modest, carefully governed dataset — and explain why that dataset is representative enough — is worth more to a medtech team than one who needs millions of labelled examples to get started.

## What a Working Week in Imaging AI Often Looks Like

A typical week mixes very different activities. There may be a design review in which an algorithm change is discussed with system architects and risk managers. There may be hours spent with a clinical scientist examining failure cases image by image. A verification protocol needs updating because a new scanner configuration was added. A reader study with radiologists is being planned. And somewhere in between, there is actual model development, experimentation and code.

Engineers who expect to spend most of their time training models are often surprised by this mix. Those who enjoy it describe the variety as one of the most attractive aspects of the work: the problems are technical, clinical and organisational at the same time.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **Where does the training and validation data come from, and how long does access take?** The answer reveals how mature the data pipeline is.
- **Which regulatory class does the software fall into, and who owns the technical documentation?** This shows how the work is divided between engineers and regulatory specialists.
- **How are clinicians involved during development, not only at the end?** Early clinical involvement usually indicates a stronger product process.
- **Am I employed by the manufacturer or by a service company placed on the project?** Both are common around a large site; the difference affects career path, benefits and continuity.
- **What happened with the last algorithm that did not make it to market, and why?** A candid answer tells you a lot about the team's culture.
