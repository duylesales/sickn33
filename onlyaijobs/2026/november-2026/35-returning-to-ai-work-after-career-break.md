People step away from work for many reasons: raising young children, caring for a parent, recovering from illness or burnout, studying, relocating with a partner, or simply taking time off. In most professions, returning after one or two years means a period of adjustment. In AI and data work, where tools and techniques seem to change every few months, a career break can feel like falling off a moving train. The good news is that the fundamentals of the field change much more slowly than the headlines suggest, Dutch law offers meaningful protections, and many employers value the perspective and maturity that returners bring.

## What Actually Changes During a Break — and What Doesn't

**What changes quickly.** Tools, libraries, model releases, cloud services and popular frameworks evolve continuously. Generative AI in particular has changed how many teams write code, search documentation, build prototypes and create applications on top of language models. Regulatory context also changes: the EU AI Act, for example, introduced new obligations that affect many AI teams.

**What changes slowly.** Statistics, evaluation, data quality, experimental design, understanding business problems and communicating uncertainty remain as important as ever. The ability to recognise data leakage, choose the right metric or explain why a model should not be deployed has not become obsolete. In many teams, these fundamentals have become more valuable, because powerful tools make it easier to build impressive-looking systems that fail in practice.

A returner who refreshes tools and understands recent developments while bringing solid fundamentals is often in a stronger position than they fear.

## A Practical Plan to Refresh Your Skills

**Step 1: Map the changes in your specialism.** Read recent job descriptions for roles similar to your previous one. Note which tools, platforms and concepts appear repeatedly that you do not know yet. This gives a realistic picture of what employers expect now.

**Step 2: Refresh the core toolchain.** Update your programming environment, work through recent versions of the main libraries you used and practise version control and testing. Use AI coding assistants carefully to accelerate learning, while checking results.

**Step 3: Learn the essentials of generative AI applications.** Even if you do not plan to specialise in generative AI, understanding how teams use language models — retrieval-augmented generation, evaluation of model outputs, prompt and system design, and risks such as hallucination and data leakage — has become part of general AI literacy.

**Step 4: Understand the regulatory context.** Learn the basics of the EU AI Act, including risk categories, transparency obligations and the AI literacy requirement for organisations.

**Step 5: Build one recent project.** A small, well-documented project using current tools demonstrates that your skills are up to date and gives you something concrete to discuss in interviews.

A realistic refresh takes weeks to a few months, depending on the length of the break and the specialism, not years.

## How to Explain a Career Gap

**Be brief and matter-of-fact.** A single line on your CV — for example, "Career break for family care" or "Sabbatical and relocation" — is sufficient. You do not need to share private details, such as medical information.

**Focus on readiness.** In interviews, spend less time explaining why you were away and more time explaining what you have done to prepare for returning and what you want to work on now.

**Mention relevant activities.** Courses, projects, volunteering, community involvement or caregiving experience that developed organisational skills can be mentioned when relevant, without overstating them.

**Do not apologise.** Career breaks are normal. The **NVP Recruitment Code**, a widely used Dutch code of conduct for recruitment, emphasises that selection should focus on job-relevant criteria.

## Legal Protections in the Netherlands

**General Equal Treatment Act.** Dutch equal treatment law prohibits discrimination on grounds such as sex, including pregnancy and motherhood, as well as other protected grounds. Rejecting a candidate because of pregnancy or caring responsibilities associated with a protected ground can constitute unlawful discrimination. The **Netherlands Institute for Human Rights** can assess complaints and issue opinions.

**Work and Care Act.** Employees returning from leave, such as maternity, birth or parental leave, retain their employment rights. Parental leave is partly paid in the first year of a child's life, and parents can often take leave part-time, which helps with a gradual return.

**Flexible Working Act.** After returning, employees may request changes in working hours, which can support a phased return or a permanent part-time arrangement.

**Gatekeeper Improvement Act.** For employees returning after illness, including burnout, Dutch law requires both employer and employee to cooperate on reintegration during the first two years of illness, guided by an **occupational physician (bedrijfsarts)**. A plan of action is drawn up and progress is evaluated, and the **UWV** assesses reintegration efforts if an employee applies for disability benefits after two years.

## Returning After Burnout or Illness

Returning after burnout or illness requires special care. Cognitive work such as programming and modelling can be demanding, and a too rapid return can lead to relapse.

- **Follow the reintegration plan.** Work with your occupational physician and employer on gradual increases in hours and responsibilities.
- **Start with well-defined tasks.** Tasks with clear scope and limited time pressure are often more suitable at the beginning than urgent production issues.
- **Adjust on-call and deadlines.** Temporary exemption from on-call duties can be part of a reasonable plan.
- **Communicate boundaries.** Clear agreements about availability and meeting load help sustain the return.

Candidates who apply to a new employer after recovery are not required to disclose past health issues. Focus on your current capacity and what support, if any, would help.

## Returning Part-Time or Gradually

Many returners prefer to start with fewer hours and increase them over time. Part-time work is common in the Netherlands, and many employers — particularly in the public sector, healthcare, research and education — are used to such arrangements. When applying, you can propose a concrete arrangement, such as starting at 24 or 32 hours with an evaluation after several months.

## Negotiating Pay and Level

Returners sometimes accept a lower level or salary than their experience justifies, out of concern that the break has reduced their value. While refreshing skills is important, years of prior experience remain relevant. As the **EU Pay Transparency Directive** is implemented, employers will need to provide more information about pay ranges and use objective criteria for pay, which can make it easier to negotiate a fair level. Ask how the role is classified and what criteria determine salary.

## Rebuilding Your Network Before You Apply

Many opportunities for returners come through people rather than job boards. Former colleagues know your work, former managers can vouch for your capabilities, and people who moved to other organisations may know about teams looking for experienced professionals.

Start by contacting a handful of former colleagues for informal conversations. Ask what has changed in their teams, which tools they use now and which organisations are hiring. These conversations also help you rebuild professional confidence and vocabulary before formal interviews.

Attend a few local meetups or online events in your specialism. Data and AI communities in cities such as Amsterdam, Utrecht, Eindhoven, Rotterdam and Groningen organise regular events where new tools and practices are discussed. Even one or two events can quickly show which developments matter in practice and which are mostly hype.

Update your professional profile gradually rather than all at once. A short note that you are returning to work and interested in specific types of roles can prompt people in your network to reach out. Keep the message positive and specific — for example, mentioning the domain and type of work you want — so that others can easily think of relevant opportunities.

## Returner Programmes and Communities

Some companies and organisations offer returner programmes, sometimes called returnships, designed to help professionals re-enter technical work after a break with structured onboarding, training and mentoring. Communities for women and underrepresented groups in technology, local data and AI meetups and alumni networks can also provide contacts, encouragement and information about employers that support returners.

## A Concrete Scenario: Returning After Three Years of Care

A data scientist with seven years of experience in insurance analytics takes a three-year break to care for a family member. When she decides to return, she feels overwhelmed by generative AI and new tools.

She starts by reviewing vacancies and discovers that most roles still require the skills she had: modelling, SQL, Python and stakeholder communication, with additions such as cloud platforms, MLOps practices and some familiarity with language models. She spends two months refreshing her Python environment, completing a short course on generative AI applications and building a small project that uses public Dutch data to predict claim-like events, including a simple retrieval component for policy documents.

On her CV, she writes one line about the career break. In interviews, she focuses on her insurance domain knowledge and her refreshed skills. She receives an offer from an insurer at a senior level, with an agreement to start at 32 hours. Her domain experience proves to be more valuable than any tool she missed.

## A Common Misconception About Career Breaks in AI

The most common misconception is that a break of more than a year makes an AI career impossible to restart. In reality, employers struggle to find experienced professionals with domain knowledge and sound judgement, and those qualities do not disappear during a break. A second misconception is that returners must master every new development before applying. It is enough to show current competence in the core tools of your specialism and an understanding of relevant changes; learning continues on the job.

## Questions Worth Asking Employers

- **How does onboarding work for new team members?** Structured onboarding helps returners.
- **Is part-time or phased starting possible?**
- **How does the team use generative AI tools, and what training is available?**
- **What development budget is available in the first year?**
- **Have you hired people returning from a career break before?** Positive examples indicate a supportive culture.

## Key Takeaways

- Tools and frameworks change quickly, but fundamentals such as statistics, evaluation, data quality and communication remain central.
- A structured refresh — mapping changes, updating tools, learning generative AI basics, understanding the AI Act and building a recent project — takes weeks to months.
- Explain a career gap briefly and focus on readiness; you do not need to share private details.
- Dutch equal treatment law, the Work and Care Act, the Flexible Working Act and the Gatekeeper Improvement Act offer important protections and support.
- Returners often bring valuable domain knowledge and maturity; negotiate a level that reflects prior experience.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: professional after a long break) Is it possible to return to AI work after several years away?
Yes — employers value experience and domain knowledge, and a focused refresh of tools and recent developments is usually sufficient.

### (Scenario: candidate writing a CV) How should I describe a career gap on my CV?
Briefly and factually, for example "Career break for family care", focusing the rest of the CV on skills and readiness.

### (Scenario: returner overwhelmed by generative AI) Do I need to become a generative AI expert before returning?
No — understanding the basics of how teams use language models and their risks is enough for most roles, unless you specialise in that area.

### (Scenario: employee returning after burnout) How does returning after illness work in the Netherlands?
Under the Gatekeeper Improvement Act, employer and employee cooperate on gradual reintegration guided by an occupational physician.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about discrimination) Can an employer reject me because of pregnancy or care responsibilities?
Rejecting a candidate on grounds such as pregnancy can constitute unlawful discrimination under Dutch equal treatment law.
