Once you have a few years of experience in machine learning or data engineering, the messages start arriving: "Exciting opportunity for a Senior AI Engineer", "Are you open to a new challenge?", "I came across your impressive profile". Some come from in-house recruiters at companies you would genuinely like to work for. Others come from agencies sending the same message to hundreds of people, from recruiters who have not read your profile or, increasingly, from automated tools and scammers. Experienced professionals often respond by ignoring everything — and occasionally miss an opportunity that would have been right. A simple way to evaluate recruiter outreach saves time and helps you keep control over your career and your personal data.

## Why AI Professionals Receive So Many Messages

Demand for experienced AI and data professionals remains high, and recruiters are often measured on the number of qualified candidates they present. Search tools on professional networks make it easy to find profiles with keywords such as "machine learning", "Python" or "LLM". Agencies earn fees when they place candidates, which encourages high-volume outreach. And generative AI tools make it cheap to send personalised-looking messages at scale.

The result is a stream of messages in which a few genuine opportunities are hidden among many poorly matched or generic ones.

## Types of Recruiters and How They Work

| Type | Who they work for | How they are paid | What to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-house recruiter | The employer directly | Salary from the employer | Knows the team and role in detail; represents one employer |
| Agency recruiter (contingency) | Multiple client employers | Fee when a candidate is hired | May approach many candidates; quality varies strongly |
| Retained or executive search | One client for a specific senior role | Fee agreed in advance, regardless of outcome | Targeted, fewer candidates, often senior roles |
| Secondment or staffing firm | The firm itself, which places you at clients | Margin on your billable hours | You become an employee of the firm, not the client |
| Freelance platform or intermediary | Clients seeking interim professionals | Margin or fee on assignments | Relevant for freelance and interim work |

In the Netherlands, candidates should never pay a recruiter or agency for finding them a job. Employers or clients pay the fees.

## Dutch Rules and Standards for Recruitment

**NVP Recruitment Code.** The Dutch Association for Personnel Management and Organisational Development (NVP) publishes a recruitment code that many employers and recruiters follow, covering transparency about the vacancy, selection procedures, handling of candidate data and communication.

**Industry associations and certification.** Employment agencies can be members of associations such as **ABU** and **NBBU**, which have codes of conduct. The **Stichting Normering Arbeid (SNA)** certification checks whether labour providers meet obligations such as paying wages and taxes correctly.

**Waadi registration.** Under the Placement of Personnel by Intermediaries Act (Waadi), companies that make workers available to others must be registered as such in the trade register of the **Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK)**. You can check a company's registration in the KVK register.

**GDPR.** Recruiters who collect and store your personal data — for example from your public profile — must comply with the GDPR. If a recruiter obtained your data from a source other than you, they must inform you about the processing, including its purpose and your rights, within a reasonable period. You have the right to access your data, request deletion and object to processing. The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** supervises compliance.

**Pay Transparency Directive.** As the EU Pay Transparency Directive is implemented, candidates will be entitled to information about the initial pay or pay range before the job interview, and employers may not ask about pay history. This will make it easier to judge early whether an opportunity is worth pursuing.

## Signals of a Message Worth Answering

**Specificity.** The message names the company or at least the sector, team and type of problem, and explains why your profile fits.

**Real information.** It includes salary range, location or remote policy, contract type and level. As pay transparency rules take effect, missing salary information becomes more notable.

**Evidence of reading your profile.** References to your actual experience, projects or technologies — not just keywords.

**Transparency about the recruiter's role.** Clear statement of whether they are in-house, an agency or a secondment firm.

**Respect for your time.** A clear proposal for a short call rather than an immediate request for your CV.

## Signals of Noise

- Generic greetings and superlatives without concrete details.
- Roles that clearly do not match your level or specialism.
- Refusal to name the company or sector even after you express interest.
- Pressure to share your CV immediately "so I can present you".
- Requests for exclusivity before you know anything about the role.
- Salary described only as "competitive".

Noise does not necessarily mean bad intent. It often reflects high-volume recruitment practices. But answering it rarely helps your career.

## Signals of a Scam

Job scams target professionals in technology too. Warning signs include:

- **Requests for payment**, for example for training, equipment, visa processing or "registration fees".
- **Requests for sensitive personal data** such as copies of identity documents, bank details or citizen service numbers (BSN) before any formal job offer or employment contract.
- **Interviews only via chat apps**, with no video call or contact with identifiable company employees.
- **Offers that are too good to be true**, such as very high pay for little work or an offer without any interview.
- **Company details that cannot be verified**, such as no registration in the KVK register, a website created recently or email addresses from free providers.
- **Requests to receive and forward money or goods**, which may involve criminal activity.

If you suspect a scam, stop contact, do not share further data and report it to the **Fraudehelpdesk** and, if you suffered damage, to the police.

## Managing Your CV and Data Carefully

**Agree before your CV is sent.** Ask agencies to confirm which company they will present you to before sending your CV. Being presented by multiple agencies to the same employer can create conflicts and may damage your application.

**Keep a simple log.** Note which recruiter presented you to which company and when.

**Exercise your rights.** If an agency keeps contacting you without your interest, you can ask them to delete your data and stop processing it.

**Share only what is necessary.** A CV rarely needs to include your full address, date of birth or photo.

## Using Recruiters to Your Advantage

Good recruiters can be valuable. They may know about roles that are not publicly advertised, understand salary levels in a sector, give feedback after interviews and help negotiate offers. Building a relationship with a few specialised recruiters who understand AI and data roles — and who treat your data and time respectfully — can be more useful than ignoring all recruiters.

When a message seems promising, ask a few questions before investing time:

- **Which company and team is this for?**
- **What is the salary range and contract type?**
- **Why is the position open?**
- **What does the interview process look like?**
- **Are you working exclusively on this role, or is it also posted publicly?**

## Responding Efficiently Without Burning Bridges

Evaluating messages is easier when you have a consistent way of responding. Many experienced professionals use a few short, reusable replies.

**When you are not looking.** A brief thank-you, a statement that you are not currently exploring new roles and, if relevant, the type of opportunity that would interest you in the future. This keeps the door open for good recruiters without inviting further generic messages.

**When the message is interesting but incomplete.** A short reply asking for the company or sector, salary range, location or remote policy and contract type. Recruiters with genuine, suitable roles usually provide this information quickly; those without it often do not reply.

**When the role does not fit.** A polite note explaining what you are looking for, such as a specific domain, seniority or type of work. Specialised recruiters value this information and may return with better-matched roles.

**When a message looks suspicious.** No reply is needed. Do not click unknown links or download attachments, and report clear scams.

Setting clear criteria also helps with timing. Many professionals reserve a few minutes once a week to process recruiter messages rather than responding throughout the day. This reduces distraction while ensuring genuine opportunities are not missed.

## A Concrete Scenario: Three Messages in One Week

A machine learning engineer receives three messages in one week. The first comes from an agency recruiter who writes about an "exciting AI opportunity" without details and asks for a CV. The second comes from an in-house recruiter at a logistics company, mentioning a specific forecasting team, a salary range, a hybrid policy and a project similar to the engineer's previous work. The third offers a remote "AI trainer" role with very high hourly pay, requests a copy of a passport and communicates only via a messaging app.

The engineer ignores the first message after asking for the company name and receiving no answer, accepts a call for the second and discovers a promising role, and reports the third to the Fraudehelpdesk after checking that the company does not exist in the KVK register. Ten minutes of evaluation prevents wasted time, protects personal data and surfaces the one genuine opportunity.

## A Common Misconception About Recruiter Outreach

A common misconception is that responding to a recruiter commits you to a process or harms your relationship with your current employer. A short, polite conversation to learn about an opportunity is normal professional practice, and good recruiters respect confidentiality. Another misconception is that recruiter messages reflect the whole job market. Many excellent roles — particularly at smaller companies without large recruitment budgets — are never promoted through outreach at all and can only be found through direct searches or specialist platforms.

## Key Takeaways

- AI professionals receive many recruiter messages because of high demand, fee-based recruitment and automated outreach tools.
- Distinguish in-house recruiters, agencies, retained search, secondment firms and intermediaries; candidates should never pay for being placed.
- The NVP Recruitment Code, industry associations, SNA certification, Waadi registration, GDPR and the Pay Transparency Directive shape recruitment practices and your rights.
- Worthwhile messages are specific and transparent; noise is generic; scams ask for payment, sensitive data or cannot be verified.
- Control where your CV is sent, keep a log, exercise your data rights and build relationships with a few specialised recruiters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: engineer flooded with messages) How do I decide which recruiter messages to answer?
Answer messages that are specific about company, team, salary and fit; ignore generic messages and avoid any that request payment or sensitive data.

### (Scenario: candidate asked to pay a fee) Should I ever pay a recruiter or agency?
No — in the Netherlands, recruitment fees are paid by employers or clients, not by candidates.

### (Scenario: candidate concerned about privacy) Can a recruiter store my data from my public profile?
Only in compliance with the GDPR; they must inform you and you can request access, deletion or object to processing.

### (Scenario: candidate suspecting a fake job) How can I verify whether a job offer is genuine?
Check the company in the KVK register, verify the website and employees, insist on video interviews and never share identity documents or pay fees before a real contract.

### (Scenario: candidate working with several agencies) Can multiple agencies send my CV to the same company?
It can happen and may create conflicts; always agree which company an agency will present you to before your CV is sent.
