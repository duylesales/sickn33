Walk across a Dutch science park and you pass dozens of organisations within a few hundred metres: a multinational research lab, a university faculty, a scale-up with thirty employees, a startup in an incubator, a shared testing facility. Many of them are looking for people who can work with data and AI. Yet a data science graduate searching for jobs rarely sees this campus as one labour market. Each organisation publishes vacancies separately — or not at all — and small companies remain invisible next to large brands. For campus organisations whose mission includes building ecosystems, the combined job market on their own site is one of the most underused assets they have.

## Why Campuses Matter for AI Talent

Dutch science parks and campuses were created to bring research, education and business together. That concentration creates specific advantages for talent:

**Career mobility without relocation.** Professionals can change employers — from a startup to a corporate lab, from research to product development — without moving house or changing commute.

**Learning environment.** Seminars, shared facilities and informal contact expose employees to knowledge beyond their own organisation.

**Internship and graduation opportunities.** Proximity to universities and universities of applied sciences makes student projects easier to organise.

**Startup resilience.** When a startup fails, its employees can often find new roles elsewhere on the same campus, keeping talent in the ecosystem.

## The Visibility Problem on Campuses

**Fragmented vacancy information.** Large companies publish vacancies on their own career sites; startups post on social media or rely on networks; research groups publish through university systems. There is rarely one place showing all AI and data roles on a campus.

**Unknown small employers.** Candidates recognise the big names but not the dozens of smaller companies, even when those companies offer interesting technical work and faster growth.

**Specialist titles.** Roles are often labelled as bioinformatician, process engineer, algorithm developer or research engineer, without the words "AI" or "data scientist".

**Location blind spots.** Candidates searching by city may miss campuses located at the edge of a city or in a neighbouring municipality.

## What Campus Organisations Can Do

**1. Aggregate and showcase vacancies.** Make AI and data vacancies from all campus organisations visible together, at exact location, and share them through campus channels. Cooperating with specialist platforms is usually more effective than building a separate portal.

**2. Profile smaller employers.** Short profiles of startups and scale-ups — what they build, what data they work with, how many people they employ — help candidates discover them.

**3. Organise talent-focused events.** Campus-wide career events, data meetups and open days for students connect talent with multiple employers at once.

**4. Coordinate internships and graduation projects.** Help smaller companies define projects and connect them with nearby knowledge institutions.

**5. Support talent circulation.** When companies downsize or startups close, actively connect affected employees with other campus employers.

**6. Address practical barriers.** Housing for starters and international employees, public transport and cycling access, childcare and amenities affect whether talent chooses and stays on a campus.

**7. Measure the campus labour market.** Track vacancies, hires, internships and employee movements between campus organisations.

## Shared Services That Strengthen Talent Attraction

Campuses can offer services that individual small companies cannot organise alone:

- **Joint employer branding** presenting the campus as an attractive place to work.
- **International onboarding support,** such as information on permits, housing and registration, in cooperation with expat centres.
- **Shared training programmes** in data skills, AI literacy and responsible AI, which also help companies meet the AI Act's AI literacy requirement.
- **Mentoring networks** connecting junior data professionals with experienced colleagues across organisations.
- **Community platforms** for professionals to share knowledge and opportunities.

## A Concrete Scenario: Making a Campus Labour Market Visible

A campus hosting around one hundred organisations notices that many startups struggle to hire data engineers, while students at the nearby university say they cannot find local tech jobs and move to Amsterdam after graduating.

The campus organisation surveys its tenants and finds dozens of open or planned AI and data roles, many never publicly advertised. It encourages companies to publish vacancies on a specialist platform that shows jobs at exact location, adds a campus-wide overview to its newsletter and website and organises a quarterly "data careers on campus" event with the university. Within a year, several companies report hires from the local university and the campus has concrete data on its AI labour market — information that also supports discussions with the municipality and province about housing and mobility.

## Housing and Amenities as Talent Infrastructure

A campus can offer excellent jobs and still lose talent if people cannot live nearby or if the site feels empty after working hours. Young professionals and international employees in particular weigh housing availability, public transport, sports facilities, childcare and places to meet when choosing where to work.

Several Dutch campuses have developed or advocated for housing for students, researchers and young professionals near the site, improved bus and cycling connections and added facilities such as restaurants, sports centres and event spaces. Campus organisations rarely control all of these themselves, but they can bring employer demand data to municipalities, housing corporations and transport authorities — making a concrete case for investments that support talent retention.

## Talent Formats That Work on Campuses

- **Campus career days** where all tenants present vacancies and internships together.
- **Tech talks and data meetups** hosted by different companies in rotation, giving each visibility.
- **Graduate programmes shared by several smaller companies,** allowing graduates to rotate between employers.
- **Welcome programmes for new employees** across organisations, building a campus-wide network.
- **Alumni networks** for former employees and students, keeping talent connected to the ecosystem.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Building a standalone campus job portal** that few candidates visit.
- **Focusing on anchor companies only,** leaving smaller employers invisible.
- **Events without follow-up,** where contacts are not translated into applications or internships.
- **Treating talent as each tenant's responsibility alone,** missing the value of ecosystem-level action.
- **Ignoring practical living conditions** that determine whether talent stays.

## Why Startups on Campuses Struggle Most With Hiring

Startups and young scale-ups are often the most innovative AI employers on a campus, yet they face the greatest hiring difficulties. They lack recruitment staff and employer brand recognition. They cannot match the salaries of large technology companies. Their hiring needs change quickly as funding rounds close or products pivot. And many candidates perceive them as risky.

At the same time, startups offer what many AI professionals seek: broad responsibility, direct impact, fast learning and the chance to work on new technology. The problem is often not attractiveness but reach. A campus organisation that makes startup vacancies visible alongside larger employers, explains what these companies build and helps candidates understand the career benefits of joining a startup ecosystem addresses exactly this gap.

Campus-level talent circulation also reduces perceived risk. A candidate who knows that other campus employers are hiring is more willing to join a startup, because a failure would not require leaving the region.

## International Talent on Campuses

Many campuses attract international researchers, PhD candidates and professionals. They bring expertise that regional employers need, but they face specific barriers: residence permits, housing, language, networks and understanding the Dutch labour market.

Campus organisations can help by providing clear information about permits and the orientation year for graduates, cooperating with regional expat centres, organising networking events that include international talent and encouraging employers to state language requirements explicitly in vacancies. International PhD candidates finishing their research are a particularly valuable group: they have deep expertise and often want to stay, but may not know the companies located a few buildings away.

## Measuring the Campus Labour Market

| Indicator | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Number of AI and data vacancies on campus | Demand for skills across tenants |
| Share of vacancies from startups and SMEs | Visibility of smaller employers |
| Internships and graduation projects with tenants | Connection to knowledge institutions |
| Hires of graduates from nearby institutions | Retention of regional talent |
| Moves of employees between campus organisations | Talent circulation within the ecosystem |
| Participation in campus talent events | Engagement of employers and talent |

Collecting these indicators annually helps campus organisations demonstrate their value to shareholders, municipalities and provinces and identify where to focus efforts.

## When Companies Leave or Close

Campuses inevitably see companies relocate, downsize or close. These moments can either drain talent from the ecosystem or keep it circulating. Campus organisations that respond quickly — sharing information about affected employees' skills with other tenants (with consent), organising matching sessions and highlighting open vacancies — help retain valuable expertise. This also strengthens the campus's reputation as a place where a career is resilient, which in turn attracts new talent.

## How This Connects to Regional Policy

Campuses are often part of regional innovation strategies supported by municipalities, provinces and regional development agencies. Making the campus labour market visible provides evidence of the ecosystem's economic value and helps align policies on housing, mobility and education with actual demand. It also supports broader regional goals of retaining graduates who might otherwise leave for the Randstad.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch science parks and campuses combine many organisations needing AI and data skills, creating career mobility without relocation.
- Their combined labour market is often invisible because of fragmented vacancy information, unknown small employers, specialist titles and location blind spots.
- Campus organisations can aggregate vacancies, profile smaller employers, organise events, coordinate internships, support talent circulation and address practical barriers.
- Shared services such as joint employer branding, international onboarding and AI literacy training benefit smaller tenants.
- Visible campus labour market data strengthens cooperation with regional policy on housing, mobility and education.
