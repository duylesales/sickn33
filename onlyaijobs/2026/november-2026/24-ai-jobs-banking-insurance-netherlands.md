If you counted the data scientists, data engineers and quantitative analysts employed by Dutch organisations, banks and insurers would be near the top of the list. Large banks in Amsterdam and Utrecht and insurers in Utrecht, The Hague, Zeist, Apeldoorn and Tilburg have invested heavily in data teams for more than a decade. For candidates, financial services offer mature data platforms, strong salaries and a wide range of problems — but also one of the most regulated environments for AI in Europe. Understanding that regulation is not an administrative detail; it defines what the work looks like.

## Why Financial Services Became Data-Driven Early

Banks and insurers have always been information businesses. Credit risk models, actuarial pricing and fraud detection used statistics long before machine learning became popular. Digitalisation of customer contact, strict regulatory reporting requirements and competition from digital challengers accelerated investment in data platforms and analytics.

At the same time, financial institutions have faced major enforcement actions for failures in anti-money laundering controls, both in the Netherlands and elsewhere. This has made financial crime detection one of the largest areas of data work in Dutch banks, with thousands of employees involved in customer due diligence and transaction monitoring across the sector.

## The Named Organisations and Supervisors

**Banks.** The largest Dutch banks — ING, Rabobank and ABN AMRO — employ large data and analytics workforces, along with de Volksbank, Triodos Bank and a growing number of digital banks and payment companies.

**Insurers.** Achmea, NN Group, a.s.r. and other insurers, as well as health insurers, use data for pricing, claims, customer service and investment management.

**De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB).** The Dutch central bank supervises the prudential soundness of banks, insurers and pension funds and has published principles and discussion papers on the responsible use of AI in the financial sector.

**Dutch Authority for the Financial Markets (AFM).** The AFM supervises conduct — how financial institutions treat customers — including the use of algorithms in customer-facing decisions.

**European Central Bank (ECB) Banking Supervision.** The largest Dutch banks are directly supervised by the ECB within the Single Supervisory Mechanism, which includes review of internal models used for capital requirements.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** The act classifies AI systems used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score as high-risk, with an exception for fraud detection. AI systems used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance for natural persons are also high-risk. Providers and deployers of such systems face requirements on risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and robustness.

**GDPR and automated decision-making.** Article 22 of the GDPR restricts decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect individuals. In its SCHUFA judgment (C-634/21), the Court of Justice of the EU held that generating a credit score can itself be an automated decision when it plays a determining role in a lender's decision — with direct implications for credit scoring models.

**Consumer Credit Directive (EU) 2023/2225.** The revised directive, applicable from late 2026, strengthens rules on creditworthiness assessments, including rights for consumers when assessments involve automated processing.

**Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act (Wwft).** Requires institutions to perform customer due diligence and monitor transactions for unusual activity. The EU's new anti-money laundering package, including a new EU authority, will further shape requirements in the coming years.

**Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA).** Applicable since January 2025, DORA sets requirements for ICT risk management, incident reporting, resilience testing and management of third-party ICT providers — relevant for cloud-based AI platforms and external model providers.

**Capital requirements and Solvency II.** Internal models used for capital requirements must meet strict supervisory standards, which influences how far machine learning can be used in those models.

## Model Risk: The Discipline That Shapes Everything

Financial institutions typically maintain a model inventory, classify models by risk and require independent validation before and during use. A machine learning model for transaction monitoring or pricing goes through development documentation, validation by a separate team, approval by a model committee and periodic monitoring.

For data scientists, this means a model is not finished when it performs well on a test set. It must be explainable to validators, stable over time, fair across customer groups where relevant and monitored for drift. Some candidates find this frustrating; others value the discipline, because it forces better models and protects customers. Model validation itself has become an attractive career path for experienced data scientists who enjoy critical analysis.

## A Concrete Scenario: Reducing False Positives in Transaction Monitoring

A bank's rule-based transaction monitoring system generates large numbers of alerts, the vast majority of which turn out to be harmless after manual review by analysts. A data science team develops a machine learning model to prioritise alerts and detect patterns the rules miss, such as networks of accounts moving money in structured ways.

The model reduces false positives in testing. But the compliance department and model validators ask critical questions. Will the model miss types of criminal behaviour that are underrepresented in historical cases? Can analysts understand why an alert was raised? How will the model be monitored as criminals adapt? Could customers be treated unfairly based on characteristics that correlate with nationality? The team adds explanations, conservative thresholds, monitoring and periodic review, and implements the model alongside rules rather than replacing them. This is typical of financial AI: effectiveness, explainability and fairness must be demonstrated together.

## Generative AI in Banks and Insurers

Financial institutions have large volumes of text: policies, contracts, customer emails, complaint letters, product conditions, internal procedures and regulatory documents. Generative AI is therefore being applied to support customer service agents, summarise case files, help employees search internal knowledge and assist with drafting. Insurers explore document understanding for claims, and banks use language models to support know-your-customer reviews.

The constraints are strict. Customer data is confidential, outputs must be accurate and traceable, and responsibility for advice or decisions must remain with qualified employees. Under DORA, reliance on external AI providers is part of ICT third-party risk management. Many institutions therefore start with internal-facing applications where an employee reviews output, rather than letting generative AI communicate directly with customers. Data professionals working in this area spend significant time on evaluation, guardrails, access control and documentation.

## Pay, Collective Agreements and Working Culture

Large Dutch banks generally have their own collective labour agreements, and the insurance sector has a sector-wide agreement alongside company-specific arrangements. Salary scales are often published, which gives candidates a clear view of pay ranges. Compensation for experienced data scientists in financial services is typically among the higher levels in the Dutch market, although variable pay at banks is limited by law.

The working culture tends to be structured, with clear roles, formal governance and a strong emphasis on risk awareness. Hybrid working is common, and large institutions offer extensive training budgets and internal mobility between business units.

## How to Prepare for a Financial Services Interview

- **Be ready to explain a model to a non-technical reviewer.** Interviewers often test whether you can justify modelling choices clearly.
- **Show awareness of fairness and bias.** Expect questions about how you would detect and mitigate unfair outcomes.
- **Know the basics of the relevant regulation.** A short, accurate explanation of why credit scoring is high-risk under the AI Act makes a strong impression.
- **Prepare examples of monitoring and maintenance.** Institutions value people who think beyond deployment.
- **Understand the business line.** Financial crime, credit and insurance pricing each have distinct objectives and data.

## A Common Misconception About Financial Services AI

Some candidates expect banks and insurers to be slow, legacy-bound organisations where innovative AI work is impossible. Legacy systems do exist, and approval processes take time. But many Dutch financial institutions run modern cloud data platforms, employ strong machine learning teams and apply generative AI in customer service and internal processes. The real difference with technology companies is accountability: every model affecting customers must be defensible to supervisors and courts.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Statistical modelling and machine learning.** Especially gradient boosting, logistic regression, survival models and network analysis.
- **Explainability and fairness.** Methods to explain model decisions and assess bias across groups.
- **Model risk and validation.** Understanding documentation, validation standards and monitoring.
- **Regulatory literacy.** Familiarity with the AI Act, GDPR, AML rules, DORA and relevant supervisory expectations.
- **Data engineering at scale.** Working with large transactional datasets on governed platforms.
- **Domain knowledge.** Credit, insurance, payments or financial crime.
- **Communication.** Explaining models to compliance, legal, risk and business stakeholders.

## Career Paths in Financial Services

Data professionals in banking and insurance can grow along several paths: senior modelling and lead data scientist roles, model validation and model risk management, data and AI governance, product ownership for data-driven products, or quantitative roles in risk and treasury. Experience in a highly regulated environment is valued elsewhere as the AI Act brings similar requirements to other sectors, making financial services a strong training ground for responsible AI.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **How does the model lifecycle work, from development to validation and monitoring?** This reveals the balance between speed and control.
- **Which models the team works on are high-risk under the AI Act?** The answer shows regulatory preparedness.
- **How are explainability and fairness assessed?** Mature teams have concrete methods and standards.
- **What data platform and tooling are used?** This affects productivity and learning.
- **How do compliance and business teams collaborate with data scientists?** Collaboration determines whether models reach production.

## Key Takeaways

- Dutch banks and insurers are among the largest employers of data professionals, with work spanning financial crime, credit risk, pricing, claims, customer service and data platforms.
- DNB, the AFM and ECB Banking Supervision oversee financial institutions and their use of models and algorithms.
- The AI Act classifies credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing for individuals as high-risk; GDPR Article 22 and the SCHUFA ruling affect automated scoring.
- The Wwft, DORA, the revised Consumer Credit Directive, capital rules and Solvency II further shape the work.
- Model risk management defines the model lifecycle; model validation is itself a strong career path.
