A rose cut in Kenya in the morning can be sold in Aalsmeer the next day and be on sale in a German supermarket shortly after. Between those moments lies one of the most data-intensive trading systems in agriculture: a marketplace where prices are set in seconds, products lose value by the hour and logistics must work flawlessly at enormous volume. Aalsmeer is the historic centre of that system. For data scientists interested in pricing, marketplaces and perishable logistics, it is a surprisingly advanced environment that rarely appears in AI job searches.

## Why Aalsmeer Matters to the Global Flower Trade

**Royal FloraHolland** is a cooperative of growers and the world's largest marketplace for flowers and plants. Its largest location is in Aalsmeer, historically known for one of the biggest commercial buildings in the world by floor area, where trolleys full of flowers move through halls every morning. Other major locations include Naaldwijk in the Westland and Rijnsburg.

For more than a century, the auction clock set flower prices: the price starts high and drops until a buyer presses a button. That mechanism — a descending or "Dutch" auction — is famous in economics textbooks. What has changed is how the trade happens. Buyers increasingly bid remotely, and a growing share of trade takes place through direct digital trading between growers and buyers rather than through the clock. The digital platform **Floriday** connects growers, buyers and logistics service providers for this trade.

## The Wider Ecosystem Around Greenport Aalsmeer

**Greenport Aalsmeer.** The regional cluster around Aalsmeer brings together growers, exporters, wholesalers, logistics companies and technology suppliers. Many exporters and wholesalers are located on business parks around the auction, turning incoming flowers into mixed shipments for retailers and florists across Europe.

**Floricode.** This organisation maintains product coding and standards for the floriculture sector, including product codes and characteristics used in digital trading. Standardised product data is what makes digital marketplaces and analytics possible.

**Amsterdam Airport Schiphol.** A short distance from Aalsmeer, Schiphol handles flowers imported from producing countries in Africa and South America and exported to destinations worldwide. The proximity of the airport and the marketplace is central to the Dutch position in the flower trade.

**Inspection organisations.** The **Netherlands Food and Consumer Product Safety Authority (NVWA)** is responsible for plant health supervision, while inspection services such as **Naktuinbouw** and **KCB (Kwaliteits-Controle-Bureau)** carry out inspections in horticulture and the ornamental plant trade.

## Pricing a Product That Loses Value Every Hour

Most pricing problems assume that an unsold product can be sold tomorrow. Flowers cannot wait. That makes forecasting and pricing in floriculture unusually sensitive: overestimating demand means waste, underestimating it means missed revenue at exactly the moments that matter most, such as Valentine's Day or Mother's Day.

Prices depend on weather in producing countries, harvest timing, transport disruptions, holidays in consumer markets and even the colour trends of a season. A price forecasting model must deal with extreme seasonality, many product varieties with sparse histories and structural changes in how trade happens. For data scientists who enjoy difficult forecasting problems, few sectors offer this combination.

## Regulation and Standards

**EU Plant Health Regulation (EU) 2016/2031.** Plants and plant products entering and moving within the EU are subject to rules to prevent the spread of harmful organisms. Import inspections, phytosanitary certificates and plant passports create data flows that must be accurate and fast, because delays damage perishable goods.

**Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD).** Larger companies in the chain must report on sustainability, including emissions and value chain impacts. Floriculture involves greenhouse energy, air freight and water use, making reliable data on the chain a growing requirement. Sector certification schemes add further data demands.

**GDPR.** Trading platforms process data about buyers and growers, including business contacts and transactional behaviour.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Most marketplace and logistics applications are not high-risk, but organisations must ensure AI literacy among staff and consider transparency when AI influences prices or rankings for business users.

**Competition law.** Price information in a marketplace is sensitive. Data products that share market insight must be designed so they do not facilitate coordination between competitors — a consideration that shapes what analytics may be shown to whom.

## A Concrete Scenario: Recommending Products to Buyers

A digital marketplace wants to help buyers find products faster. A recommender system trained on past purchases suggests varieties that similar buyers bought. Early tests show a problem: the system keeps recommending the same popular varieties, pushing smaller growers with niche products further out of sight.

The team adds constraints to promote relevant diversity and gives growers insight into how their products are found. The technical solution is manageable; the harder part is agreeing on what a fair marketplace looks like in a cooperative owned by growers. This is typical of marketplace AI: ranking decisions are business and governance decisions, not only model outputs.

## A Common Misconception About Agricultural Trade

Candidates often associate agriculture with low digital maturity. Floriculture trade contradicts that assumption. The sector has worked with electronic auction systems for decades, uses standardised product data and operates a highly time-critical logistics system. The challenge for newcomers is not a lack of data but its complexity: many product varieties, many small trading parties and rapid change in how trade is organised.

## The Seasonal Calendar Behind the Data

Anyone joining a data team in floriculture learns the calendar quickly. Valentine's Day creates one of the sharpest demand peaks of the year, particularly for red roses. Mother's Day, which falls on different dates in different countries, creates a series of peaks that exporters must plan for market by market. International Women's Day matters in several Eastern European markets. Christmas and the end of the year bring their own assortment of plants and decorative products. Summer, by contrast, shifts demand towards garden plants.

Supply has its own seasons. In winter, a larger share of cut flowers comes from producing countries with favourable climates, such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Colombia, which increases the dependence on air freight and on customs and inspection processes. In spring and summer, European production plays a larger role.

For a forecasting model, this means that "last year" is often a poor guide: holidays move, weather in producing regions changes harvest timing and transport disruptions shift supply. Teams therefore combine statistical models with the knowledge of experienced traders — and the best data scientists in the sector learn to talk to those traders early and often.

## Working With Many Small Parties

The floriculture chain consists of thousands of growers, many exporters and wholesalers, and numerous logistics and service companies. Many of these are family businesses with limited IT capacity. For data professionals, this means integration is a recurring challenge: data arrives in different formats, with different levels of quality, and adoption of new digital tools depends on convincing busy business owners that the tool saves them time.

This makes the work more interesting than it may seem. Designing a data product for a sector of small businesses requires simplicity, reliability and a clear benefit — lessons that apply to many B2B marketplaces.

## Questions Worth Asking Before Joining

- **Which part of the trade does the team support — clock auction, direct digital trade or logistics?** Each has different data and different users.
- **How is data from growers and buyers collected and standardised?** Data quality is often the main constraint.
- **How are commercially sensitive price insights shared?** The answer shows awareness of competition law and marketplace fairness.
- **How do traders and planners use the models today?** Adoption by experienced professionals is the real test of value.
