Boxmeer is a small town on the Meuse in the far north-east of North Brabant, part of the municipality of Land van Cuijk since 2022. It is not a place many data scientists would list as a career destination. Yet it is the headquarters location of Hendrix Genetics, an international animal breeding company whose genetics are used in poultry, pig, turkey and aquaculture production around the world. Animal breeding has become one of the most data-intensive applications of genetics, and the region around Boxmeer sits inside a broader agrifood economy. For data professionals interested in genomics, statistics and food production, Boxmeer is a surprisingly concentrated opportunity.

## Why Animal Breeding Is a Data Discipline

Animal breeding aims to improve traits such as health, robustness, feed efficiency, fertility, product quality and animal welfare over generations. Traditionally, breeders selected animals based on observed performance and pedigree. Since the introduction of genomic selection, breeding decisions increasingly use DNA information: by genotyping animals and linking genetic markers to measured traits, breeders can estimate an animal's genetic potential earlier and more accurately.

This turns breeding into a large-scale data problem. Breeding programmes collect phenotype data from many animals across farms and countries, genotype large numbers of animals, and run statistical models to estimate breeding values. The decisions made from these models affect animals and food production for generations, which makes careful methodology essential.

## Genomic Prediction: Where Statistics and Machine Learning Meet

Genomic prediction is one of the most mature applications of statistical learning in the life sciences. The classical approach uses linear mixed models that relate genetic markers and pedigree information to observed traits. These models have strong theoretical foundations in quantitative genetics and have been used successfully in breeding for years.

Machine learning methods, including gradient boosting and neural networks, have been explored for genomic prediction, particularly for complex traits and non-additive genetic effects. In practice, the gains depend strongly on the trait, population and data size, and well-specified statistical models often remain hard to beat. For data scientists, this is an instructive field: it rewards understanding of genetics and statistics more than applying the newest model architecture.

The data engineering side is equally important. Genotype data from many animals, phenotypes from multiple sources and pedigree records must be integrated, cleaned and versioned, often across countries and species.

## Automated Phenotyping: Measuring What Matters

Breeding can only improve traits that can be measured. Some traits — such as body weight or egg production — are relatively easy to record. Others, including behaviour, welfare indicators, disease resistance and feed efficiency, are harder and more expensive to measure in large numbers of animals.

Sensors and computer vision are changing this. Cameras can track animal behaviour and movement, automated feeders record individual feed intake, and image analysis can measure physical characteristics consistently. For data professionals, automated phenotyping combines computer vision, sensor data processing and domain knowledge about animal biology. The results feed directly into breeding decisions, which means measurement errors or biases can have long-term consequences.

## Regulation and Ethics That Shape the Work

**EU Animal Breeding Regulation (EU) 2016/1012.** Sets rules for breeding programmes, including recognition of breeding organisations, performance testing and genetic evaluation for certain species, and zootechnical conditions for trade in breeding animals.

**EU Animal Health Law (EU) 2016/429.** Governs disease prevention and control, including rules on movements of animals and germinal products, which affects breeding companies that supply animals internationally.

**Animal welfare legislation.** EU and national rules on animal welfare influence breeding goals and data collection practices. Societal expectations increasingly emphasise welfare and robustness alongside productivity.

**GDPR.** Applies to data about farmers, employees and customers, and to farm-level data linked to individuals.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Most breeding and genomics applications are not high-risk, but organisations using AI must ensure AI literacy among staff and consider transparency and data governance.

**International trade rules.** Breeding companies trade genetics globally, which involves veterinary certificates, import rules and biosecurity requirements in many countries.

**Ethical considerations.** Breeding decisions affect animal welfare and genetic diversity. Responsible breeding programmes balance genetic progress with welfare and diversity, and data professionals contribute by making these trade-offs measurable.

## Why Genetic Diversity Is a Data Question

A breeding programme that selects too strongly on a few top animals makes fast progress in the short term but risks losing genetic diversity. Over generations, reduced diversity increases inbreeding, which can harm fertility, health and the population's ability to adapt to new challenges such as emerging diseases or changing production systems.

Managing this trade-off is a quantitative problem. Breeders monitor inbreeding levels and effective population size using pedigree and genomic data. Methods such as optimal contribution selection determine how many offspring each selected animal should contribute, maximising genetic progress while constraining the rate of inbreeding. Genomic data makes it possible to measure relatedness more precisely than pedigree alone, including identifying regions of the genome where diversity is being lost.

For data professionals, this adds an optimisation and simulation dimension to breeding work. Simulating breeding programmes over many generations helps companies evaluate strategies before implementing them — an area where computational skills directly influence the long-term sustainability of animal populations.

## A Concrete Scenario: Selecting for Robustness

A breeding programme wants to improve robustness — the ability of animals to remain healthy and productive under varying conditions. Robustness is difficult to measure directly. The data team combines records of mortality, disease treatments, growth under different farm conditions and sensor-based behaviour data to construct indicators.

Initial models suggest that some animal lines are more robust than others. But further analysis reveals that part of the difference is explained by the farms where animals were raised, not their genetics. The team adjusts the models to separate environmental and genetic effects more carefully and validates predictions across farms and countries. The final indicators are integrated into the breeding goal, with weights discussed between geneticists, veterinarians and commercial teams. The project shows how breeding data science combines statistics, biology and ethical choices.

## A Common Misconception About Agrifood Data Work

Many candidates assume that agrifood companies are less technologically advanced than tech companies. Animal breeding contradicts that assumption: genomic selection was one of the earliest large-scale applications of predictive modelling on genetic data, and breeding companies have used sophisticated statistics for decades. The difference is that results are measured over generations rather than product sprints — which suits people who value rigour and long-term impact.

## Questions Worth Asking Before Joining

- **Which species and breeding programmes will I work on?** Species differ strongly in generation intervals, data availability and traits.
- **How are phenotype data collected, and how reliable are they?** Data quality determines what models can achieve.
- **How does the company balance production traits with welfare and diversity?** The answer reveals the company's breeding philosophy.
- **What computing infrastructure is available for genomic analyses?** Large genotype datasets require significant compute.
- **How international is the role?** Breeding companies often work with farms, partners and colleagues in many countries.
