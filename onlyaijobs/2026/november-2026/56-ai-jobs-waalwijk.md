A century ago, the Langstraat — the string of towns between Tilburg and Den Bosch that includes Waalwijk — was the centre of the Dutch shoe and leather industry. Today, driving along the A59, you see a different landscape: vast distribution centres, truck parks and business parks where goods for webshops and retailers across Europe are stored, picked and shipped. Waalwijk has become one of the most frequently mentioned logistics locations in the Netherlands. A few kilometres away lies one of Europe's best-known theme parks, where millions of visitors per year create an entirely different kind of data. For data professionals who like operational problems, Waalwijk offers far more than its modest size suggests.

## From Leather to Logistics

The Langstraat's shoe and leather industry declined in the second half of the twentieth century as production moved to countries with lower labour costs. What remained was a strategic location: between the ports of Rotterdam and Antwerp and the German hinterland, with direct motorway access and space for large buildings. Municipalities developed business parks, and logistics service providers, retailers and webshops built distribution centres.

The rise of e-commerce accelerated this development. Online retail requires fulfilment centres that handle huge numbers of small orders, often with next-day or same-day delivery promises. Waalwijk and the surrounding region became one of the areas where such operations concentrated. The town's shoe history still echoes in local identity — including a museum dedicated to leather and shoes — but its economy is now firmly logistical.

## The Employers Behind Waalwijk's Data Market

**Fulfilment centres for e-commerce and retail.** Distribution centres in Waalwijk serve webshops, retail chains and brands, handling storage, order picking, packing, returns and shipping. Some are operated by retailers themselves; many are run by logistics service providers on behalf of clients.

**Logistics service providers.** Third-party logistics companies manage warehousing and transport for multiple clients, often with varying product ranges, seasonal peaks and service agreements. Planning and data analysis are central to profitability.

**Transport companies.** Road transport operators connect the distribution centres with ports, suppliers and customers across Europe, with planning, routing and emissions reporting as key data topics.

**Efteling.** The theme park in Kaatsheuvel, part of the neighbouring municipality of Loon op Zand, is one of the largest in Europe and a major regional employer. Visitor flows, queue times, ticketing, dynamic pricing, hotel occupancy and staff planning all involve data.

**Knowledge institutions nearby.** **Tilburg University**, the **Jheronimus Academy of Data Science (JADS)** in Den Bosch and **Breda University of Applied Sciences**, known for logistics and leisure and tourism programmes, supply data and logistics talent to the region.

## The Peak Problem: Planning for Black Friday

E-commerce logistics is defined by peaks. Black Friday, Cyber Monday, the Sinterklaas season and Christmas can multiply daily order volumes. A fulfilment centre must plan months in advance for extra staff, temporary storage and transport capacity — while clients' forecasts are often uncertain and promotions may change at short notice.

Data scientists in this environment work on forecasting order volumes per client and per day, translating forecasts into staffing plans, and monitoring actual volumes in real time to adjust. Errors are expensive in both directions: too few staff means delayed orders and penalties, too many means unnecessary costs in a low-margin business. The combination of uncertainty, operational constraints and financial pressure makes peak planning one of the most practical and valued data skills in logistics.

## Returns: The Hidden Half of E-commerce

In some product categories, such as fashion and shoes, a significant share of online orders is returned. Returns must be received, inspected, graded and either put back into stock, refurbished, sold through other channels or disposed of. Each step costs time and money, and returned goods lose value while waiting.

Data can help at several points: predicting return volumes to plan capacity, identifying products or customers with unusually high return rates, and deciding automatically where a returned item should go. Computer vision is increasingly explored for grading the condition of returned products. For a region with roots in the shoe trade, it is fitting that returns of fashion and footwear are now among its data challenges.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**Dutch Works Councils Act.** Warehouse analytics often involve measuring worker productivity. In companies with a works council, introducing systems that monitor employees generally requires the council's consent, and transparency towards workers is essential.

**Working Hours Act.** Staff planning must respect legal limits on working hours and rest periods, which become hard constraints in scheduling models.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems used to allocate tasks, monitor performance or make decisions affecting workers are listed among high-risk applications, with requirements for risk management, human oversight and transparency. This is directly relevant to algorithmic management in warehouses.

**GDPR.** Applies to employee data and to customer data in e-commerce and leisure, including ticketing and loyalty programmes.

**Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD).** Large companies must report on emissions, including transport emissions in their value chain. Standards such as **ISO 14083** and the **GLEC Framework** provide methods for calculating logistics emissions, creating demand for accurate transport data.

**Platform and labour market rules.** Much warehouse work involves temporary agency workers, and rules on agency work and equal treatment affect how workforce data and planning systems are organised.

## How Automation Is Changing Fulfilment Work

Labour shortages and rising wages have pushed logistics companies to automate. Goods-to-person systems bring shelves or totes to stationary pickers instead of pickers walking through aisles. Autonomous mobile robots transport goods within warehouses. Automated packing machines size boxes to individual orders. Sorting systems route parcels to the right trucks without manual handling.

Each step changes the data work. In a manual warehouse, the main data questions concern labour planning and pick paths. In an automated warehouse, they shift towards system throughput, bottleneck analysis, equipment reliability and how humans and machines work together. Because automation investments are large and long-term, companies use data and simulation to decide which processes to automate first and to verify that expected benefits are realised.

Automation also changes the workforce. Fewer people walk long distances, while more people are needed for exception handling, maintenance and supervision of automated systems. Data professionals who understand both the technical system and the human side of the operation help companies make these transitions without losing reliability — or the goodwill of the people on the floor.

## A Concrete Scenario: Predicting Queues at a Theme Park

A theme park wants to reduce frustration from long queues. Historical data includes ticket sales, weather forecasts, school holidays in different regions and countries, attraction capacity and observed queue times. A model predicts queue times per attraction throughout the day.

The first use is informational: visitors see predicted waiting times in an app. The next step is operational: staff can open extra capacity, adjust show schedules or guide visitors to less crowded areas. A more advanced step links the forecast to pricing and capacity management — encouraging visits on quieter days. Each step raises the bar for accuracy and trust, and each involves teams from operations, marketing and IT. The case shows that leisure analytics is not a soft domain: it is operations research with emotional stakes.

## A Common Misconception About Logistics Jobs

Many data professionals think logistics data work means building reports on warehouse output. In reality, modern fulfilment involves optimisation, forecasting, simulation and automation at scale, with direct impact on costs, delivery promises and working conditions. The margin for error is small and results are visible every day. Logistics companies also often give data professionals significant responsibility early, because data teams are smaller than in tech companies.

## Questions Worth Asking Before Joining

- **Does the company operate its own distribution centre or serve multiple clients?** Multi-client logistics adds complexity and variety.
- **How are forecasts from clients combined with the company's own data?** Client forecasts are often unreliable, and good teams have a clear process for this.
- **How are workers informed about analytics that measure their work?** The answer reveals the company's approach to responsible algorithmic management.
- **What automation is planned in the next few years?** Automation projects create interesting data and robotics work.
- **For leisure roles: how do data insights reach operational staff on busy days?** Real-time use shows the maturity of the analytics.

## Commuting and Living Near Waalwijk

Waalwijk has no railway station, but it lies on the A59 between Den Bosch and the A27, with bus connections to Tilburg and Den Bosch. Many employees commute by car or bike from surrounding towns such as Kaatsheuvel, Drunen, Sprang-Capelle and Heusden, or from the cities of Tilburg and Den Bosch, which are within a short drive. Housing is more affordable than in the Randstad, and the area offers easy access to nature, including the Loonse en Drunense Duinen National Park.

## Career Growth in Logistics and Leisure Data

Logistics data experience is widely transferable: the same forecasting, optimisation and simulation skills are valued in retail, manufacturing, healthcare logistics and transport. Within the region, the concentration of distribution centres means professionals can change employers without moving. Leisure analytics experience is less common and therefore distinctive, with applications in hospitality, events, travel and public venues. For data professionals in Brabant who assume that interesting operational data work requires moving to Rotterdam or Amsterdam, the Langstraat is a strong counterexample.
