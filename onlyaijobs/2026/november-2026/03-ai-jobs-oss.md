Oss is often mentioned in the same breath as meat processing and food history, but for more than a century the town has also been a pharmaceutical location. The research campus of the former Organon became Pivot Park, a life sciences park where drug discovery companies share laboratories, screening facilities and expertise. Pharmaceutical manufacturing continues nearby. The result is a small but serious market for data scientists who want to work on chemistry, biology and manufacturing data.

## From Company Research Site to Open Life Sciences Park

For decades, Oss was home to Organon's research and development. When pharmaceutical R&D in Oss was scaled down after the company changed hands, the site was not abandoned. Instead, with support from public partners, it was transformed into **Pivot Park**, an open campus where startups, scale-ups and service companies in drug discovery and development could use the existing infrastructure.

That history explains the character of the park: it combines deep, experienced pharmaceutical know-how with a younger generation of companies. Many professionals on the park have worked in large pharmaceutical research and now work in smaller, more specialised organisations.

## The Named Organisations Behind Oss's Life Sciences Market

**Pivot Park.** The life sciences park offers laboratories, offices and shared facilities to companies in drug discovery, development and related services. It is the most visible concentration of R&D employment in Oss.

**Pivot Park Screening Centre.** A screening centre that performs high-throughput screening of compound libraries for drug discovery projects, including participation in European public-private screening initiatives. High-throughput screening generates large, structured datasets that are a natural fit for data science.

**Organon.** The women's health company that was spun off from MSD in 2021 has manufacturing activities in Oss, continuing the pharmaceutical tradition of the town.

**Aspen Oss.** A pharmaceutical manufacturing site in Oss, active in the production of active pharmaceutical ingredients and medicines.

**Knowledge institutions nearby.** **Radboud University** and Radboud university medical center in Nijmegen and **HAN University of Applied Sciences** in Arnhem and Nijmegen supply life sciences researchers, bioinformaticians and laboratory professionals to the region.

## Two Very Different Types of Data Work

The pharmaceutical market in Oss contains two worlds that look similar from the outside but work very differently.

**Discovery data.** Screening results, assay data, chemical structures, dose-response curves and biological readouts. Data science here supports decisions about which compounds to pursue: hit identification, structure-activity relationship analysis, quality control of assay plates and, increasingly, machine learning models that predict properties of molecules before they are made.

**Manufacturing and quality data.** Process parameters, batch records, laboratory results and equipment data. Here data science supports process understanding, yield improvement, deviation analysis and predictive maintenance — within the strict framework of Good Manufacturing Practice.

| Aspect | Discovery data | Manufacturing data |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | Which molecules are worth pursuing? | Is every batch produced consistently and compliantly? |
| Typical methods | Cheminformatics, QSAR, active learning, assay analytics | Multivariate statistics, process analytics, anomaly detection |
| Tolerance for exploration | High | Low — changes must be validated |
| Regulatory framework | Research data integrity | GMP, GAMP 5, data integrity guidance |
| Typical employer | Biotech startups, screening and service companies | Manufacturing sites |

## Regulation and Data Integrity

**Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP).** Pharmaceutical manufacturing in the EU must comply with GMP requirements supervised by national authorities; in the Netherlands, the Health and Youth Care Inspectorate (IGJ) plays a central role. GMP affects everything from equipment qualification to how data is recorded and changed.

**GAMP 5.** The industry guidance for computerised systems in regulated environments describes a risk-based approach to validating software. A machine learning model used to support a GMP decision must fit into this validation framework.

**Data integrity principles.** Regulators expect data to be attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate — often summarised as ALCOA, with extensions for completeness and consistency. For data scientists, this means audit trails, controlled access and reproducible analysis are not optional.

**European Medicines Agency (EMA).** The EMA has published reflection work on the use of artificial intelligence across the medicinal product lifecycle, signalling that AI used in development and manufacturing will be assessed on its reliability and on the evidence behind it.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Many drug discovery applications fall outside the high-risk categories, but organisations must still ensure AI literacy among staff and consider transparency and data governance obligations.

## A Concrete Scenario: Cleaning Up Before Predicting

A small biotech company on the park wants to use its screening history to train a model that predicts which compounds are likely to be active. The first months are not spent on model architecture. They are spent discovering that assay protocols changed three times over the years, that some plates had edge effects, that compound identifiers were not consistent between an old and a new database, and that negative results were stored less carefully than positive ones.

Once these issues are resolved, a relatively simple model trained on clean, well-annotated data outperforms a sophisticated model trained on the raw export. This is one of the most consistent lessons in pharmaceutical data science: data curation determines the ceiling of what any model can achieve.

## A Common Misconception About AI in Drug Discovery

Headlines about AI designing new drugs create the impression that machine learning replaces laboratory work. In practice, models in drug discovery mostly help prioritise experiments: they narrow down which compounds to synthesise and test next. The laboratory remains the source of truth. Data scientists who understand this — and who enjoy working closely with chemists and biologists who design the experiments — are far more effective than those who expect the model to be the final answer.

## Skills and Tools That Stand Out

- **Cheminformatics.** Molecular representations, fingerprints, similarity searching and toolkits such as RDKit.
- **Statistics for experiments.** Dose-response modelling, plate normalisation, design of experiments and multivariate analysis.
- **Machine learning for molecules.** Property prediction, active learning and graph-based models, combined with honest validation using time or scaffold splits.
- **Laboratory data systems.** Familiarity with electronic lab notebooks, LIMS and structured data capture.
- **Computer system validation.** Understanding of GAMP 5 and data integrity for roles near manufacturing.
- **Scientific communication.** Explaining model output and uncertainty to scientists who decide what to make next.

A park with many small companies offers a different career path than a single large employer. Engineers and scientists often move between companies on the same campus, building broad experience across projects and modalities. The downside is less stability: small biotech companies depend on funding and partnership milestones. Candidates should evaluate the financial position of a company before joining, while recognising that experience gained on the park remains valuable to the next employer across the street.

## An Industrial Town With Deep Roots in Processing

Oss's industrial identity did not start with pharmaceuticals. The town was a centre of margarine and meat processing in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: the margarine producers Jurgens and Van den Bergh, both from Oss, were among the companies whose later mergers led to the formation of Unilever. Organon itself grew out of the meat processing industry, using animal organs as raw material for hormone preparations — which is where the company name came from.

That history is more than a curiosity. It explains why Oss has a culture of process industry, quality control and large-scale production, and why manufacturing expertise remains strong in the region today. For data professionals, it means the local labour market values people who understand processes, not only models.

## Why Negative Results Are a Hidden Asset

In drug discovery, most compounds tested are inactive, and most ideas do not work. For decades, many organisations treated these negative results as less important than positive ones: they were stored less carefully, annotated less completely and sometimes not stored in structured form at all.

For machine learning, this is a serious problem. A model that learns only from successes cannot learn where the boundaries of activity lie. Companies that have systematically captured negative results, with consistent protocols and metadata, have a data asset that competitors cannot easily copy. Data scientists who join a discovery organisation often find that one of their most valuable early contributions is not a model at all, but a better data capture process that makes future models possible.

## Working on a Shared Campus

A life sciences park changes the social structure of work. Scientists from different companies share facilities, meet in the same canteen and sometimes collaborate on projects through service agreements. Knowledge travels faster than inside a single corporate site, and so do job opportunities: many moves on a campus like Pivot Park happen through personal networks before a vacancy is ever published.

For a data scientist who is new to the region, this means visibility matters. Attending campus events, meeting people from the screening centre and service companies, and understanding which companies work with which types of data can open doors that a search engine will not show.

## Questions to Ask Before Joining a Small Biotech

- **How long is the current funding runway, and what milestone does the next round depend on?** Small drug discovery companies live from milestone to milestone.
- **Who owns the data I will work with?** In partnered programmes, data may belong to a pharmaceutical partner, which affects what you can use and publish.
- **Is there an existing data infrastructure, or will I build it?** A first data hire often spends the first year on foundations.
- **How closely will I work with chemists and biologists?** Daily contact with experimental scientists is the strongest predictor of useful models.
- **What happens to my work if a programme is stopped?** Portfolio decisions are frequent; understanding how the company handles them shows how resilient the role is.
