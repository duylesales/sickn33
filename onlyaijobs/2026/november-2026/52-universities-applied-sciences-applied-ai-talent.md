Public debate about AI talent in the Netherlands often focuses on research universities, PhD programmes and international top talent. Yet the practical adoption of AI in regional manufacturing companies, logistics firms, healthcare organisations, municipalities and agrifood businesses depends heavily on another part of the education system: universities of applied sciences (hogescholen). They educate large numbers of ICT, data, engineering, business and healthcare professionals, and many of their graduates work in the region where they studied. Their lectorates conduct practice-based research directly with small and medium-sized enterprises. For regional AI adoption, these institutions are not a secondary channel — they are often the main one.

## Why Universities of Applied Sciences Matter for AI Adoption

**Scale and regional reach.** Universities of applied sciences have campuses across the country, including in cities and regions without a research university. Their students often come from the surrounding region and many stay after graduation.

**Practice orientation.** Programmes emphasise applying knowledge in professional contexts, with internships, projects and graduation assignments at companies and organisations.

**SME connections.** Lecturers, lectors and students work with regional businesses that rarely collaborate with research universities, including companies taking their first steps with data.

**Broad AI literacy.** AI is not only a subject for computer science students. Programmes in logistics, healthcare, business, engineering, agriculture and education increasingly include data and AI components — exactly the combination of domain and data skills many employers need.

## The Named Instruments and Organisations

**Netherlands Association of Universities of Applied Sciences (Vereniging Hogescholen).** The association represents universities of applied sciences and coordinates sector-wide themes, including practice-based research and digitalisation.

**Lectorates.** A lector leads a research group (lectoraat) focused on practice-based research in a specific theme, connecting education, professional practice and research. Many universities of applied sciences have lectorates related to data science, AI, smart industry, digital health or logistics innovation.

**Centres of Expertise.** These public-private partnerships bring together universities of applied sciences, companies and public organisations around specific sectors or themes, combining education, research and innovation.

**Taskforce for Applied Research SIA (Regieorgaan SIA).** Part of the Dutch Research Council (NWO), SIA funds practice-based research at universities of applied sciences, including through **RAAK programmes** that support collaboration with SMEs and public organisations.

**Professional Doctorate.** The Netherlands has been piloting a professional doctorate at universities of applied sciences, a third-cycle programme focused on designing solutions for complex practical problems — a potential route for advanced applied AI expertise.

**Associate degree programmes.** Two-year higher professional education programmes offer shorter routes into data and IT roles and can attract working adults.

## How HBO Graduates Fit the AI Labour Market

| Role | Contribution of HBO graduates |
|---|---|
| Data analyst and BI specialist | Strong fit, combining technical skills with business context |
| Data engineer | Strong fit, particularly with software engineering background |
| Machine learning engineer | Increasingly common, especially for applied roles |
| Data scientist | Possible, particularly in applied settings; research-heavy roles often prefer university graduates |
| AI product owner or domain specialist | Strong fit for graduates from domain programmes with data skills |
| Automation and smart industry engineer | Strong fit for engineering graduates working with sensor and production data |

Employers who define roles by actual tasks rather than by degree level often find that HBO graduates are among the most practical and quickly productive candidates for applied AI work.

## Graduate Destinations and Labour Market Information

The **HBO-Monitor**, a national survey of recent graduates coordinated with the **Research Centre for Education and the Labour Market (ROA)**, provides information about graduates' labour market position, including whether they found work matching their education. Such data help institutions and regions understand outcomes. However, national surveys are general and do not show in real time which regional employers need which AI and data skills. Combining graduate surveys with current vacancy data gives a more complete picture.

## The Visibility Challenge for Internships and Graduate Jobs

Universities of applied sciences depend on internships and graduation assignments at companies. Yet many regional SMEs with suitable data projects never offer them, because they do not know how, do not think of their work as "AI" or cannot find students. At the same time, students search for internships in the same places they search for jobs — often large platforms dominated by bigger employers.

Making regional internships, graduation projects and entry-level data roles visible at exact location helps both sides. Students find opportunities within reasonable travel distance, and SMEs reach students who might otherwise never consider them.

## How Universities of Applied Sciences Can Strengthen the Link With Employers

**1. Map regional demand.** Use lectorate contacts, Centres of Expertise and vacancy data to identify which regional employers need data skills.

**2. Help SMEs formulate projects.** Many SMEs need support in turning a vague ambition — "doing something with data" — into a feasible student project.

**3. Integrate AI literacy across programmes.** Since February 2025, the **EU AI Act** requires organisations using AI to ensure AI literacy among staff. Graduates who understand AI's possibilities and risks in their own field are valuable to employers facing this obligation.

**4. Offer lifelong learning.** Short courses and part-time programmes help regional employers upskill employees, strengthening the relationship beyond student placements.

**5. Promote regional careers to students and alumni.** Share examples of alumni working with data at regional employers and publish internships and graduate roles where students search.

**6. Collaborate with regional partners.** Provinces, regional development agencies, municipalities and business associations can amplify outreach to employers.

## A Concrete Scenario: A Lectorate Connecting SMEs and Students

A lectorate in smart manufacturing at a university of applied sciences works with several regional machine builders. Through RAAK-funded research, the lector discovers that many companies collect machine data but do not use it. Students in engineering and ICT programmes are eager to work on real data but struggle to find companies offering such projects.

The lectorate organises short intake sessions with SMEs to define feasible data projects and publishes graduation assignments and junior data roles from these companies where students search for jobs and internships. Within a year, several graduation projects lead to predictive maintenance prototypes, and three graduates are hired by participating companies. The collaboration also results in a short course for company employees on working with machine data.

## What Employers Say They Need From Graduates

Conversations with regional employers about data and AI roles tend to highlight similar needs:

- **Practical data skills:** working with messy real-world data, SQL and at least one programming language.
- **Understanding of the domain:** knowing how a production line, logistics operation or care process works.
- **Communication:** explaining results to colleagues without technical backgrounds.
- **Critical thinking about AI:** recognising when a model is unreliable, biased or unnecessary.
- **Professional practices:** version control, documentation and working in teams.

Universities of applied sciences are well placed to deliver this combination, because their programmes combine domain knowledge with practical projects. Structured feedback from regional employers, for example through advisory boards and Centres of Expertise, helps keep curricula aligned with these needs.

## Student Teams, Challenges and Hackathons

Beyond individual internships, many universities of applied sciences organise team-based formats in which groups of students work on a company's problem for several weeks or a semester. Data challenges and hackathons bring together students from ICT, business and domain programmes to explore a question with real data in a short period.

For regional employers, these formats offer a low-threshold introduction to working with students and data. A company can test several ideas at once, see how students approach problems and identify talented individuals for internships or jobs. For students, team projects build collaboration skills and a portfolio of practical work. Institutions can increase impact by selecting challenges from regional SMEs, ensuring data is available before the project starts and following up with companies afterwards to discuss next steps, such as a graduation assignment or vacancy.

## Pathways From MBO to HBO

Many data and IT professionals follow a route from vocational education (MBO) to higher professional education (HBO). MBO graduates in ICT, laboratory work or technical fields can progress to HBO programmes in data science, software engineering or engineering, often bringing strong practical skills. Some MBO institutions have practice-based research positions known as practoraten, which also work with regional employers.

Supporting these pathways — through bridging programmes, recognition of prior learning and cooperation between MBO and HBO institutions — widens the regional talent pool for AI and data roles and gives more young people access to technical careers without leaving their region.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Treating AI as a topic only for ICT programmes,** missing domain-specific demand.
- **Relying on a small group of large partner companies,** while SMEs remain disconnected.
- **Projects that are too ambitious for students** or lack company supervision.
- **Limited visibility of internships and graduate roles** outside institutional systems.
- **Little follow-up** on graduate destinations and employer satisfaction.

## A Lifelong Learning Offer for Regional Employers

Regional employers do not only need new graduates; they also need existing employees who can work with data and AI. Universities of applied sciences are well placed to provide this through part-time programmes, short courses and customised training.

Effective lifelong learning offers for AI and data share several characteristics. They are modular, so employees can combine learning with work. They use cases from the participants' own sector, such as production data for manufacturing or care registration data for healthcare. They include practical assignments that participants apply in their own organisations. And they address responsible use of AI, which helps employers meet the AI Act's AI literacy obligation.

Such offers also strengthen institutional relationships with employers: companies that send employees to courses are more likely to offer internships, participate in research projects and hire graduates. Funding options for employers, such as the SLIM scheme for SMEs and sector training funds, can make participation more accessible.

## Measuring Regional Impact

Universities of applied sciences are increasingly asked to demonstrate their regional impact. For AI and data, useful indicators include:

- **Graduates working in the region** in data-related roles, using the HBO-Monitor and alumni data.
- **Number of regional organisations** offering internships and graduation projects in AI and data.
- **Practice-based research projects** with regional SMEs and public organisations, and their outcomes.
- **Participation of regional employees** in lifelong learning programmes.
- **Regional vacancy trends** for roles matching programme profiles.

Combining these indicators with qualitative stories — a lectorate helping a company deploy its first model, or an alumnus leading data work at a regional employer — gives policymakers and partners a convincing picture of the institution's role in regional AI adoption.

## Key Takeaways

- Universities of applied sciences are central to regional AI adoption through their scale, practice orientation and SME connections.
- Lectorates, Centres of Expertise, SIA and RAAK programmes, professional doctorate pilots and associate degrees provide instruments for applied AI.
- HBO graduates fit many applied AI and data roles, particularly when employers define roles by tasks rather than degree level.
- The HBO-Monitor provides graduate outcome data; combining it with current vacancy data improves insight into regional demand.
- Helping SMEs formulate projects, integrating AI literacy and increasing visibility of internships and graduate roles strengthen links with employers.
