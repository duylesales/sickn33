A single vacancy for a data scientist can generate hundreds of CVs, motivation letters, assessment results, interview notes, reference checks and emails. All of it is personal data. Many employers handle this information informally: CVs forwarded between managers, spreadsheets with candidate comments stored indefinitely, quick online searches about candidates, rejected applications kept "just in case". For companies hiring AI and data professionals, this carries an extra reputational risk: candidates who work with data every day are more likely to notice — and question — careless handling of their own. The GDPR sets clear boundaries, and Dutch regulators and rules add practical specifics.

This article provides general information, not legal advice.

## The Principles That Apply to Every Recruitment Process

The GDPR's core principles translate directly into recruitment practice:

- **Lawfulness, fairness and transparency:** candidates must know what happens with their data.
- **Purpose limitation:** data collected for a specific vacancy should not be reused for unrelated purposes without a legal basis.
- **Data minimisation:** collect only what is necessary to assess suitability.
- **Accuracy:** assessments and notes should be factual and relevant.
- **Storage limitation:** delete data when it is no longer needed.
- **Integrity and confidentiality:** protect data against unauthorised access.
- **Accountability:** be able to demonstrate compliance.

## Legal Basis for Processing Applicant Data

Employers typically rely on steps taken at the candidate's request prior to entering into a contract, or on legitimate interests, for processing application data. Consent plays a more limited role in recruitment because of the imbalance of power between employer and candidate, but it is relevant for specific purposes, such as keeping a candidate's data for future vacancies.

**Special categories of data.** Data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union membership, health, sexual orientation or biometric data for identification is subject to stricter rules. In recruitment, employers should generally avoid collecting such data. Diversity monitoring can be possible under specific conditions, but requires careful legal assessment.

**Citizen service number (BSN).** Under Dutch law, the BSN may only be processed where a legal provision permits it. Employers need the BSN for payroll after hiring, not during selection, so it should not be requested from applicants.

## Retention: How Long May You Keep Application Data?

The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** has indicated that application data should generally be deleted no later than four weeks after the end of the application procedure. With the candidate's consent, the data can be kept for longer — for example, up to one year — to consider the candidate for future vacancies. The data of the candidate who is hired becomes part of the personnel file.

In practice, this means employers need:

- A clear retention schedule in their applicant tracking system.
- A consent mechanism for longer retention.
- A process to delete data from emails, shared drives and personal notes, not only from the main system.

## Online Screening of Candidates

Searching for candidates online — on professional networks, code repositories or social media — is common, particularly for technical roles where public work is relevant. Under the GDPR, online screening must be necessary and proportionate to the role, limited to relevant information and transparent to candidates.

Practical guidelines:

- **Inform candidates in advance** that online information may be reviewed, for example in the vacancy or privacy notice.
- **Focus on professional information,** such as public code, publications or professional profiles, rather than private social media.
- **Give candidates the opportunity to respond** if online information influences a decision.
- **Do not create profiles** with unrelated personal information or store screenshots unnecessarily.

For AI and data candidates, reviewing a public repository the candidate has shared in their application is generally relevant and expected. Searching private social media accounts is rarely justified.

## References

Contacting references can provide valuable information, but should be done with the candidate's knowledge and permission. The **NVP Recruitment Code** used by many Dutch employers states that references should only be contacted with the candidate's consent. Ask job-related questions, record only relevant information and inform the candidate if references influence the outcome.

## Certificates of Conduct and Background Screening

**Certificate of Conduct (VOG).** For some positions, employers ask candidates for a Certificate of Conduct (Verklaring Omtrent het Gedrag), issued by **Justis** on behalf of the Ministry of Justice and Security. The VOG does not disclose criminal records to the employer; it states whether the candidate's history is relevant to the specific risks of the position. Employers must specify the relevant screening profile. For AI and data roles with access to sensitive data or financial systems, a VOG can be appropriate.

**Pre-employment screening.** More extensive screening — verifying diplomas, previous employment or financial integrity — is common in financial services, government and security-sensitive roles. It must be proportionate to the position, transparent to candidates and conducted only at an appropriate stage, usually for selected candidates.

## Medical Questions and Examinations

The **Medical Examinations Act** restricts pre-employment medical examinations: they are only allowed when a position involves special requirements regarding medical fitness, and questions about health are generally not permitted during recruitment. For AI and data roles, it is difficult to imagine medical requirements that would justify a pre-employment examination. Recruiters should not ask about health, sick leave history or pregnancy.

Candidates may ask for reasonable accommodations in the selection process, such as extra time for an assessment. Employers should handle such requests confidentially and process only the information necessary to arrange the accommodation.

## Automated Decision-Making and AI Tools

If recruitment tools automatically reject or rank candidates, **Article 22 of the GDPR** on automated individual decision-making may apply, requiring safeguards such as human intervention. Systematic evaluation of candidates using new technologies is also likely to require a **data protection impact assessment**. Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems used to filter applications or evaluate candidates are classified as high-risk, with obligations for deployers including human oversight and informing candidates.

## International Recruitment Tools and Data Transfers

Many applicant tracking systems, assessment platforms and video interview tools are provided by companies outside the EU. Transfers of personal data outside the European Economic Area require a valid mechanism, such as an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards. For transfers to the United States, the **EU–US Data Privacy Framework** allows transfers to certified US companies under an adequacy decision. Employers should also conclude data processing agreements with vendors acting as processors and verify where data is stored.

## Recruitment Agencies and Shared Responsibilities

When working with recruitment agencies, clarify roles. Agencies may act as independent controllers for their own candidate databases, while acting as processors for specific tasks performed on behalf of the employer. Agree on how candidates are informed, how data is shared and how long it is retained. Avoid receiving CVs from agencies for candidates who have not agreed to be presented.

## Candidates' Rights in Practice

Candidates have the right to access their data, including assessments and interview notes, subject to limitations that protect others' rights. They may request correction of inaccurate data, deletion when data is no longer needed and object to certain processing. Employers must respond within one month, extendable in certain cases.

Interview notes should therefore be written as if the candidate might read them: factual, job-related and respectful.

## Technical Assessments and Candidate Code

Hiring for AI and data roles often involves technical assessments: take-home assignments, coding platforms, shared notebooks or presentations. These create additional personal data and some specific privacy questions.

**Assessment platforms.** Online coding and assessment platforms process candidate data, sometimes including recordings, keystroke data or behavioural signals intended to detect cheating. Check what data is collected, whether it is necessary, where it is stored and whether the platform acts as a processor under a data processing agreement. Features that analyse behaviour or biometrics require particular caution.

**Test datasets.** Never share real personal data with candidates in assignments. Use public, synthetic or properly anonymised data.

**Submissions.** Candidate submissions — code, reports, presentations — are personal data linked to the candidate and should follow the same retention rules as other application data. Do not reuse candidates' work for business purposes without agreement.

**Evaluation notes.** Scores and comments on technical work should be based on predefined criteria and written factually, as candidates can request access to them.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Spreadsheet That Never Disappeared

A scale-up recruiting data engineers keeps a shared spreadsheet with candidate names, links to profiles, salary expectations and informal comments from interviewers. After two years, the spreadsheet contains hundreds of candidates, including comments such as "seems too old for startup pace" and "not a culture fit".

A rejected candidate submits an access request. The company discovers the spreadsheet, realises that retention periods were not respected and that some comments could suggest discriminatory reasoning. It responds to the request, deletes outdated data, introduces an applicant tracking system with automatic retention settings, trains interviewers to write job-related notes and updates its privacy notice. The incident costs time and reputation — but it is resolved before it reaches a regulator.

## A Common Misconception

Many employers assume that GDPR compliance in recruitment is mainly about having a privacy notice. In practice, most risks arise from everyday behaviour: forwarding CVs by email, informal notes, unlimited retention, unnecessary online searches and unclear agreements with agencies and vendors. Another misconception is that small companies are exempt. The GDPR applies to organisations of all sizes, although the measures should be proportionate.

## A Recruitment Privacy Checklist

- **Privacy notice for candidates** explaining purposes, legal bases, retention, online screening and rights.
- **Data minimisation** in application forms: no BSN, no health questions, no unnecessary personal data.
- **Retention schedule:** deletion within about four weeks after the procedure unless consent for longer retention.
- **Secure systems** instead of email chains and shared spreadsheets.
- **Job-related interview notes.**
- **References only with consent.**
- **VOG or screening only where proportionate** and at the appropriate stage.
- **DPIA and human oversight** for AI and automated tools.
- **Processor agreements and transfer mechanisms** for vendors.
- **Clear agreements with recruitment agencies.**
- **Process for handling access and deletion requests.**

## Key Takeaways

- Recruitment involves extensive personal data; GDPR principles of minimisation, transparency, storage limitation and security apply throughout.
- The Dutch Data Protection Authority indicates deletion of application data within four weeks after the procedure, or longer with consent.
- Online screening, references, VOGs and background checks must be relevant, proportionate and transparent.
- Health questions are generally not permitted, and the BSN should not be requested during selection.
- AI tools require attention to automated decision-making rules, DPIAs and AI Act obligations; vendors and agencies need clear agreements.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: recruiter keeping CVs for future roles) How long may we keep rejected candidates' data?
Generally up to four weeks after the procedure ends, according to the Dutch Data Protection Authority, or longer — for example up to a year — with the candidate's consent.

### (Scenario: hiring manager checking candidates online) May we search for candidates online?
Only when necessary and proportionate, limited to relevant professional information and with candidates informed in advance.

### (Scenario: employer asking for identification) Can we ask applicants for their BSN?
No — the BSN may only be processed where the law permits it, which typically applies after hiring for payroll purposes.

### (Scenario: employer hiring for sensitive data roles) Can we require a Certificate of Conduct?
Yes, when proportionate to the risks of the position, using the relevant screening profile, usually for the selected candidate.

### (Scenario: candidate requesting interview notes) Do we have to share interview notes with candidates?
Candidates can request access to their personal data, which can include notes, subject to limitations protecting others' rights.
