For every AI or data role filled, dozens of other candidates hear "no" — or hear nothing at all. Many employers treat rejection as an administrative afterthought: a template email sent weeks later, or silence after an interview. In the Dutch AI and data community, that is a costly habit. The community is small enough that people meet at meetups, study together, work at the same clients and share experiences in online groups. A candidate rejected today may be the ideal hire next year, a future customer or the colleague who recommends — or warns against — your company. How you reject candidates is part of your employer brand.

## Why Rejection Quality Matters More in AI Hiring

**Small, connected community.** Experienced ML engineers and data scientists often know each other through universities, conferences, open-source projects and previous employers.

**High investment by candidates.** AI and data hiring processes often include technical assessments or take-home assignments. Candidates who invest hours and then receive no response or feedback are likely to share that experience.

**Future talent pipeline.** Strong runners-up for one role are frequently excellent candidates for the next vacancy.

**Public reviews.** Employer review platforms and professional networks make candidate experiences visible to future applicants.

**Legal and privacy obligations.** Rejections involve personal data and must respect equal treatment rules.

## What Candidates Expect at Each Stage

| Stage | Reasonable candidate expectation | Recommended employer practice |
|---|---|---|
| After applying | Confirmation of receipt and an indication of timeline | Automatic confirmation with expected response time |
| After CV screening | A decision within a reasonable period | Short, respectful rejection within the stated timeline |
| After first interview | Personal communication | Email or call with brief reasons if possible |
| After technical assessment | Feedback proportional to effort | Short written feedback on key strengths and gaps |
| After final round | Personal call and meaningful feedback | Call from hiring manager, invitation to stay in touch |

The **NVP Recruitment Code**, which many Dutch employers use, emphasises informing candidates about the procedure, handling applications within reasonable periods and providing reasons for rejection upon request.

## Timing: The Most Important Factor

Silence is worse than rejection. Candidates can accept a "no"; what damages trust is waiting weeks without information. Practical rules:

- **State timelines in the vacancy** and communicate when they change.
- **Reject early-stage candidates promptly** once it is clear they will not progress, rather than keeping them as backup for weeks.
- **Inform finalists as soon as a decision is made,** ideally within days.
- **If the process is delayed,** send a short update rather than waiting until a decision exists.

## How to Write a Good Rejection Message

A good rejection message is short, respectful and clear. It includes:

- **Thanks** for the candidate's time and interest.
- **A clear decision** without ambiguity.
- **A brief reason where appropriate,** particularly after interviews.
- **An offer of feedback or contact** for later-stage candidates.
- **Information about data retention** and, if relevant, an invitation to join a talent pool with consent.

Avoid overly generic phrases such as "we received many excellent applications" as the only explanation after an interview, and avoid statements that could imply discriminatory reasons.

## Giving Feedback Without Legal Risk

Many employers avoid giving feedback out of fear of legal disputes. In practice, well-formulated feedback reduces risk and improves trust.

**Base feedback on job-related criteria.** Refer to the requirements and assessment criteria defined before the process, such as depth of experience in deploying models or clarity in explaining evaluation choices.

**Be specific and factual.** "Your solution did not include validation across time, which is important for our forecasting work" is useful and defensible. "Not a culture fit" is vague and can raise suspicion of bias.

**Avoid references to protected characteristics.** Comments about age, nationality, family situation, religion or similar characteristics — even indirectly, such as "we are looking for a young team" — can indicate discrimination under the **General Equal Treatment Act** and related legislation. Candidates who suspect discrimination can request an opinion from the **Netherlands Institute for Human Rights**.

**Keep notes consistent with feedback.** Under the **GDPR**, candidates can request access to their personal data, which may include interview notes. Feedback and notes should tell the same story.

## Rejecting Candidates After Technical Assessments

Candidates who complete take-home assignments or technical tests deserve feedback proportional to their effort. A short structured response is often sufficient:

1. **One or two strengths,** such as clear code structure or a good baseline.
2. **One or two key gaps** relative to the role's requirements.
3. **An indication of how close the candidate was,** if appropriate.

This takes an interviewer perhaps fifteen minutes but significantly changes how candidates perceive the company. For AI candidates, specific technical feedback is also seen as a signal of a mature team.

## Automated Rejections and AI Tools

Many applicant tracking systems send automated rejections, and some use AI to screen applications. Under the **GDPR**, candidates have rights regarding decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them, including human intervention. Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems that filter applications or evaluate candidates are high-risk, and deployers must ensure human oversight and inform candidates when such systems are used.

Practical implications: avoid fully automated rejections without human review, especially after candidates have invested effort, and make it easy for candidates to ask questions or request review.

AI tools can also help write rejection messages. Use them for tone and clarity, but ensure that reasons are accurate and personalised where it matters. Candidates in AI roles often recognise generic generated text quickly.

## Talent Pools and Data Retention

Strong candidates who were not selected can be valuable for future roles. Under Dutch data protection guidance, application data should generally be deleted within about four weeks after the procedure ends, unless the candidate consents to longer retention, for example up to one year, to be considered for future vacancies. The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** supervises compliance.

A good practice is to ask strong runners-up explicitly whether they want to be kept in a talent pool, explain for how long and for what purpose, and contact them personally when a relevant role opens.

## Rejecting Internal Candidates

Internal candidates who apply for a new AI or data role and are rejected require special care. They remain colleagues and may feel their development is not valued. Provide a personal conversation, concrete feedback and a development perspective, such as training, mentoring or a future opportunity. Handling internal rejections poorly can lead to the loss of employees with valuable domain knowledge.

## Handling Candidates Who Challenge a Decision

Occasionally a rejected candidate disagrees with the decision, asks for a detailed explanation or suggests that the process was unfair. How the employer responds matters for both reputation and legal risk.

**Respond calmly and promptly.** Acknowledge the message and explain when the candidate can expect a substantive response.

**Review the process internally.** Check whether the assessment followed the predefined criteria, whether notes are consistent and whether all candidates were treated comparably.

**Explain the criteria, not the comparison.** Candidates are entitled to reasons related to their own assessment, but not to confidential information about other candidates.

**Handle access requests correctly.** If the candidate requests access to personal data, respond within the GDPR timeframe and provide data such as notes and assessment results, subject to limitations protecting others' rights.

**Take discrimination concerns seriously.** If the candidate suggests discrimination, involve HR or legal advisers and review the case carefully. Candidates can request an opinion from the Netherlands Institute for Human Rights, and a careful, documented process is the best protection.

**Learn from the case.** Even if the decision stands, challenges can reveal weaknesses in communication, criteria or documentation that are worth fixing.

Most challenges end when candidates receive a respectful, specific explanation. Escalation is far more likely when they feel ignored.

## Measuring Candidate Experience

Organisations that take candidate experience seriously measure it. Options include:

- **Time to respond** at each stage.
- **Percentage of candidates** who receive a response.
- **Short surveys** for candidates after interviews, including those rejected.
- **Monitoring public reviews** and responding constructively.
- **Tracking rehires** — candidates rejected once and hired later.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Runner-Up Who Became a Hire

A scale-up hiring a senior ML engineer has two strong finalists. It selects one candidate. The hiring manager calls the other finalist the same day, explains that the decision was close, gives specific feedback about experience with large-scale deployment and asks whether they may keep the candidate's details for future roles. The candidate agrees.

Four months later, the scale-up opens a new role focused on model evaluation — an area where the runner-up excelled. The recruiter contacts the candidate personally. The candidate, impressed by the respectful rejection, accepts an interview and is hired within three weeks, saving the company time and recruitment costs.

## A Common Misconception

Many employers believe that giving feedback to rejected candidates creates legal risk and costs too much time. In reality, feedback based on predefined, job-related criteria is both defensible and efficient, while silence or vague explanations are more likely to create frustration and suspicion. Another misconception is that candidate experience matters only for hired candidates. In a small AI talent market, the experiences of rejected candidates shape your reputation far more, because there are many more of them.

## Rejection Messages for Different Situations

Different situations call for different messages. A few principles per situation help recruiters respond consistently.

**Early screening rejection.** Keep it short and timely. Thank the candidate, state the decision clearly and, where true, mention that the rejection is based on the requirements of this specific role. Include information about data retention.

**Rejection after a first interview.** Personalise the message. Refer to the conversation, give one or two job-related reasons and offer the possibility to ask questions.

**Rejection after a technical assessment.** Include structured feedback on the submission, acknowledge the time invested and mention whether the candidate may apply for future roles.

**Rejection of a finalist.** Call rather than email where possible. Explain that the decision was close if that is the case, give specific feedback and ask whether the candidate wants to stay in contact or join a talent pool.

**Vacancy cancelled.** Be honest that the role will not be filled, for example because of changed priorities or budget, so candidates do not assume they were rejected on merit.

**Internal candidate.** Hold a conversation in person, provide concrete feedback and discuss development opportunities.

Standardising these message types in the applicant tracking system — while leaving room for personalisation — saves time and improves consistency.

## A Rejection Process Checklist

- **Timelines stated in vacancies and updated when they change.**
- **Prompt rejections at early stages.**
- **Personal communication after interviews.**
- **Structured feedback after technical assessments and final rounds.**
- **Feedback based on predefined, job-related criteria.**
- **No references to protected characteristics.**
- **Human review of automated rejections and transparency about AI tools.**
- **Consent-based talent pool with clear retention periods.**
- **Special care for internal candidates.**
- **Measurement of candidate experience.**

## Key Takeaways

- In the connected Dutch AI community, how you reject candidates shapes your employer brand and future pipeline.
- Timely communication matters more than perfect wording; silence damages trust most.
- Give feedback proportional to candidates' effort, based on job-related criteria and consistent with interview notes.
- Respect GDPR retention rules and access rights, avoid discriminatory statements and ensure human oversight of automated rejections under the AI Act.
- Use consent-based talent pools and handle internal candidates with particular care.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer with many applicants) Do we have to respond to every applicant?
Responding to every applicant is good practice and part of recruitment codes such as the NVP Recruitment Code; automated confirmations and prompt rejections make it feasible.

### (Scenario: hiring manager worried about legal risk) Is it risky to give feedback to rejected candidates?
Feedback based on predefined, job-related criteria and free of references to protected characteristics is generally defensible and builds trust.

### (Scenario: recruiter keeping strong candidates) Can we keep rejected candidates' data for future vacancies?
Yes, with the candidate's consent and for a limited period, such as up to one year; otherwise delete data within about four weeks.

### (Scenario: employer using an applicant tracking system) Can we use automated rejections?
Only with care — decisions based solely on automated processing trigger GDPR safeguards, and AI screening tools require human oversight under the AI Act.

### (Scenario: employer after a take-home assignment) How much feedback should candidates receive after a technical assessment?
A short structured response with key strengths, main gaps and an indication of how close they were is usually appropriate.
