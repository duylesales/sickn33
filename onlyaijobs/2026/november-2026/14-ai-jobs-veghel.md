If you have checked in a suitcase at a major airport or received a parcel from a large webshop, there is a fair chance it travelled over equipment designed in Veghel. The town, part of the municipality of Meierijstad in North Brabant, hosts the headquarters of Vanderlande, a global leader in material handling automation. It is also home to the head office of Jumbo, one of the largest Dutch supermarket chains, and to a large Mars chocolate factory. For data scientists and engineers interested in logistics, robotics and food production, few towns of this size offer so much concentrated work — and few are so rarely included in a job search.

## Why Veghel Became a Logistics and Food Hub

Veghel lies on the Zuid-Willemsvaart canal and the A50 motorway, between Den Bosch, Eindhoven and Nijmegen. Its location and a strong agricultural hinterland made it a natural place for food processing and distribution. Over the decades, companies in food production, logistics and material handling grew here, and the town developed large business parks focused on food and logistics.

The region is part of **Agrifood Capital**, the cooperation of companies, knowledge institutions and governments in Northeast Brabant that positions the region around agrifood, logistics and technology. Veghel is one of its industrial centres.

## The Named Organisations Behind Veghel's Technology Market

**Vanderlande.** Headquartered in Veghel and part of **Toyota Industries** since 2017, Vanderlande designs and delivers automated material handling systems for airports, warehouses and parcel distribution centres. Baggage handling systems at airports, automated storage and picking in warehouses and sorting systems for parcels all involve complex software, controls and data. Vanderlande employs large numbers of engineers in software, controls, systems engineering and data science.

**Jumbo Supermarkten.** The supermarket chain has its head office in Veghel. Grocery retail at national scale involves demand forecasting, store replenishment, distribution centre operations, e-commerce and customer data.

**Mars.** Mars operates a large chocolate factory in Veghel, producing well-known confectionery brands for European markets. High-volume food production involves process control, quality assurance, maintenance and supply chain planning.

**Food and logistics companies.** Veghel's business parks host additional food producers, logistics service providers and suppliers, creating a local ecosystem of operational data work.

**Knowledge institutions nearby.** The **Jheronimus Academy of Data Science (JADS)** in Den Bosch, a collaboration between Tilburg University and TU/e, and **Eindhoven University of Technology (TU/e)** are both within reach and supply data scientists and engineers to the region.

## Material Handling: Where Software Moves Physical Objects

Material handling systems are among the most complex automated systems in daily use. A large airport's baggage system can include many kilometres of conveyors, thousands of motors and sensors and software that must route each bag correctly and on time. A modern distribution centre combines automated storage, robotic picking and sorting to process huge numbers of orders per day.

For data scientists and engineers, these systems offer rich problems. Simulation and digital twins help design systems before they are built. Machine learning helps robots pick items with widely varying shapes and packaging. Operational data helps predict when equipment needs maintenance and how to balance capacity during peaks such as holiday seasons or morning flight banks. Because systems are delivered to customers worldwide, solutions must work across very different operations — a strong test of whether a model generalises.

## Robotic Picking: A Hard Problem at Scale

Picking individual items from totes is one of the hardest problems in warehouse automation. Products vary in shape, size, weight, transparency and packaging. A robot that handles boxes well may fail on soft bags, shiny packaging or items lying close together. Computer vision models must detect graspable surfaces, and planning algorithms must choose how to pick without damaging products or dropping them.

The engineering target is not just accuracy but reliability over millions of picks, with speed comparable to human workers and graceful handling of failures. That combination of perception, robotics and operations engineering makes robotic picking one of the most technically demanding AI applications in logistics.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**EU Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230.** Applying from January 2027, the regulation covers machinery including software-based safety functions and AI-enabled behaviour, relevant for automated handling systems and robots working near people.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems that are safety components of machinery can be high-risk, and AI systems used in employment contexts — such as systems that allocate tasks or monitor worker performance — are listed among high-risk applications.

**Dutch Works Councils Act.** In companies with a works council, introducing systems that can monitor employees' behaviour or performance generally requires the council's consent. Warehouse and production analytics involving workers must therefore be designed and introduced carefully.

**GDPR.** Applies to employee data in operations and to customer data in retail and e-commerce.

**NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555.** Food production and distribution, as well as certain transport and logistics activities, fall within the scope of stricter cybersecurity obligations for qualifying organisations, increasing attention to secure operational technology.

**Food safety rules.** Food producers must comply with EU food hygiene and traceability requirements, affecting how production data is recorded and retained.

## A Concrete Scenario: Predicting Peak Load at an Airport

A baggage handling system at a busy airport experiences occasional congestion during morning peaks, causing bags to miss flights. A data science team builds a model to forecast baggage volumes per hour based on flight schedules, passenger numbers and historical patterns.

The forecast itself is reasonably accurate. The real value comes when it is linked to operations: the system uses forecasts to pre-position empty storage capacity, schedule maintenance outside peaks and alert operators when load is expected to exceed capacity. Unexpected events — delayed flights arriving together, a security incident, a sudden weather change — still require real-time adjustments, so the solution combines forecasting with live monitoring. The result is fewer mishandled bags, which matters for passengers and airlines alike.

## Digital Twins: Designing a System Before It Exists

A new airport baggage system or automated warehouse can take years to design and build, and mistakes discovered after installation are extremely expensive. That is why simulation and digital twins play a central role in material handling. Engineers model the entire system — conveyors, storage, robots, operators and incoming flows — and test how it performs under different scenarios: a busy summer morning, a failure in one sorter, an unusual mix of order sizes.

Data makes these models realistic. Historical flight schedules, order profiles from existing warehouses and equipment performance data calibrate the simulations. Machine learning can also speed up simulation studies by learning surrogate models that approximate expensive simulations, allowing engineers to explore many more design options.

After installation, a digital twin can continue to be useful. Connected to live operational data, it can be used to test changes before applying them to the real system, to train operators and to analyse incidents. For data scientists and simulation engineers, this lifecycle — from design to operation — offers varied work on the same system over many years.

The main difficulty is keeping models trustworthy. A simulation that is not validated against real operations gives false confidence. Teams therefore invest in comparing simulated and actual performance and updating models when systems or operations change.

## A Common Misconception About Logistics Data Work

Some candidates see logistics as a less exciting domain than consumer technology or research. In practice, logistics automation combines robotics, computer vision, simulation, optimisation and large-scale software engineering, applied to systems that must work continuously at high reliability. The impact is tangible: every improvement is visible in throughput, delays or energy consumption. For engineers who like problems with physical consequences, logistics is one of the most interesting fields available.

## The Role of Suppliers and Engineering Service Companies

Large companies in Veghel work with numerous suppliers and engineering service firms, both for designing systems and for installing and servicing them worldwide. Many software, controls and data engineers work on projects through such firms, sometimes on site at customer locations abroad during commissioning. For candidates, this creates two routes into the sector: joining one of the large companies directly, or starting at a service company that places engineers on projects. The second route can offer faster exposure to different systems; the first offers more continuity and deeper involvement in product development.

## Questions Worth Asking Before Joining

- **Does the role focus on product development or on customer projects?** Product roles build platforms; project roles adapt them to specific customers, often with travel.
- **How much travel is involved during installation and commissioning?** Material handling projects can require time at customer sites.
- **How are models and algorithms validated before deployment in live operations?** Reliability expectations are high.
- **For retail roles: how do forecasts translate into orders and deliveries?** The link to operations shows the maturity of the team.
- **For production roles: how reliable and accessible is machine data today?** Many projects start by building the data foundation.

## Commuting and Living Near Veghel

Veghel has no railway station, so most commuters travel by car via the A50 or by bus. Den Bosch, Eindhoven, Uden and Oss are all within a reasonable drive, and many employees live in surrounding towns and villages in Meierijstad and beyond. Housing is more affordable than in the Randstad, and the location between several Brabant cities means professionals can often switch employers without moving house.
