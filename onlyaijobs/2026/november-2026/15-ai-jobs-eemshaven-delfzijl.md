On the Groningen coast, where the Eems estuary meets the Wadden Sea, two ports tell the story of how the Netherlands' energy and digital economy is changing. Eemshaven, a relatively young port, has become a landing point for power cables from Norway and Denmark and for offshore wind, and it hosts one of the largest data centre campuses in Europe. A few kilometres south-east, Delfzijl's chemical park produces salt-based chemicals, high-performance fibres and other materials while working on greener production. For data professionals, this remote-looking corner of the country offers work in some of the most critical infrastructure in Europe — and demand that is largely invisible to anyone searching from the Randstad.

## Why the Groningen Coast Became Critical Infrastructure

The province of Groningen was for decades synonymous with natural gas. Production from the Groningen field caused earthquakes that damaged homes and led to a long political and social crisis; production ended and the field was formally closed. The region's energy role did not end with it. Its location on the North Sea coast, access to high-voltage grid connections and available land made Eemshaven attractive for electricity interconnectors, power plants, offshore wind connections and energy-intensive users such as data centres.

At the same time, the chemical industry in Delfzijl, historically built on local salt and natural gas, has been transforming towards production based on renewable energy, recycling and bio-based raw materials. Together, the two ports are managed by **Groningen Seaports**.

## The Named Organisations Behind the Region's Technology Market

**Google's Eemshaven data centre.** Google opened a large data centre campus in Eemshaven in the mid-2010s and has expanded it since. Hyperscale data centres require specialists in electrical and mechanical engineering, operations, networking and increasingly data-driven optimisation of energy and cooling.

**TenneT and interconnectors.** TenneT, the national electricity transmission system operator, operates high-voltage infrastructure in the region. The **NorNed** interconnector between Norway and the Netherlands lands at Eemshaven, as does the COBRAcable connection with Denmark, making the area a hub for cross-border electricity flows.

**Power generation and offshore wind.** Eemshaven hosts power plants and grid connections for offshore wind farms in the North Sea, making it a key node in balancing supply and demand in the northern grid.

**Chemie Park Delfzijl.** The chemical park hosts companies such as **Nouryon**, which produces salt and chlor-alkali products, and **Teijin Aramid**, which produces aramid fibres used in protective equipment, cables and composites. Other companies produce materials such as silicon carbide and methanol-related products.

**Chemport Europe.** This cooperation of companies, knowledge institutions and governments in the northern Netherlands aims to develop a green chemical industry, linking the chemical parks with research and innovation.

**Knowledge institutions and energy research.** The **University of Groningen** and **Hanze University of Applied Sciences** in Groningen conduct energy and chemistry research, including through Hanze's energy transition centre of expertise, EnTranCe.

**Recovery organisations.** The **Institute for Mining Damage Groningen (IMG)** handles damage claims caused by gas extraction, while national programmes manage the structural reinforcement of buildings. Handling large numbers of claims and building assessments creates substantial data and process work in the city of Groningen.

## Data Centres: The Energy Question Behind AI

The growth of AI has made data centres central to energy policy. Large data centres consume significant amounts of electricity, and their arrival in the Netherlands has been politically debated, particularly in relation to grid capacity and the use of renewable energy. For engineers working at or with data centres, this creates concrete optimisation problems: reducing cooling energy, shifting flexible workloads to times with abundant renewable supply, predicting capacity needs and making use of residual heat where possible.

European regulation has added transparency requirements. Under the recast **Energy Efficiency Directive** and its implementing rules, operators of larger data centres must report energy performance and sustainability indicators to a European database. That turns data centre efficiency into a measured, reported and compared metric — and increases demand for people who can collect and analyse the underlying data reliably.

## Chemical Industry in Transition

Chemical production is energy intensive, and many processes still depend on fossil energy or raw materials. Transitioning to renewable electricity, green hydrogen, recycled or bio-based feedstocks changes how plants operate. Electrified processes may need to respond to fluctuating electricity prices and availability. New feedstocks can vary more in quality than the traditional ones.

Data-driven process control helps manage this variability. Soft sensors estimate quality parameters that are hard to measure in real time, optimisation models balance energy costs against production targets and predictive maintenance reduces unplanned stops in continuous processes. Engineers who understand chemical processes and can apply data methods are among the hardest profiles to find in the industry.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**Critical Entities Resilience Directive (EU) 2022/2557.** Requires member states to identify critical entities in sectors such as energy, transport and digital infrastructure and to ensure their resilience against physical and other threats.

**NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555.** Energy, digital infrastructure including data centre services, chemicals and ports fall within the scope of stricter cybersecurity obligations. Operational technology security is a major concern.

**Seveso III Directive.** Chemical sites handling hazardous substances must meet strict safety requirements, affecting how process data, alarms and safety systems are managed.

**EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS).** Emission allowances and reporting create strong incentives for accurate emissions data and efficiency improvements.

**Energy Efficiency Directive.** Introduces reporting obligations for larger data centres, as described above.

**GDPR.** Applies to damage claims, reinforcement programmes and any processing of residents' data — which in Groningen involves sensitive situations for many households.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems used as safety components in the management of critical infrastructure, including electricity supply, can be classified as high-risk.

## The Groningen Recovery as a Data Challenge

The consequences of decades of gas extraction created one of the largest administrative and data challenges in the Netherlands. Tens of thousands of homes and buildings were assessed for damage and for structural safety, and many residents went through lengthy procedures. Public organisations responsible for damage claims and building reinforcement handle large volumes of cases, inspection reports, building data and correspondence.

For data professionals, this work is less visible than data centres or chemical plants, but it has a direct human dimension. Data integration between organisations, geospatial analysis linking buildings to earthquake data, document processing and process analytics to identify bottlenecks can shorten procedures for residents. At the same time, the history of distrust between residents and institutions means that any use of algorithms must be transparent, explainable and carefully governed. Automated decision-making that affects residents is subject to strict GDPR rules, and public organisations are expected to document algorithms that influence citizens.

People who work on these problems often describe it as meaningful work: the goal is not efficiency for its own sake but reducing uncertainty and waiting time for people whose homes were affected.

## A Concrete Scenario: Cooling a Data Hall on a Hot Day

A data centre operator wants to reduce cooling energy. Historical sensor data covers temperatures, fan speeds, pump speeds, outside weather and IT load. A model is trained to recommend setpoints that keep equipment within safe temperature limits while using less energy.

In simulations, the model saves significant energy. In practice, operators are cautious: a mistake could cause equipment to overheat. The team introduces the model in advisory mode first, with strict safety constraints and automatic fallback to standard control. Over months, operators gain confidence as recommendations prove safe, and more control is delegated. The approach — advise first, constrain carefully, earn trust, then automate — is typical for AI in critical infrastructure.

## A Common Misconception About Remote Industrial Regions

Many job seekers assume that remote industrial locations offer only operational jobs, with interesting data work concentrated in city offices. At critical infrastructure sites, the opposite is often true: some of the most important optimisation and reliability work must happen close to the physical assets, because operators, engineers and equipment are there. Hybrid arrangements are common for analysis work, but regular presence on site is often necessary to understand what the data means.

## Questions Worth Asking Before Joining

- **How much of the work requires presence on site?** Critical infrastructure often limits fully remote work.
- **How is operational technology data accessed securely?** OT security procedures shape daily workflows.
- **What is the decision process for moving from advisory models to automated control?** The answer reveals the organisation's safety culture.
- **For chemical companies: how is the energy transition affecting investment plans?** Transition projects create both opportunity and uncertainty.
- **For data centre roles: is the position with the operator or a contractor?** Many technical roles are filled through service companies.

## Skills and Tools That Stand Out

- **Control and optimisation.** Understanding of control systems and constrained optimisation for physical processes.
- **Energy systems knowledge.** Electricity markets, grid constraints and renewable generation variability.
- **Process engineering.** For chemical plants: thermodynamics, reaction engineering and process modelling.
- **Industrial data platforms.** Historians, SCADA systems, OPC UA and time series databases.
- **Operational technology security awareness.** Working safely with data from critical systems.
- **Safety mindset.** Designing models with fallbacks, constraints and human oversight.

## Commuting and Living on the Groningen Coast

Delfzijl has a railway connection to Groningen, while Eemshaven is mainly reached by car or bus. Many professionals working in the ports live in the city of Groningen, roughly half an hour to three quarters of an hour away depending on the location and mode of transport, or in towns and villages closer to the coast. Groningen offers a lively student city with a university, cultural life and more affordable housing than the Randstad. For graduates of the University of Groningen and Hanze who want to stay in the north, the ports represent a significant and growing employment base.

## Career Growth in Energy and Industrial Infrastructure

Energy transition and digital infrastructure are long-term trends, and experience gained on the Groningen coast is relevant nationally and internationally. Skills in grid analysis, data centre optimisation and chemical process control transfer to energy companies, industrial clusters in Rotterdam, Terneuzen and Sittard-Geleen, and technology suppliers. Because these sectors struggle to find people who combine physical engineering with data skills, professionals with this profile are likely to have strong career options for years.
