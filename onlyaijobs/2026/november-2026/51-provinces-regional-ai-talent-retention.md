OnlyAIJobs was founded on a simple observation from research into regional labour markets: many job seekers from North Brabant move to the Randstad not necessarily because they want to live there, but because they do not know which interesting companies exist in their own region. That observation applies far beyond Brabant. Across the Netherlands, provinces invest in innovation programmes, high-tech clusters, science parks and knowledge institutions — and then watch a share of the graduates they helped educate leave for Amsterdam, Utrecht or Rotterdam. For AI and data talent, which every region's economy increasingly needs, this is a costly policy gap. The good news is that part of the gap is about information, and information problems can be solved at relatively low cost.

## Why Provinces Care About AI and Data Talent

Dutch provinces have responsibilities and ambitions in regional economic development, spatial planning, mobility and innovation. AI and data talent connects to all of them.

**Economic competitiveness.** Manufacturing, agrifood, logistics, energy and healthcare — the sectors that dominate many regional economies — depend increasingly on data skills to automate, optimise and innovate.

**Energy transition and climate adaptation.** Grid congestion, water management and sustainable agriculture require data-driven planning in which provinces are closely involved.

**Return on investment in knowledge.** Provinces co-fund innovation ecosystems, campuses and applied research. When graduates leave, part of that investment benefits other regions.

**Liveability and demographics.** Regions facing ageing populations need young professionals to stay or return, supporting services, housing markets and community life.

## Understanding the Retention Problem

Graduates leave regions for many reasons: career opportunities, salaries, urban lifestyle, partners, housing and networks. Policy cannot change all of these. But several drivers are directly influenced by regional action.

| Driver | Why graduates leave | What regions can influence |
|---|---|---|
| Visibility of employers | Graduates are unaware of relevant employers nearby | Make local vacancies and companies visible |
| Early connections | Students have no contact with regional employers during studies | Internships, projects, company visits |
| Perceived career paths | Regional careers seem limited | Show career paths and clusters of employers |
| Housing | Starter housing is scarce | Housing policy, temporary housing for starters |
| Mobility | Employers are hard to reach without a car | Regional public transport and cycling connections |
| Networks | Professional communities concentrate in large cities | Support regional meetups and communities |
| Returnees | Former residents lack information about opportunities at home | Targeted information for people who have moved away |

## The Visibility Gap in AI and Data Vacancies

Many regional employers with AI and data needs are small and medium-sized companies without large recruitment budgets. On generalist job boards, their vacancies compete with large companies that pay for promotion. Their job titles often do not use AI vocabulary — "process engineer data", "planning analyst", "quality specialist" — so graduates searching for "data scientist" or "AI" do not find them. And city-based searches miss employers located in smaller municipalities, business parks or rural areas.

The result is a structural information asymmetry: jobs exist, but talented people nearby cannot see them. Making vacancies visible at exact location, sorted by distance from home and categorised by type of work rather than job title, directly addresses this asymmetry.

## The Regional Ecosystem Provinces Can Mobilise

**Regional development agencies (ROMs).** Organisations such as the **Brabant Development Agency (BOM)**, **Oost NL**, the **Northern Netherlands Development Agency (NOM)**, **InnovationQuarter** in South Holland and their counterparts in other regions support innovation, investment and business development, and have direct relationships with growing companies.

**Cluster organisations.** Organisations such as **Brainport Development** and sector clusters in agrifood, logistics, energy and health bring employers together around shared challenges, including talent.

**Knowledge institutions.** Universities, universities of applied sciences and vocational institutions educate talent and run projects with companies.

**Municipalities and labour market regions.** Municipalities, employer service points and regional labour market partnerships work on skills and matching.

**Interprovincial cooperation.** The **Interprovincial Consultation (IPO)** allows provinces to share approaches and coordinate on common themes.

**Funding instruments.** Regional economic and labour market programmes can be supported through national instruments such as **Regio Deals** and European programmes such as the **European Regional Development Fund (ERDF)**, managed through regional programmes.

## Policy Levers That Improve Retention

**1. Make regional AI and data employment visible.** Support platforms and campaigns that show local vacancies and employers, including in smaller municipalities. Encourage regional employers — especially SMEs — to publish vacancies where specialist talent searches.

**2. Connect students to regional employers early.** Fund or facilitate internships, graduation projects and company challenges with regional employers, particularly SMEs that lack capacity to organise them.

**3. Show regional career paths.** Communicate concrete stories of professionals who built data careers in the region, and map clusters of employers that allow career moves without relocating.

**4. Address practical barriers.** Include starters' housing and public transport access to business parks in talent strategies.

**5. Target returnees.** Reach former residents who moved to the Randstad with information about regional opportunities when they reconsider their situation, for example when starting a family.

**6. Support regional professional communities.** Meetups, data communities and events help professionals feel connected without moving to large cities.

**7. Measure and learn.** Use data on graduate destinations, vacancy trends and employer needs to evaluate interventions.

## Measuring What Matters

Provinces often lack timely data on AI and data labour markets. Official statistics from **Statistics Netherlands (CBS)** provide essential structural information about employment, education and migration, but they are published with delay and rarely capture specific emerging roles. Vacancy data from specialist platforms can complement official statistics by showing where AI and data vacancies appear, which sectors are hiring and how demand changes over time.

Useful indicators include the number and location of AI and data vacancies, the share of graduates from regional institutions working in the region after graduation, internship placements with regional employers, and employer-reported recruitment difficulties.

## A Concrete Scenario: A Province Tests a Visibility Pilot

A province with a strong manufacturing and agrifood base notices in employer surveys that companies struggle to hire data professionals, while regional universities of applied sciences report that many ICT and data graduates move to the Randstad.

Together with its regional development agency and two universities of applied sciences, the province runs a one-year pilot. Employers in the region are encouraged to publish AI and data vacancies on a specialist platform showing jobs at exact location. The development agency includes the pilot in its company contacts, universities promote it to students and alumni, and the province funds a series of company visits and a regional data meetup. At the end of the year, the partners evaluate the number of vacancies published, internships arranged and graduates hired regionally. The pilot is modest in cost, and the lessons feed into the province's next economic programme.

## Labour Markets Do Not Stop at Provincial Borders

Graduates and professionals choose jobs based on travel time, not administrative boundaries. A data engineer living in Nijmegen may work in Oss or Arnhem; a professional in Maastricht may consider Heerlen, Sittard-Geleen or Aachen; someone in Zwolle may commute to Lelystad or Apeldoorn. Talent strategies that promote only employers within one province miss part of the realistic labour market — and may compete unnecessarily with neighbouring regions.

Provinces can benefit from cooperation: sharing information about vacancies within commuting distance across borders, coordinating events and supporting mobility connections between neighbouring cities. In border regions, cooperation with Belgian and German partners through Euregio structures adds a cross-border dimension.

## The Role of Employers Themselves

Policy can create conditions, but employers determine whether talent chooses to stay. Regional employers improve their attractiveness by publishing clear vacancies with salary ranges, offering internships and graduation projects, providing development opportunities and flexible working, and describing the concrete technical work involved. Provinces and development agencies can encourage these practices — for example by sharing examples of employers that successfully attract data talent, or by including recruitment practices in business support programmes.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Building a new regional job portal from scratch,** which is expensive and struggles to attract users.
- **Campaigns without concrete vacancies,** which create awareness but no action.
- **Focusing only on large companies,** while SMEs represent much of the hidden demand.
- **Ignoring practical barriers** such as housing and mobility.
- **One-off initiatives** without evaluation or continuity.
- **Competing between neighbouring regions** instead of recognising that labour markets cross administrative borders.

## Housing and Mobility as Talent Policy

Talent strategies often focus on jobs and education, while the decisive barriers for young professionals lie elsewhere. A graduate offered a data role at a manufacturer in a smaller town may decline if no rental housing is available or if the business park cannot be reached without a car. Provinces have direct influence on regional public transport and spatial planning, and they cooperate with municipalities on housing programmes.

Including talent considerations in these policies can make a significant difference: bus connections to business parks and campuses aligned with working hours, safe cycling routes between stations and employers, and housing programmes that include starter homes and temporary housing for young professionals. Employers can contribute too, for example through mobility budgets or cooperation with housing providers. When talent policy, mobility policy and housing policy are designed together, each becomes more effective.

## Using Vacancy Data in Provincial Monitoring

Provinces regularly publish economic monitors and labour market reports. Adding indicators about AI and data vacancies — where they appear, in which sectors and at which employers — provides a more current picture than annual statistics alone. Location-based vacancy data can reveal clusters of demand outside city centres, such as business parks or campuses, that deserve attention in mobility or housing planning. Tracking these indicators over time also shows whether interventions such as internship programmes or visibility campaigns coincide with changes in regional hiring.

## What This Is Not

Improving visibility of regional vacancies is not a substitute for broader economic, housing and education policy. It will not keep every graduate in the region, nor should it: mobility is valuable for individuals and the economy. The goal is that graduates make informed choices — and that those who would prefer to stay are not pushed away by a lack of information.

## Key Takeaways

- Many graduates leave their region partly because they do not know relevant employers nearby — an information problem regions can address.
- AI and data vacancies at regional SMEs are often invisible because of limited budgets, non-AI job titles and city-based searches.
- Provinces can mobilise regional development agencies, clusters, knowledge institutions, municipalities and funding instruments such as Regio Deals and ERDF programmes.
- Effective levers include visibility of local vacancies, early student-employer connections, career stories, housing and mobility, returnee outreach and professional communities.
- Complement CBS statistics with vacancy data and evaluate pilots to build evidence-based policy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: provincial policy adviser) Why should a province care about AI and data talent?
Regional sectors such as manufacturing, agrifood, logistics, energy and healthcare increasingly depend on data skills, and retaining graduates protects public investments in knowledge and innovation.

### (Scenario: policymaker unsure about causes) Why do graduates leave regions even when jobs exist?
Among other reasons, because they are unaware of relevant regional employers, whose vacancies are often less visible or use titles without AI terminology.

### (Scenario: province considering its own job portal) Should a province build its own regional job platform?
Usually not — building and maintaining a portal is costly; cooperating with existing specialist platforms and partners is often more effective.

### (Scenario: regional development agency) What role can regional development agencies play?
They can encourage companies to publish vacancies, connect employers with knowledge institutions and include talent visibility in business support.

### (Scenario: province wanting evidence) How can a province measure progress on talent retention?
Combine CBS statistics with indicators such as regional vacancy data, internship placements and graduate destinations from regional institutions.
