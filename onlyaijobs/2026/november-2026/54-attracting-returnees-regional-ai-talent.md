A software engineer grew up in Zeeland, studied in Delft and now works as an ML engineer in Amsterdam. A data analyst from Twente moved to Utrecht after graduating. A data scientist from Limburg has lived in Rotterdam for eight years. At some point, many of them start thinking about moving back: when they have children, when housing in the city becomes unaffordable, when parents need support or when hybrid working makes a long daily commute unnecessary. Yet when they look for jobs in their home region, they often find little — not because there is nothing, but because they do not know where to look. For regions trying to attract AI and data talent, these returnees are one of the most promising and least targeted groups.

## Why Returnees Are an Attractive Talent Group

**Existing connection to the region.** Returnees know the area, often have family and friends there and are more likely to stay long-term than newcomers without ties.

**Valuable experience.** Many have gained experience at larger companies, consultancies or technology firms in the Randstad, bringing skills that regional employers struggle to find.

**Life-stage motivation.** Decisions to move back often coincide with life events such as starting a family, which also increases the likelihood of settling permanently.

**Networks.** Returnees bring professional networks from other regions, which can benefit regional employers and ecosystems.

**Regional impact.** They contribute to local communities, services and the housing market in ways that support regional liveability.

## Understanding the Returnee Decision

| Factor | Why it matters for returnees | What regions can influence |
|---|---|---|
| Job opportunities | Few want to move without a suitable job | Visibility of regional AI and data vacancies |
| Career prospects | Concern that regional careers are limited | Show career paths and employer clusters |
| Partner's career | Couples need two suitable jobs | Dual career support and visibility of broader vacancies |
| Housing | Availability and affordability of homes | Housing policy, information about options |
| Schools and childcare | Important for families | Information and quality of facilities |
| Hybrid working | Allows combining regional living with employers elsewhere | Coworking spaces, digital infrastructure |
| Social connection | Family, friends and community | Returnee networks and events |
| Salary expectations | Concern about lower regional salaries | Transparent salary information, cost-of-living comparison |

## The Information Gap for Returnees

Professionals living in the Randstad rarely follow the regional labour market of their home province. Their networks, recruiters and news sources focus on where they live now. When they search for regional jobs, generalist platforms often show vacancies by city name, missing employers in smaller towns, business parks and campuses. Many regional employers with AI and data needs do not use AI terminology in job titles or do not advertise widely.

As a result, returnees frequently conclude that "there is nothing for me there" — a conclusion based on incomplete information. Visible regional vacancies, shown at exact location and categorised by type of work, directly address this gap.

## Hybrid Working Changes the Picture

The broad adoption of hybrid working has made moving back more feasible. A professional may live in their home region, work partly remotely for an employer elsewhere and gradually switch to a regional employer. In the Netherlands, the **Flexible Working Act** allows employees to request changes in working location, although employers retain considerable discretion regarding working from home.

For regions, this offers opportunities and risks. Returnees working remotely for Randstad employers still contribute to local communities and may later join regional employers. At the same time, regions should ensure that attractive regional jobs are visible, so that returnees consider local employers when they are ready to change jobs.

## Salary Expectations and Transparency

A common concern among potential returnees is that regional salaries are lower. In some cases they are, but lower housing costs and shorter commutes often compensate. Salary transparency helps returnees compare realistically. Under the **EU Pay Transparency Directive**, employers must provide information on pay or pay ranges before interviews, making it easier for returnees to evaluate regional offers without lengthy processes.

## What Regions Can Do

**1. Make regional AI and data employment visible.** Encourage regional employers to publish vacancies where specialist talent searches, including location-based platforms that show jobs by distance.

**2. Reach former residents directly.** Use alumni networks of regional universities and universities of applied sciences, school alumni associations, regional media with national reach and social media campaigns targeting people who have moved away.

**3. Tell realistic returnee stories.** Share examples of AI and data professionals who moved back and built careers with regional employers, including practical details about housing, commuting and work-life balance.

**4. Support dual careers.** Help partners of returnees find suitable jobs by connecting them with regional employers and networks.

**5. Provide practical information.** Offer clear information about housing, schools, childcare, coworking spaces and transport connections in one accessible place.

**6. Organise returnee events.** Events during holiday periods, when many former residents visit family, can connect them with regional employers.

**7. Work with employers.** Encourage employers to highlight flexibility, development opportunities and regional quality of life in vacancies.

## Measuring Returnee Attraction

**Statistics Netherlands (CBS)** publishes data on internal migration, which can show movement patterns between regions and age groups. Regions can complement these statistics with indicators such as the number of returnees reached through campaigns, applications from people living outside the region, hires of former residents reported by employers and participation in returnee events.

## A Concrete Scenario: A Returnee Campaign Linked to Real Vacancies

A region with a strong manufacturing and agrifood sector wants to attract data professionals. Instead of a general image campaign, the regional marketing organisation and province work with employers and a regional university of applied sciences.

Employers publish AI and data vacancies on a specialist platform showing jobs at exact location. The campaign targets alumni who moved to the Randstad, featuring stories of three professionals who returned: a data engineer at a machine builder, an analyst at a food producer and an ML engineer at a logistics company. The campaign links directly to current vacancies and practical information about housing and schools. During the Christmas period, the region hosts an informal event for former residents with participating employers. Several returnees apply, and employers report hires of experienced professionals they would not otherwise have reached.

## Dual Careers in Practice

For many returnees, the decisive question is not whether they can find a job, but whether their partner can too. A data scientist may find an attractive regional role, while their partner — perhaps a teacher, nurse, lawyer or marketing professional — needs suitable work as well. If one partner's career would suffer, the move often does not happen.

Regions can support dual careers in practical ways: connecting partners with regional employer networks across sectors, sharing broader vacancy information beyond technology roles, offering introductions to recruitment contacts at larger regional employers and highlighting flexible and hybrid possibilities. Some employers include partner support in relocation packages for experienced hires. Even a single contact person who can point partners in the right direction can make a difference, because it shows that the region takes the whole household's situation seriously.

## The Role of Regional Media, Communities and Families

Returnees are reached not only through campaigns but through the channels they already follow. Many former residents still read regional news websites, follow local sports clubs or community pages on social media and hear about developments from family members. Regional media stories about technology employers and professionals who returned can reach former residents more authentically than advertising.

Families and friends are an underestimated channel. Parents often share news about jobs and developments in the home region with their children. Community events, school reunions and holiday gatherings create moments where former residents reconnect. Regional organisations can support these informal channels by producing shareable stories and making vacancy information easy to forward.

Professional communities also matter. A regional data meetup or AI network that welcomes visitors from outside the region gives returnees a way to explore the local professional scene before deciding to move — and to meet potential employers informally.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Image campaigns without concrete jobs,** which raise interest but offer no next step.
- **Targeting only young graduates,** while many returnees are mid-career professionals.
- **Ignoring partners' careers,** which often determine whether a family moves.
- **Overstating regional salaries or opportunities,** which damages trust.
- **One-off campaigns** without continuous visibility of vacancies.

## Different Returnee Profiles Need Different Messages

Returnees are not one group. Their motivations and concerns vary by life stage and career situation.

| Profile | Typical motivation | Main concerns | Effective message |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young professional after a few years in the city | Housing costs, friends and family | Career growth, social life | Concrete roles with growth, active regional communities |
| Professional starting a family | Space, schools, family support | Partner's career, childcare, salary | Dual career support, quality of life, practical information |
| Experienced specialist or manager | Work-life balance, leadership roles | Availability of senior roles | Leadership opportunities at regional employers |
| Remote worker | Lower living costs, proximity to family | Isolation, future job options | Coworking spaces, regional employers for future moves |
| Professional caring for parents | Family responsibilities | Flexibility, part-time options | Flexible and part-time roles, understanding employers |

Campaigns that address these differences — rather than using a single generic message — are more likely to resonate.

## Working With Employers on Returnee-Friendly Vacancies

Regional employers can make their vacancies more attractive to returnees with relatively small adjustments. Stating flexibility clearly helps professionals who want to combine a phased move with hybrid working. Mentioning part-time possibilities reaches parents and carers. Publishing salary ranges allows comparison with current jobs. Describing the concrete technical work shows that regional roles can be as challenging as those in the Randstad. And mentioning practical support, such as help with relocation or flexible start dates, lowers barriers.

Regional partners can encourage these practices by sharing examples of successful returnee hires and offering employers simple guidance on writing vacancies for experienced professionals considering a move.

## Timing Matters

Decisions to move back often develop over months or years. Many returnees start browsing regional jobs long before they are ready to move, often during specific moments: holidays spent with family, the arrival of a child, a change in housing situation or a reorganisation at their current employer. Regions that maintain continuous visibility of vacancies — rather than running short campaigns — are present when these moments occur. Seasonal peaks, such as the Christmas period or summer holidays, are good times for targeted events and outreach.

## What This Is Not

Returnee attraction is not about persuading people to move against their interests. It is about giving former residents accurate information about opportunities in their home region, so they can make informed choices when they consider moving back.

## Key Takeaways

- Returnees — professionals who moved away and consider returning — are a valuable, often overlooked talent group for regional AI and data employment.
- They bring experience, networks and a higher likelihood of staying, and their decisions often coincide with life events.
- The main barrier is an information gap: regional AI and data vacancies are often invisible to people living elsewhere.
- Hybrid working, salary transparency and practical information about housing, schools and dual careers make returning more feasible.
- Effective campaigns link returnee stories directly to real vacancies, reach alumni networks and are sustained over time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: regional marketing organisation) Why focus on returnees rather than new talent?
Returnees have existing ties to the region, valuable experience from elsewhere and a higher likelihood of staying long-term.

### (Scenario: policymaker) Why don't returnees find regional AI jobs?
Regional vacancies are often less visible to people living elsewhere, use titles without AI terminology or are located outside major cities.

### (Scenario: municipality) How does hybrid working affect returnee attraction?
It allows professionals to move back while keeping jobs elsewhere, making it important that attractive regional jobs are visible when they consider switching.

### (Scenario: province planning a campaign) What makes a returnee campaign effective?
Linking realistic returnee stories to current vacancies, reaching alumni networks, providing practical information and sustaining visibility over time.
