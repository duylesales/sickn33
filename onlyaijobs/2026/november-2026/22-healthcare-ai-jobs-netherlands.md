Few fields attract as many aspiring AI professionals as healthcare, and few are as misunderstood from the outside. Candidates imagine training models that detect disease in scans. That work exists, but it is a small part of a much larger picture. Dutch hospitals struggle with staff shortages and administrative burden, health insurers analyse care costs and quality, national organisations build infrastructure for sharing health data, and medtech companies bring certified AI products to market. For data professionals, healthcare offers meaningful work — but only for those who understand how the sector, its data and its rules actually work.

## Why Healthcare Needs Data Professionals Now

The Dutch healthcare system faces structural pressure. An ageing population increases demand for care, while the number of available healthcare workers does not grow at the same pace. National agreements between government, healthcare providers and insurers, such as the Integrated Care Agreement (Integraal Zorgakkoord), emphasise appropriate care, prevention, reducing administrative burden and better use of data and digital tools.

This shifts the focus of healthcare AI. The most urgent questions are often not about diagnostic accuracy but about capacity: predicting bed occupancy, planning operating rooms, reducing time spent on documentation, identifying patients who could be treated at home and making sure scarce specialists spend time where they add the most value.

## The Named Organisations in the Dutch Health Data Landscape

**University medical centres.** The Netherlands has seven university medical centres — Amsterdam UMC, Erasmus MC, LUMC, Maastricht UMC+, Radboudumc, UMCG and UMC Utrecht — represented jointly by the **Netherlands Federation of University Medical Centres (NFU)**. They combine patient care, education and research and host many AI research groups, including well-known medical image analysis groups.

**Health-RI.** The national initiative for health data infrastructure works on making health data findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable (FAIR) for research and innovation, and on preparing the Netherlands for the European Health Data Space.

**Nictiz.** The Dutch competence centre for electronic exchange of health information develops and maintains standards for data exchange in healthcare — essential for making data usable across organisations.

**Health and Youth Care Inspectorate (IGJ).** The inspectorate supervises the quality and safety of care and medical devices, including software used as a medical device.

**Health insurers.** Large insurers such as Zilveren Kruis (Achmea), CZ, VGZ and Menzis purchase care and analyse claims data at national scale.

**Electronic health record vendors.** Hospitals use electronic health record systems from both international and Dutch vendors, and the quality of data in these systems strongly affects what analytics and AI can achieve.

## Regulation: The Defining Feature of Healthcare Data Work

**GDPR.** Health data is a special category of personal data. Processing requires a specific legal basis, and secondary use for research or AI development requires careful assessment, often involving pseudonymisation, data protection impact assessments and governance procedures.

**Medical Treatment Contracts Act (WGBO).** Governs the relationship between patients and healthcare providers, including confidentiality and patient rights regarding medical records.

**Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).** Research that falls within its scope must be reviewed by an accredited medical research ethics committee. Whether an AI study requires such review depends on its design, and many retrospective data studies follow separate institutional procedures.

**EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR).** Software intended for medical purposes, including many AI tools that support diagnosis or treatment decisions, qualifies as a medical device and requires conformity assessment, clinical evaluation and post-market surveillance.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI systems that are medical devices requiring third-party conformity assessment are high-risk under the act, with requirements designed to align with the MDR.

**European Health Data Space Regulation (EU) 2025/327.** The EHDS regulation creates a framework for patients' access to their electronic health data and for secondary use of health data for research, innovation and policy, with obligations phasing in over several years.

**Electronic Data Exchange in Healthcare Act (Wegiz).** The Dutch law makes electronic data exchange mandatory for specific situations in healthcare, based on agreed standards.

**NEN 7510.** The Dutch standard for information security in healthcare is widely required for organisations handling patient data.

## A Concrete Scenario: Predicting Discharge to Free Up Beds

A general hospital struggles with full wards. Patients who are medically ready to go home sometimes stay longer because follow-up care, transport or home support is not arranged in time. A data team builds a model that predicts, a day or two in advance, which patients are likely to be ready for discharge.

The model's accuracy is decent, but its value depends on what happens next. The team works with nurses, discharge coordinators and elderly care organisations to integrate predictions into daily bed meetings. Privacy officers review data use, and the team documents clearly that the model supports planning rather than making medical decisions. After several months, the hospital sees earlier discharge planning for predicted patients. The project succeeds not because of an advanced algorithm but because the prediction is tied to an organisational process that can act on it.

## What a Week in Hospital Data Work Often Looks Like

A data scientist at a hospital rarely spends a full week modelling. A typical week might include a meeting with an intensive care physician about which patients deteriorate unexpectedly, several hours validating whether a data extraction from the electronic health record matches what clinicians register in practice, a session with the privacy officer about a new data request, an update to a dashboard used by operating room planners and time spent documenting an analysis so it can be reproduced. Somewhere in between, there is actual analysis.

This mix is not a sign of inefficiency; it reflects the reality that healthcare data is complex, sensitive and closely linked to clinical practice. People who enjoy variety and collaboration tend to thrive, while those who want long uninterrupted modelling time may prefer research groups or medtech companies.

## Secure Research Environments and Federated Analysis

Because health data is so sensitive, Dutch organisations increasingly analyse it in controlled environments rather than copying datasets to personal laptops or general cloud storage. Secure research environments provide access to pseudonymised data within a controlled workspace where outputs are checked before they leave. Some national and European initiatives go further with federated analysis: instead of bringing data together, analysis code is sent to the data at each hospital or institution, and only aggregated results are shared.

For data professionals, these environments change how work is done. Installing packages may require approval, computing resources may be limited and exporting results takes time. But they make analyses possible that would otherwise be blocked by privacy concerns — and experience with them is increasingly valuable as the European Health Data Space takes shape.

## Pay and Employment Conditions in Healthcare

Employment conditions in Dutch healthcare are largely set by collective labour agreements, such as the agreement for university medical centres and the agreement for general hospitals. Salary scales are published, which makes pay transparent. Salaries for senior data roles in hospitals can be lower than at banks or large technology companies, although conditions such as pension, leave and working hours are generally good. Health insurers and medtech companies often pay closer to commercial market rates. Many professionals choose healthcare for the meaning of the work, while knowing that compensation may differ from other sectors.

## A Common Misconception About Healthcare AI Careers

The most common misconception is that healthcare AI is primarily about diagnostic imaging models. Imaging AI is important and some of the strongest research groups in Europe are Dutch, but the majority of healthcare data jobs involve operational analytics, data engineering, quality measurement, interoperability and care purchasing. A second misconception is that clinical data is readily available: in practice, obtaining access, cleaning EHR data and aligning definitions across departments often take more time than modelling.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Health data understanding.** Knowledge of EHR data structures, clinical coding systems and how data is registered in practice.
- **Interoperability standards.** Familiarity with HL7 FHIR, SNOMED CT and other terminology and exchange standards.
- **Statistics and epidemiology.** Bias, confounding, missing data and appropriate evaluation for clinical questions.
- **Privacy engineering.** Pseudonymisation, secure research environments, federated analysis and data protection impact assessments.
- **Clinical collaboration.** Working with doctors, nurses and managers who have limited time and very specific questions.
- **Regulatory literacy.** Understanding when software becomes a medical device and what that implies.

## How to Move Into Healthcare From Another Sector

Data professionals from finance, retail or technology can transition successfully into healthcare, but they should expect a learning period. Useful steps include learning the basics of Dutch healthcare financing and organisation, understanding EHR data and clinical terminology, and reframing past experience in terms healthcare employers value: data quality, careful evaluation, privacy and working with non-technical stakeholders. Hospitals often value analysts who can make data understandable to clinicians more than those with the most advanced modelling skills.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **Which data can I actually access, and how long does access take?** The answer reveals data maturity.
- **Is the work research, operational analytics or product development?** Each has different rules, timelines and outputs.
- **How are clinicians involved in projects?** Clinical ownership is essential for adoption.
- **Could the tool qualify as a medical device?** This affects documentation and timelines.
- **What secure research or analytics environment is available?** Infrastructure strongly influences productivity.

## Key Takeaways

- Healthcare AI in the Netherlands is driven by capacity pressure, administrative burden and national agreements on appropriate care.
- Jobs exist at university medical centres, general hospitals, insurers, Health-RI, Nictiz, medtech companies, care organisations and public health institutes.
- GDPR, WGBO, WMO, MDR, the AI Act, the EHDS Regulation, Wegiz and NEN 7510 shape what data can be used and how.
- Most healthcare data work is operational, infrastructural or analytical rather than diagnostic model development.
- Health data knowledge, interoperability standards, epidemiology, privacy engineering and clinical collaboration are key skills.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate who wants to build diagnostic models) Is most healthcare AI work about medical imaging?
No — imaging AI is important, but most healthcare data work involves operational analytics, data infrastructure, quality measurement and care purchasing.

### (Scenario: data scientist from another sector) Can I move into healthcare data science without a medical background?
Yes, but expect to learn health data structures, clinical terminology and healthcare regulation; communication with clinicians is essential.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure about regulation) When does healthcare AI software become a medical device?
When it is intended for medical purposes such as diagnosis or treatment support; it then falls under the MDR and, often, the AI Act's high-risk rules.

### (Scenario: candidate curious about health data sharing) What is the European Health Data Space?
An EU regulation creating rules for patients' access to electronic health data and for secondary use of health data for research, innovation and policy.

### (Scenario: candidate choosing between employers) Which healthcare employers hire data professionals?
University medical centres, general hospitals, health insurers, health data organisations, medtech companies, care organisations and public health institutes.
