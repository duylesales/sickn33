In the Netherlands, PhD candidates are usually employees rather than students: they have employment contracts, salaries and pension accrual, typically for a four-year project. At the end of that contract, most face a choice. Academic positions are scarce and often temporary, while industry demand for people with deep expertise in machine learning, statistics and scientific computing remains strong. Many PhD graduates therefore build careers outside universities. Yet the move can feel daunting. Researchers worry that their experience does not translate, that industry work will be less intellectually interesting or that leaving academia is a kind of failure. None of these fears needs to be true.

## Why Industry Wants PhD Graduates — and Where It Hesitates

Industry employers value several qualities that PhD research develops:

- **Deep technical expertise** in a specific area, such as computer vision, NLP, optimisation or statistics.
- **Independent problem-solving** with open-ended questions and no known solution.
- **Rigour** in experimental design, evaluation and interpretation.
- **Persistence** through long projects with setbacks.
- **Communication** of complex results through papers and presentations.

At the same time, hiring managers sometimes hesitate. They may worry that a PhD graduate will pursue perfection instead of pragmatic results, lack software engineering practices, struggle with shorter timelines or become bored with applied problems. Successful candidates address these concerns explicitly rather than hoping they will not come up.

## Types of Industry Roles for PhD Graduates

| Role type | What it involves | Typical employers |
|---|---|---|
| Research scientist | Developing new methods, publishing, long-term research | Large technology companies, research labs, some scale-ups |
| Applied scientist or ML scientist | Adapting research to products, experimentation | Technology companies, e-commerce, platforms |
| Data scientist | Modelling, analysis and experimentation for business questions | Banks, insurers, retailers, energy companies, government |
| Machine learning engineer | Building and deploying models in production | Technology companies, scale-ups, consultancies |
| Research engineer at an institute | Applied research with industry partners | Applied research organisations such as TNO |
| Domain specialist | Combining research domain with data science | Pharmaceutical, medtech, semiconductor, energy and agrifood companies |
| Quantitative analyst | Mathematical modelling in finance | Banks, trading firms, insurers |
| Consultant | Advising on data and AI strategy and implementation | Consultancies and advisory firms |

Pure research scientist roles in industry are relatively few in the Netherlands compared with applied roles. Most PhD graduates find positions where research skills are applied to concrete products or decisions.

## Timing Your Move

**Start early.** Many PhD candidates wait until their thesis is almost complete before looking for jobs. Industry recruitment processes can take one to three months, and notice periods for new employers vary. Beginning orientation six to nine months before your contract ends gives time to explore options.

**Finish or not finish first?** Many employers accept candidates whose thesis is submitted but not yet defended. Starting a full-time job before submitting the thesis is risky: finishing a dissertation alongside a demanding job often takes much longer than expected. If you must start before completion, agree on realistic arrangements with your new employer.

**Contract end and income.** Because PhD candidates are employees, a gap between the end of the PhD contract and a new job may affect income and, in some cases, entitlement to unemployment benefits. Check your situation early.

## Special Points for International PhD Graduates

**Orientation year for highly educated persons.** Graduates who obtained a PhD in the Netherlands can apply for a residence permit for the orientation year (zoekjaar), which allows them to look for work or start a business without needing a work permit, within a limited period after completing their studies or research. Conditions are set by the **Immigration and Naturalisation Service (IND)**.

**Highly skilled migrant scheme.** After the orientation year, a lower salary threshold can apply for a highly skilled migrant permit, which makes it easier for employers to hire recent graduates. Check current thresholds and conditions with the IND.

**Timing permits and contracts.** Because residence permits depend on employment or orientation status, plan transitions carefully to avoid gaps.

## Translating Research Experience for Industry

**Rewrite your CV.** An academic CV lists publications, conferences and teaching. An industry CV should highlight problems solved, methods used, tools and impact. Replace "published five papers on graph neural networks" with a description of what you built, which data you worked with and what the results enabled.

**Emphasise transferable skills.** Handling large or messy datasets, writing research software, collaborating with other disciplines, supervising students and managing multi-year projects are all relevant.

**Show engineering practices.** Share code repositories, contributions to research software or evidence of working with version control, testing and reproducible workflows. This directly addresses concerns about software engineering skills.

**Explain your research in two minutes.** Practise describing your PhD for a non-specialist: the problem, why it matters, what you did and what you learned.

## Industry Interviews Differ From Academic Evaluations

Industry interviews often include coding exercises, machine learning design questions, case studies and behavioural questions. Unlike a thesis defence, interviews assess how you approach unfamiliar problems under time pressure and how you communicate trade-offs.

**Prepare for coding.** Even research-focused roles may include coding tests.

**Prepare for ML system design.** Expect questions about how you would build, evaluate and deploy a model for a business problem, including data collection, metrics and monitoring.

**Prepare for pragmatism.** Interviewers may ask when a simpler approach would be sufficient. Show that you can choose "good enough" when appropriate.

**Prepare questions about research culture.** Ask how much time is available for exploration, whether publishing is possible and how research results reach products.

## Intellectual Property and Publishing

Academic researchers are used to publishing results openly. In industry, publication policies vary. Some companies encourage publications and conference participation; others restrict publication to protect competitive advantage. Inventions may be subject to company patent processes, and under the **Dutch Patents Act 1995**, employers are entitled to inventions when the nature of the job involves making such inventions. If publishing matters to you, ask about publication policies and patent processes during interviews.

## Cultural Shifts to Expect

**Pace and scope.** Projects are shorter, priorities change more frequently and results must often be delivered in weeks rather than years.

**Collaboration.** Industry work typically involves product managers, engineers, business stakeholders and sometimes customers. Decisions are shared rather than made by an individual researcher.

**Definition of success.** Success is measured by impact on products, customers or decisions, not by novelty or publication.

**Identity.** Many former researchers go through a period of adjustment in professional identity. It helps to recognise that scientific thinking remains valuable — it is simply applied to different questions.

## Bridges Between Academia and Industry

Several routes make the transition smoother or keep connections alive:

- **Public-private partnerships and industrial PhDs**, where research is conducted with industry partners, often funded through programmes of the **Dutch Research Council (NWO)** and industry.
- **Applied research organisations** such as **TNO**, which combine research with applied projects for industry and government.
- **Part-time academic affiliations**, such as guest researcher positions, which some companies allow alongside employment.
- **Supervising students** through internships and thesis projects in industry.

These bridges show that the choice between academia and industry is not always binary.

## Salary Expectations When Moving From Academia

PhD salaries at Dutch universities follow published scales in the Collective Labour Agreement for Dutch Universities, and many PhD graduates are uncertain how to value their experience in industry negotiations. A PhD typically counts as relevant professional experience, especially when the research involved machine learning, statistics or software development closely related to the role. Employers differ in how they weigh it: some treat PhD years as fully equivalent to industry experience, while others place PhD graduates at a level between junior and medior.

Research salary information for your target sector and level, and be ready to explain concretely how your PhD experience maps to the responsibilities of the role. Do not undervalue yourself because the work happened in a university: independent project ownership over several years, supervision of students and complex technical work are substantial experience. As pay transparency rules come into effect, it will become easier to see how employers classify roles and experience.

## What to Do With Unfinished Research Ideas

Most researchers leave academia with ideas they did not have time to pursue. Leaving does not have to mean abandoning them. Options include publishing remaining results with former colleagues, releasing code and data as open-source resources, supervising a student project that continues the work or maintaining a guest affiliation with a university, if your employer allows it.

Check your new employment contract first. Intellectual property clauses, confidentiality obligations and policies on side activities may affect what you can continue to do, particularly if the research topic is related to your new employer's business. Discussing ongoing academic commitments openly during the hiring process usually leads to workable agreements.

## A Concrete Scenario: Translating a PhD in Medical Imaging

A PhD candidate specialising in segmentation of medical images applies to an energy company for a data scientist role focused on inspection images of grid assets. At first, she hesitates because the domain is completely different.

In her application, she describes her experience with limited labelled data, careful evaluation, domain shift between hospitals and collaboration with clinicians. In the interview, she explains how those lessons apply to inspection images from different camera types and field conditions. She also shares a well-documented repository from her PhD. The hiring team is impressed less by her publications than by her ability to transfer methods and her understanding of evaluation pitfalls. She receives the offer — and discovers that the transition to a new domain is faster than she expected.

## A Common Misconception About Leaving Academia

The most persistent misconception is that leaving academia means abandoning research. In practice, many industry roles involve research-like work: formulating hypotheses, designing experiments, analysing data and developing new methods. The differences lie in timelines, objectives and collaboration. A second misconception is that industry experience closes the door to academia. Some researchers return to academic positions later, bringing valuable applied experience, and the national **Recognition and Rewards** programme aims to value a broader range of career paths.

## Questions Worth Asking Industry Employers

- **How much time is available for exploration and learning?**
- **Can I publish or attend conferences, and what are the approval processes?**
- **How do research results move into products or decisions?**
- **Who will I work with daily — researchers, engineers, product teams?**
- **What does career progression look like for specialists who do not want to become managers?**

## Key Takeaways

- Most PhD graduates in the Netherlands build careers outside academia, and industry values their expertise, rigour and problem-solving.
- Roles range from research scientist and applied scientist to data scientist, ML engineer, research engineer, domain specialist, quant and consultant.
- Start orientation six to nine months before your contract ends, rewrite your CV for industry and prepare for coding and system design interviews.
- International graduates can use the orientation year and the highly skilled migrant scheme, with conditions set by the IND.
- Expect differences in pace, collaboration, success criteria and publication policies — while recognising that scientific thinking remains a major asset.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: PhD candidate nearing the end) When should I start looking for an industry job?
About six to nine months before your PhD contract ends, as recruitment processes and planning take time.

### (Scenario: PhD candidate worried about unfinished thesis) Can I start an industry job before defending my thesis?
Many employers accept candidates whose thesis is submitted; starting before submission is possible but often delays completion significantly.

### (Scenario: international PhD graduate) What residence options exist after a Dutch PhD?
The orientation year for highly educated persons and, afterwards, the highly skilled migrant scheme with a lower salary threshold may apply; check IND conditions.

### (Scenario: researcher worried about losing research work) Will I still do research in industry?
Often yes in a different form — hypotheses, experiments and new methods are common, with shorter timelines and product-focused goals.

### (Scenario: PhD graduate preparing for interviews) How are industry interviews different from academic evaluations?
They often include coding, ML system design, case studies and behavioural questions focused on pragmatic problem-solving and communication.
