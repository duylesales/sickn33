Recruitment has become one of the most common places where organisations use AI without calling it AI. Applicant tracking systems rank CVs. Sourcing tools recommend candidates. Chatbots answer questions and schedule interviews. Some platforms analyse video interviews or score assessments automatically. For companies hiring AI and data professionals, there is an additional irony: the candidates being assessed often understand these systems better than the recruiters using them. The **EU AI Act** classifies AI systems used for recruitment and selection as high-risk and prohibits certain practices outright. Dutch employers using AI in hiring need to understand what that means in practice.

This article provides general information, not legal advice.

## Which Recruitment AI Falls Under the AI Act

**High-risk systems (Annex III, point 4).** The AI Act lists as high-risk AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications and to evaluate candidates. It also covers AI systems intended to make decisions affecting terms of work relationships, promotion or termination, to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits, or to monitor and evaluate performance and behaviour.

**Prohibited practices (Article 5).** Since February 2025, certain AI practices are prohibited, including AI systems that infer emotions of a natural person in the workplace or in education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons. AI that analyses candidates' facial expressions or voice in interviews to infer emotions can therefore fall within a prohibition when used in a workplace context. Biometric categorisation to infer sensitive characteristics such as race, political opinions or sexual orientation is also prohibited.

**AI literacy (Article 4).** Since February 2025, providers and deployers must take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff dealing with AI systems — including recruiters using AI tools.

**Timeline for high-risk obligations.** Obligations for high-risk systems listed in Annex III were scheduled to apply from August 2026. EU institutions have discussed adjusting the timing of some high-risk obligations, so employers should check the current applicable dates. Preparing now is sensible regardless, because GDPR and equal treatment rules already apply.

## Provider or Deployer: Which Role Do You Have?

Most employers buy recruitment tools from vendors. In that case, the vendor is usually the **provider**, responsible for conformity assessment, technical documentation, risk management, data governance and registration of high-risk systems. The employer is the **deployer**.

An employer can become a provider if it develops its own AI recruitment system, puts its name on a system or makes a substantial modification to a high-risk system. Companies with strong data teams sometimes build internal ranking models for applications — which can bring provider obligations.

## Deployer Obligations Under Article 26

Deployers of high-risk AI systems must, among other things:

- **Use systems in accordance with the provider's instructions for use.**
- **Assign human oversight** to people who have the necessary competence, training and authority.
- **Ensure input data is relevant and sufficiently representative** to the extent the deployer controls it.
- **Monitor the system's operation** and inform the provider and authorities of serious incidents or risks.
- **Keep automatically generated logs** under their control for an appropriate period of at least six months, unless other law provides otherwise.
- **Inform workers' representatives and affected workers** before putting a high-risk AI system into service at the workplace.
- **Inform natural persons** that they are subject to the use of a high-risk AI system when it makes or assists decisions related to them.
- **Cooperate with competent authorities.**

Public bodies and certain private deployers must also carry out a fundamental rights impact assessment; for most private employers, the main instrument will be the data protection impact assessment under the GDPR, combined with the AI Act's deployer obligations.

## GDPR Already Applies

**Automated decision-making (Article 22).** Candidates have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that significantly affects them, subject to exceptions with safeguards. Automatically rejecting applications without meaningful human involvement can fall within this rule.

**Data protection impact assessment (Article 35).** Systematic evaluation of candidates using new technologies is likely to require a DPIA.

**Transparency.** Candidates must be informed about how their data is processed, including meaningful information about the logic of automated decision-making where applicable.

**Data minimisation and retention.** Collect only necessary data and delete application data within a reasonable period after the procedure, unless candidates consent to longer retention.

The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** supervises the GDPR and has a coordinating role in algorithm and AI supervision in the Netherlands.

## Works Council Involvement

Under **Article 27 of the Dutch Works Councils Act**, employers need the works council's consent for decisions on arrangements concerning the processing and protection of personal data of employees and on systems that can monitor employees' attendance, behaviour or performance. Whether recruitment tools fall under this depends on their scope, but AI tools that also evaluate internal candidates, allocate work or monitor performance are likely to require consent. Combined with the AI Act's obligation to inform workers' representatives, early involvement is wise.

## Equal Treatment and Bias

Dutch equal treatment law prohibits discrimination in recruitment on protected grounds. AI systems trained on historical hiring data can reproduce past patterns — for example, favouring candidates from certain universities, backgrounds or career paths. Employers remain responsible for discriminatory outcomes even if a vendor's system produced them. Regular monitoring of selection outcomes across groups, where lawful and feasible, and human review of automated recommendations help identify problems.

## Questions to Ask Recruitment Technology Vendors

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which features use AI to filter, rank or evaluate candidates? | Identifies which functions may be high-risk |
| Will you comply with AI Act provider obligations, including conformity assessment and registration? | Deployers rely on providers' compliance |
| What instructions for use and documentation will you provide? | Deployers must follow instructions and understand limitations |
| How was the system tested for bias and accuracy, and on which populations? | Shows data governance and risk management |
| Does any feature infer emotions or sensitive characteristics? | Such features can be prohibited |
| What logs are generated and how long can we retain them? | Supports log-keeping obligations |
| Can recruiters override and explain recommendations? | Enables meaningful human oversight |
| Where is data processed and stored? | Relevant for GDPR compliance |

## Writing a Candidate Notice About AI Use

Informing candidates does not need to be complicated, but it should be specific. A useful notice explains which parts of the process use AI, what the AI does, who makes decisions and how candidates can ask questions or request human review. Place it in the vacancy, the application form or the privacy notice linked from the application page.

An example: "We use software that helps our recruiters organise applications by matching the information in your CV with the requirements of the role. The software does not reject applications automatically: every application is reviewed by a recruiter before a decision is made. If you have questions about how your application was assessed or want to request human review, please contact us."

Avoid vague statements such as "we may use innovative technologies". Candidates for AI and data roles in particular are likely to appreciate clear explanations — and to be put off by vague ones.

## Human Oversight That Is Meaningful, Not Symbolic

The AI Act requires deployers to assign human oversight to people with the necessary competence, training and authority. In recruitment, oversight is easily reduced to a formality: a recruiter who clicks "approve" on the AI's ranking without understanding it, or who never looks beyond the top of a list.

Meaningful oversight involves several elements. Recruiters must understand what the tool does, its limitations and the risk of automation bias — the tendency to trust automated recommendations too readily. They must have time and authority to deviate from recommendations. Processes should require review of a sample of lower-ranked or rejected applications. And decisions to reject candidates should be based on criteria that a human can explain, not simply on a score.

Organisations can test whether oversight works by periodically checking how often recruiters deviate from AI recommendations and why. If deviations never occur, oversight may exist only on paper.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Ranking Tool Nobody Questioned

A technology company uses an applicant tracking system with an AI feature that ranks applicants for data engineering roles. Recruiters mostly review the top twenty candidates for each vacancy. After a year, the company notices that very few candidates with non-traditional backgrounds — such as career switchers or graduates from universities of applied sciences — reach interviews.

The company investigates with the vendor. The ranking model weighted certain keywords and education patterns heavily, reflecting historical hiring. Recruiters had not been trained on how the ranking worked and rarely looked beyond the top of the list. The company changes its process: recruiters review a broader set of applications, the ranking is used as one input rather than a filter, candidates are informed about the use of AI in the process, and the works council and data protection officer review the tool. Interview diversity improves, and the company is better prepared for AI Act obligations.

## A Common Misconception

Many employers believe that because a tool is bought from a reputable vendor, compliance is the vendor's responsibility. Under the AI Act, providers have extensive obligations, but deployers have their own — especially human oversight, informing candidates and workers, monitoring and log-keeping. Under the GDPR and equal treatment law, the employer remains responsible for how candidates are treated. Another misconception is that simple keyword filters are never AI. Whether a system is an AI system depends on how it works; many modern screening features use machine learning and fall within the definition.

## A Practical Roadmap for Employers

1. **Inventory** all recruitment tools and features, including those embedded in larger platforms.
2. **Classify** which features filter, rank or evaluate candidates and which might infer emotions or sensitive characteristics.
3. **Stop prohibited uses**, such as emotion inference in interviews.
4. **Engage vendors** with the questions above and obtain documentation.
5. **Conduct a DPIA** and involve the data protection officer.
6. **Inform and involve the works council** where relevant.
7. **Train recruiters** in AI literacy and meaningful human oversight.
8. **Update candidate information** to explain the use of AI.
9. **Monitor outcomes** and keep logs.

## Key Takeaways

- AI systems used to target job ads, filter applications or evaluate candidates are high-risk under the AI Act; emotion inference in the workplace is prohibited.
- Most employers are deployers with obligations for human oversight, input data, monitoring, logs and informing candidates and workers; building or substantially modifying systems can make you a provider.
- AI literacy obligations and prohibitions already apply; check the current timeline for high-risk obligations.
- GDPR rules on automated decisions, DPIAs and transparency, equal treatment law and works council consent rights apply now.
- Inventory tools, question vendors, train recruiters and monitor outcomes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer using an applicant tracking system) Is our applicant tracking system high-risk under the AI Act?
Features that use AI to analyse, filter or rank applications or evaluate candidates are high-risk; basic administrative functions are not.

### (Scenario: employer using video interview software) Can we use AI to analyse candidates' emotions in video interviews?
AI systems that infer emotions in the workplace are prohibited except for medical or safety reasons, which makes such features highly problematic.

### (Scenario: employer buying a tool from a vendor) Is the vendor responsible for AI Act compliance?
The vendor has provider obligations, but employers as deployers have their own obligations, including human oversight and informing candidates.

### (Scenario: HR manager) Do we need to inform candidates that we use AI?
Yes — under the AI Act, affected persons must be informed when high-risk AI assists decisions about them, and the GDPR requires transparency.

### (Scenario: employer with a works council) Does the works council need to be involved?
Workers' representatives must be informed before high-risk AI is used at the workplace, and consent may be required under the Works Councils Act.
