Look at the vacancies for data scientists and ML engineers in the Netherlands and a pattern emerges: a large share is posted not by banks, hospitals or manufacturers, but by consultancies, IT service providers and secondment firms. Their employees work at clients' offices or on client projects, sometimes for years. For many professionals — especially early in their careers — this is the default route into AI work. Yet the choice between a consultancy and an in-house role shapes learning, career progression, pay and daily satisfaction more than most job titles reveal. Understanding how each model works helps you choose deliberately rather than by accident.

## Three Models, Not Two

The Dutch market has three distinct models that are often lumped together.

**Project consultancy.** A consultancy delivers a defined project or advice for a client — for example, building a forecasting model, designing a data strategy or implementing a data platform. Consultants often work in teams from the consultancy, move between projects every few months and may work with several clients at once. Large firms such as **Accenture** and the Big Four, as well as specialised data and AI consultancies, work this way.

**Secondment (detachering).** A secondment firm employs a professional and places them at a client organisation, usually for an extended period, where they work largely as part of the client's team. This model is particularly common in the Netherlands and is used widely in IT, engineering and data roles. IT service providers such as **Cegeka Nederland** and many smaller firms offer secondment alongside project work.

**In-house.** The professional is employed directly by the organisation whose problems they work on: a retailer, bank, hospital, manufacturer or government body.

A fourth route, **interim or freelance work**, sits alongside these models. Firms such as **Data-First Interim** connect organisations with interim data professionals. This article focuses on employed roles, but many consultants later become interim professionals.

## How the Models Compare

| Aspect | Project consultancy | Secondment | In-house |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variety of problems | High — many clients and sectors | Medium — one client for months or years | Lower — one organisation, deeper context |
| Ownership of results | Limited — projects are handed over | Medium — part of a client team, but not formally | High — you live with your models |
| Learning curve | Steep, broad | Depends on the placement | Deep, domain-specific |
| Career structure | Clear consultancy ladder | Growth often through placements and training | Depends on organisation size |
| Stability of work location | Varies by project | Stable during placement | Stable |
| Pressure | Utilisation targets and deadlines | Balancing employer and client expectations | Organisational priorities |
| Salary development | Often fast early on, with lease car or mobility budget common | Varies; sometimes lower margins passed on | Varies strongly by sector |

## What Consultancy Offers Early in a Career

For graduates and early-career professionals, consultancy and secondment offer advantages that are hard to replicate in-house.

**Exposure.** In a few years, a consultant may work on retail forecasting, insurance claims, government data platforms and manufacturing analytics. This helps professionals discover which domains and types of work they enjoy.

**Structured training.** Consultancies invest in training, certifications and internal knowledge sharing because their people are their product.

**Network.** Working at many clients builds a professional network across organisations — valuable for later career moves.

**Speed of learning.** Joining new projects repeatedly develops the ability to understand an unfamiliar organisation, data landscape and set of stakeholders quickly.

## What Consultancy Costs You

The same model has real downsides.

**Limited ownership.** Consultants often build a model or platform and hand it over before seeing how it performs over time. Lessons from maintenance, drift, adoption and failure — the hardest parts of AI in practice — may be missed.

**Utilisation pressure.** Consultancies earn money when consultants are billable. Time between projects ("on the bench") may be used for training, but prolonged periods create pressure. Utilisation targets can also limit time for internal development.

**Dependence on client demand.** The projects available depend on what clients buy. A consultant interested in machine learning may spend long periods on reporting or data migration if that is what the market demands.

**Divided loyalty.** Seconded professionals work in a client team but are evaluated and paid by their employer. Conflicts can arise when client expectations and employer interests differ.

## What In-House Offers

In-house roles provide depth. You learn the domain, the data and the organisation thoroughly. You see a model through its entire lifecycle: from idea to production, monitoring, failure and improvement. You build long-term relationships with stakeholders and can influence strategy over years. For professionals who want to become experts in a specific domain — energy, healthcare, finance, logistics — or who want to lead data teams, in-house experience is usually essential.

The trade-offs are narrower exposure, dependence on one organisation's priorities and sometimes slower learning of new tools if the organisation's technology stack changes slowly.

## Legal Points Worth Understanding

**Placement of Personnel by Intermediaries Act (Waadi).** Secondment involves making workers available to another organisation. Dutch law includes rules for this, such as registration requirements for certain types of labour provision and the principle that workers made available must receive at least the terms of employment that apply to comparable workers at the client, for specified core terms. How this applies depends on the arrangement, so check your contract carefully.

**Non-compete clauses.** Under Article 7:653 of the Dutch Civil Code, a non-compete clause in a fixed-term contract is only valid if the employer justifies in writing that it is necessary due to substantial business interests. Courts can limit clauses that unreasonably restrict an employee.

**Client non-solicitation clauses (relatiebeding).** Consultancy and secondment contracts often prohibit working directly for a client for a period after leaving. Such clauses can significantly affect your options if a client wants to hire you in-house. Read these clauses before signing and ask what is negotiable.

**Balanced Labour Market Act (WAB).** Affects rules on successive fixed-term contracts, transition payments and on-call contracts, which matter for professionals starting with temporary contracts.

**EU Pay Transparency Directive.** As implementation progresses, employers must provide more information about pay ranges and pay structures, which will make it easier to compare offers between consultancies and in-house employers.

## AI Act Roles: Who Is the Provider?

The EU AI Act distinguishes between providers, who develop AI systems or have them developed and place them on the market under their own name, and deployers, who use AI systems. When a consultancy builds a high-risk AI system for a client, the question of who is the provider and who carries which obligations must be settled contractually and legally. Consultants working on high-risk applications should expect more documentation, clearer role definitions and more attention to compliance in project agreements.

## A Concrete Scenario: The Model Nobody Maintained

A consultancy team builds a churn prediction model for a subscription company in three months. The model performs well at launch, and the project ends successfully. The consultants move to their next assignment.

A year later, the company's customer base and pricing have changed. The model's predictions have drifted, but nobody monitored them. Marketing campaigns target the wrong customers, and trust in data science declines. The company hires an in-house data scientist, whose first task is to rebuild monitoring and retrain the model.

Neither party acted wrongly: the project scope did not include long-term maintenance. But the story illustrates the core difference between the models. Consultants learn to deliver; in-house professionals learn what happens after delivery. The strongest careers often combine both experiences.

## What Clients Expect From an External AI Professional

Organisations that hire consultants or seconded professionals usually expect more than technical skills. They expect someone who can start quickly, understand their context without extensive onboarding, communicate clearly with managers and deliver visible progress within weeks. They may also expect advice: which approach is realistic, which tools fit, and when an AI idea is not worth pursuing.

That last point matters. A good external professional sometimes tells a client that a machine learning project is premature because the data foundation is missing, or that a simple rule-based solution is sufficient. This can be uncomfortable when the consultancy's business depends on selling projects, but it builds long-term trust. Candidates considering consultancy should ask how the firm handles situations where the honest advice reduces the project scope.

## The Economics Behind Consultancy Pay

Consultancies and secondment firms earn revenue by charging clients for their professionals' time. From that revenue, they pay salaries, benefits, training, bench time, sales, management and profit margin. This explains several characteristics of consultancy employment. Utilisation matters because unbilled time costs the firm directly. Salaries can grow quickly early in a career because experienced consultants can be billed at higher rates. And the gap between what a client pays and what an employee earns is the reason some experienced consultants eventually move to interim or freelance work.

Understanding this model helps in negotiations. Training budgets, certification support, mobility arrangements and choice of assignments may be easier to negotiate than base salary, because they also increase your value to clients.

## A Common Misconception

Many professionals believe consultancy is a temporary stage before a "real" in-house job, or that in-house roles are dull compared with consultancy. Neither is true. Some professionals build excellent long-term careers in consultancy, becoming trusted advisers and specialists across sectors. Others thrive in-house, becoming domain experts and leaders. The better question is which model suits your current learning goals and personal preferences.

## How to Decide: Questions to Ask Yourself

- **Do I know which domain I want to work in?** If not, consultancy exposure can help you find out.
- **Do I want to see my models in production for years?** If yes, in-house experience matters.
- **How important is working at the same location with the same team?** Secondment and in-house roles offer more stability than project consultancy.
- **How do I respond to utilisation targets and client pressure?** Some people find it energising; others find it draining.
- **Do I want to lead teams or become a domain expert?** Both paths usually require substantial in-house experience at some point.

## Questions Worth Asking Employers

- **For consultancies: what share of projects involves machine learning versus reporting or migration?** The answer shows the real content of the work.
- **What is the utilisation target, and how is bench time used?** This affects daily pressure and development time.
- **Is there a client non-solicitation clause, and for how long?** Understand future restrictions before signing.
- **For secondment: how are placements chosen, and can I decline one?** Influence over placements matters for career direction.
- **For in-house roles: who maintains models after deployment?** Clear ownership shows maturity.

## Key Takeaways

- The Dutch AI job market includes project consultancy, secondment and in-house roles, alongside interim work — each with different learning, ownership and stability.
- Consultancy offers exposure, training and network; in-house roles offer depth, ownership and domain expertise.
- Secondment arrangements, non-compete and client non-solicitation clauses, the WAB and the Pay Transparency Directive affect contracts and options.
- The AI Act's provider and deployer roles make responsibilities in consultancy projects for high-risk AI more important.
- Many strong careers combine consultancy breadth with in-house depth; choose based on current learning goals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: graduate choosing a first job) Is consultancy a good first job in AI?
Often yes — it provides exposure to many sectors, structured training and a professional network, although ownership of results is more limited.

### (Scenario: candidate unfamiliar with Dutch secondment) What is detachering?
Secondment: you are employed by one firm and placed at a client organisation, often for months or years, where you work as part of the client's team.

### (Scenario: consultant considering a move) Can I join a client directly after working there as a consultant?
Possibly, but check your contract for a client non-solicitation clause, which may restrict this for a certain period.

### (Scenario: experienced consultant) Why do in-house employers value in-house experience?
Because it shows you have maintained models over time, dealt with drift and adoption and understood a domain in depth.

### (Scenario: candidate comparing offers) How do I compare a consultancy offer with an in-house offer?
Compare not only salary but also utilisation targets, lease car or mobility budget, training, contract clauses and the type of work you will actually do.
