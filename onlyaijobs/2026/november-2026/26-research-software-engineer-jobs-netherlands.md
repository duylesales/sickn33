Behind almost every modern scientific result is software: code that processes telescope data, simulates climate, analyses genomes, trains models on medical images or cleans survey responses. For decades, much of that software was written by PhD candidates and postdocs who learned programming along the way and moved on after a few years, leaving code that nobody else could maintain. The research software engineer — a professional who combines software engineering skills with an understanding of research — emerged to solve that problem. In the Netherlands, the role has grown from a niche into a recognised career path, and it is one of the most interesting options for data and ML engineers who want to stay close to science without pursuing an academic career.

## What a Research Software Engineer Actually Does

A research software engineer (RSE) develops, improves and maintains software used in research. The work varies widely:

- Turning a researcher's prototype script into a tested, documented package that others can use.
- Building data pipelines for large or complex datasets, such as imaging, sequencing or sensor data.
- Scaling analyses to run on high-performance computing or cloud infrastructure.
- Implementing and training machine learning models for scientific questions.
- Designing reproducible workflows so results can be verified years later.
- Supporting research groups with software architecture, code review and best practices.
- Contributing to open-source scientific software used internationally.

Unlike a typical industry software engineer, an RSE works on problems where requirements evolve with scientific understanding, users are often experts in another field and correctness may be harder to define than in commercial software.

## The Named Organisations in the Dutch RSE Landscape

**Netherlands eScience Center.** Based in Amsterdam, the national centre for research software was established by the Dutch Research Council (NWO) and SURF. It employs research software engineers who collaborate with researchers across disciplines, funds projects and promotes sustainable research software. The centre also maintains the **Research Software Directory**, which makes research software findable and citable.

**NL-RSE.** The Dutch community of research software engineers connects RSEs across universities, research institutes and other organisations, organises meetings and advocates for recognition of the profession.

**SURF.** The collaborative ICT organisation for Dutch education and research provides computing infrastructure, including national supercomputing and data services, and supports researchers and RSEs in using them.

**Dutch Research Council (NWO).** The national research funder increasingly emphasises open science and sustainable research software, including expectations around software management in funded projects.

**Digital Competence Centres.** Dutch universities have established digital competence centres or similar support units that help researchers with data management, software and computing. These centres employ data stewards, research software engineers and research data specialists.

**Universities and research institutes.** Universities, university medical centres and institutes employ RSEs directly within research groups or in central support teams. Research institutes in fields such as astronomy, physics, climate, life sciences and social sciences also hire scientific programmers and software engineers.

## Why the Role Matters: Reproducibility and FAIR Software

Science depends on results being verifiable. When analyses rely on software that is undocumented, untested or no longer runnable, reproducibility suffers. The **FAIR principles** — findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable — were originally formulated for research data and have been adapted to research software. Funders, journals and institutions increasingly expect code to be shared, versioned, documented and citable.

RSEs make this possible in practice. They introduce version control, automated testing, packaging, documentation, containerisation and workflow management. For machine learning research, they help track datasets, model versions and experiments so that published results can be reproduced.

## Recognition of RSEs in Academic Careers

Traditionally, academic careers were evaluated mainly on publications and grants, leaving little recognition for people who build software. Dutch universities, university medical centres, research funders and knowledge institutions have worked on a national programme on **Recognition and Rewards**, which aims to value a broader range of contributions, including software, data and team science. At the same time, universities have introduced or clarified job profiles for research support and research software roles within the job classification system used under the **Collective Labour Agreement for Dutch Universities**.

Progress varies by institution, and RSEs still sometimes face uncertainty about career progression or temporary contracts linked to project funding. But the direction is towards more permanent positions and clearer career paths, particularly in central support teams and national centres.

## A Concrete Scenario: From Thesis Code to Community Tool

A PhD candidate developed a machine learning method for analysing microscopy images, and several other labs want to use it. The code works on the candidate's laptop but depends on hard-coded file paths, undocumented preprocessing steps and specific library versions. The candidate is about to finish.

An RSE works with the candidate for a few months. Together they restructure the code into a package, add tests using small example datasets, document installation and usage, set up continuous integration and publish the software with a citable identifier. They also create a containerised version that runs on the university's computing cluster. A year later, the tool is used by labs in several countries, and the original method has been cited in new research — something that would have been unlikely if the code had remained on a laptop.

## Research Software Engineering for Machine Learning Research

Machine learning has become a research tool in almost every discipline, from astronomy to history. That has created a specific type of RSE work. Researchers often have strong domain questions but limited experience with training models at scale, managing GPU resources or evaluating models rigorously. RSEs help design training pipelines, set up experiment tracking, make models run efficiently on national computing infrastructure and ensure that evaluation avoids common pitfalls such as data leakage between training and test sets.

Reproducibility is particularly challenging in machine learning research. Results can depend on random seeds, library versions, hardware and small preprocessing choices. RSEs introduce practices such as fixed environments, versioned datasets, logged hyperparameters and shared model checkpoints, so that others can verify or build on results. For ML engineers from industry, this is familiar MLOps thinking applied to a research context — and a natural transition into the RSE career.

## Working With Sensitive Research Data

Not all research data can be shared openly. Medical records, interview transcripts, social media data about individuals and data from commercial partners come with legal and ethical restrictions. RSEs working with such data must understand the GDPR, institutional data protection policies and requirements from ethics committees and data providers.

In practice, this means building software that runs inside secure research environments, implementing pseudonymisation, designing workflows where only aggregated results leave a protected environment and documenting data handling for data management plans. Balancing openness — sharing code and methods — with protection of sensitive data is a core skill. The phrase often used in open science captures it well: data should be "as open as possible, as closed as necessary".

## A Common Misconception About RSE Work

Some engineers think RSE roles are less technically demanding than industry software engineering. In reality, research software often involves complex algorithms, large datasets, parallel computing, numerical accuracy and evolving requirements. RSEs must also learn enough about each research domain to understand what the software needs to do. The difference is not in technical difficulty but in goals: success is measured by scientific progress, reproducibility and reuse rather than revenue or user growth.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Software engineering fundamentals.** Version control, testing, packaging, code review and documentation.
- **Scientific programming.** Python, R, Julia or C++; numerical libraries; performance optimisation.
- **Machine learning.** Frameworks such as PyTorch, experiment tracking and reproducible training.
- **High-performance and cloud computing.** Job schedulers, parallel computing, containers and workflow systems.
- **Research data management.** Metadata, data formats, FAIR practices and privacy for sensitive data.
- **Collaboration and teaching.** Working with researchers from different disciplines and helping them adopt better practices.
- **Curiosity about science.** Willingness to learn the basics of astronomy, biology, linguistics or whichever field you support.

## Employment Conditions and Pay

Most RSE positions at universities fall under the Collective Labour Agreement for Dutch Universities, with published salary scales, generous leave, pension through ABP and support for development. Salaries are generally lower than for senior engineers in commercial technology companies, especially at higher experience levels. Many RSEs accept this trade-off in exchange for intellectual variety, flexibility, meaningful work and an international academic environment. Positions at national centres and institutes may have their own conditions.

## How to Move Into an RSE Role

Candidates arrive in RSE roles from several directions: PhD graduates who enjoyed the software side of research, industry software or data engineers seeking more meaningful work and scientific programmers who want better recognition. Useful preparation includes contributing to open-source scientific software, demonstrating reproducible workflows in a portfolio project, learning basic research data management and becoming familiar with high-performance computing environments. In applications, emphasise collaboration with non-engineers and examples of making code usable by others.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **Is the position permanent or linked to project funding?** Contract type strongly affects career stability.
- **Will I work in one research group or support many?** The breadth of work differs significantly.
- **How is RSE work recognised in evaluations and promotions?** This reveals institutional commitment to the role.
- **What computing infrastructure and tooling are available?** Access to national or cloud resources shapes what is possible.
- **How much time is available for maintaining existing software versus new projects?** Sustainable software requires maintenance time.
