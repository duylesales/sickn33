A manufacturing company with eighty employees has years of production data in its systems. A regional wholesaler wonders whether demand forecasting could reduce stock levels. A healthcare provider wants to understand why planning keeps failing. In each case, the owner or director concludes: "We need a data scientist." That conclusion is sometimes right and sometimes premature. The first data hire in a small or medium-sized enterprise (SME) is one of the most consequential and most frequently mishandled hires in the Dutch AI job market. Done well, it creates lasting capability. Done poorly, it produces an isolated specialist without data, support or clear goals, who leaves within a year.

## Why SMEs Matter for AI Employment

Most Dutch businesses are small and medium-sized, and SMEs employ a large share of the workforce. Statistics Netherlands data show that AI adoption among smaller companies lags behind larger companies but is growing. As SMEs digitalise, many will create their first data and AI roles in the coming years — often in regions and sectors that data professionals rarely associate with such work. These roles can offer broad responsibility and visible impact, but only when the organisation is prepared.

## Step 1: Assess Readiness Before Hiring

| Readiness question | Signs you are ready | Signs to prepare first |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a concrete business problem? | A specific question with measurable value, such as reducing stockouts | "We want to do something with AI" |
| Is data available and accessible? | Data in systems that can be exported or queried | Data scattered in spreadsheets, paper or inaccessible vendor systems |
| Is there an internal sponsor? | A manager who owns the problem and will use results | Interest only from the owner, without operational ownership |
| Is there time from domain experts? | Staff available to explain processes and data | Everyone too busy to support the new hire |
| Is there budget beyond salary? | Budget for tools, cloud resources and training | Salary only |
| Is there patience? | Expectation of results within six to twelve months | Expectation of transformation within weeks |

If several answers point to "prepare first", consider a smaller step before a permanent hire.

## Step 2: Choose the Right First Role

The title "data scientist" is often not the right first hire. The best choice depends on the current state of data and the problems to solve.

| Situation | Most suitable first role |
|---|---|
| Data is scattered and reporting is unreliable | Data analyst or analytics engineer to create reliable data and dashboards |
| Data is available but questions require prediction or optimisation | Data scientist with strong practical skills |
| Data must be collected from machines, sensors or multiple systems | Data engineer, possibly with support from an external partner |
| The main goal is exploring what is possible | External consultant, interim professional or student project first |
| AI tools are being introduced across the organisation | A data-savvy product or process role combined with training |

A common successful path is to hire a versatile data analyst or analytics engineer first, then add data science capability once the data foundation is reliable.

## Alternatives to a Permanent First Hire

**Student projects.** Universities of applied sciences and research universities often work with companies on student projects, internships and graduation assignments. These can explore feasibility at low cost, provided the company offers supervision and access to data.

**Interim or freelance professionals.** An experienced professional for a few months can assess data, deliver a first use case and help define the permanent role.

**Consultancies.** Useful for defined projects, although knowledge may leave with the consultant if no internal owner exists.

**SME digitalisation support.** Through the government-supported **SME Digitalisation Workshops (MKB-Werkplaatsen Digitalisering)**, SMEs can get practical help with digitalisation, often in cooperation with universities of applied sciences and regional partners.

## Funding and Support Options

**WBSO.** The Dutch R&D tax credit, administered by the **Netherlands Enterprise Agency (RVO)**, can reduce wage costs for employees working on qualifying research and development, which may include technically innovative software development. Whether data science work qualifies depends on the specific project; check conditions with RVO.

**SLIM scheme.** The SLIM subsidy supports SMEs in improving learning and development within their organisation, including training employees in new skills.

**Business associations and the KVK.** The **Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK)** and associations such as **MKB-Nederland** provide information and events on digitalisation and AI for SMEs.

**AI literacy obligation.** Since February 2025, **Article 4 of the EU AI Act** requires organisations using AI systems to take measures to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff — a reason to combine a first data hire with broader training.

## Step 3: Write a Vacancy That Attracts the Right Candidate

**Describe the problem, not just the tools.** Candidates want to know what they will work on: "forecasting demand for 3,000 products across two warehouses" is more attractive than "experience with Python and machine learning".

**Be honest about the data situation.** Stating that data needs to be organised will attract candidates who enjoy building foundations and deter those who expect clean datasets.

**Explain the support structure.** Mention who the hire will work with, whether there is an external mentor or partner and what budget exists for tools and training.

**Publish a salary range.** Under the **EU Pay Transparency Directive**, candidates must receive pay information before interviews. Clear ranges help SMEs compete by showing commitment.

**Highlight SME advantages.** Broad responsibility, direct contact with leadership, visible impact, short decision lines and often a shorter commute are genuine attractions.

## Step 4: Find Candidates Where They Look

Large generalist job boards often push SME vacancies below those of companies with bigger advertising budgets. Specialist platforms, regional networks, university career services and local meetups are often more effective for SMEs. Location matters too: many data professionals prefer working close to home, and an SME outside the Randstad can attract candidates who would otherwise commute long distances.

## Step 5: Assess Candidates Without Internal Expertise

The hardest part of a first data hire is assessing technical skills when nobody in the company has them.

- **Involve an external reviewer.** An experienced data professional from your network, a university lecturer or a consultant can review a technical assignment or join an interview.
- **Use a realistic, small assignment.** Ask candidates to analyse an anonymised sample of your data or a comparable public dataset and explain their approach.
- **Focus on communication and pragmatism.** The first data hire must explain results to non-technical colleagues and choose simple solutions when appropriate.
- **Ask about foundations.** Good candidates ask about data sources, quality and access before discussing models.
- **Check references about independence.** The first hire needs to work without a team of peers.

## Step 6: Set Up the First Six Months

| Period | Focus |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Understanding the business, meeting stakeholders, mapping data sources |
| Months 2–3 | Improving data access and quality, delivering first useful analyses |
| Months 3–4 | Selecting and scoping one use case with clear value |
| Months 4–6 | Delivering the first use case, measuring results, documenting work |

Provide a clear sponsor, regular check-ins with leadership, access to systems from day one and a budget for learning and external peer contact.

## Budgeting Realistically for the First Year

Many SMEs budget only for the salary of the first data hire and are surprised by other costs. A realistic first-year budget includes several elements.

**Employment costs.** On top of gross salary come holiday allowance, employer pension contributions, social security premiums and other employment costs, which together add substantially to the salary figure.

**Tools and infrastructure.** A data professional needs a suitable laptop, access to a database or data warehouse, analytics and visualisation tools and possibly cloud computing resources. Some costs are modest, but data platform subscriptions can add up.

**Data access.** Extracting data from existing software may require support from software vendors or IT partners, sometimes at additional cost.

**Learning and community.** Budget for training, conferences or meetups and possibly an external mentor, especially when the hire has no internal peers.

**Management time.** The sponsor and domain experts will spend time explaining processes, reviewing results and making decisions. That time is a real investment.

**Contingency for quick wins.** A small budget to implement the first improvements — for example, adjusting a planning process based on a forecast — ensures results actually change something.

Planning these costs upfront prevents a common situation in which a data professional is hired but cannot access the data or tools needed to do the job.

## Data Protection From the Start

A first data hire often gains access to customer, employee and financial data. Ensure that access is limited to what is necessary, data protection agreements with software vendors are in place and personal data is processed in accordance with the **GDPR**. The new hire can help improve data governance, but leadership remains responsible.

## Retaining Your First Data Hire

The first data professional in an SME is at particular risk of leaving. Without peers, they may feel isolated; without clear progression, they may see no future; without results being used, they may feel their work is pointless. Several measures reduce this risk.

**Create professional connection.** Budget for conferences, meetups and training, and consider an external mentor or peer group. Some regional networks connect data professionals from different SMEs.

**Make results visible.** Present outcomes to leadership and colleagues, and show how analyses change decisions. Recognition that data work matters is a strong motivator.

**Plan the next step.** Discuss how the role can grow: leading a small team, expanding into new use cases or becoming the organisation's data lead. Even if growth is gradual, a visible path helps.

**Protect focus.** First data hires are often flooded with ad hoc requests for reports. Agree on priorities so strategic work does not disappear under daily questions.

**Keep pay competitive and transparent.** As pay transparency rules come into effect, clarity about salary progression becomes even more important for retention.

## A Concrete Scenario: Two Companies, Two Outcomes

Two regional wholesalers each hire a data scientist.

The first posts a generic vacancy, hires a candidate with strong machine learning skills and asks for "AI to optimise the business". Data is spread across an old ERP system and spreadsheets, nobody has time to explain processes and the new hire spends months trying to access data. After ten months, the data scientist leaves, frustrated.

The second company first commissions a student project to map data and test a simple demand forecast. It then hires an analytics-oriented data professional, describes the forecasting problem in the vacancy, assigns the purchasing manager as sponsor and arranges monthly sessions with an external data mentor. Within six months, stock levels for a pilot product group decrease, dashboards are trusted and the company plans a second data role.

## A Common Misconception

The most common misconception is that hiring a skilled data scientist automatically creates value. In SMEs, value depends at least as much on data access, a clear problem, an engaged sponsor and realistic expectations. A second misconception is that SMEs cannot compete for data talent. Many data professionals are attracted by broad responsibility, visible impact and working close to home — advantages SMEs can offer.

## Key Takeaways

- Assess readiness before hiring: a concrete problem, accessible data, a sponsor, domain expert time, budget beyond salary and patience.
- The best first hire is often a versatile analyst or analytics engineer rather than a pure data scientist.
- Student projects, interim professionals, SME Digitalisation Workshops, WBSO and SLIM can support the first steps.
- Write vacancies around the problem, be honest about data, publish salary ranges and use specialist and regional channels.
- Involve an external reviewer in assessment and structure the first six months around foundations and one clear use case.
