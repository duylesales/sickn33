When a Dutch train timetable is built, when parcel routes are planned for the next morning, when nurses are rostered for a hospital ward or when a battery decides when to charge, the decisive technology is often not a neural network. It is mathematical optimisation: models that choose the best possible decision among millions of options, subject to hard constraints. Operations research (OR) is one of the oldest branches of applied AI and analytics, and the Netherlands has a particularly strong tradition in it. For people who enjoy mathematics, algorithms and problems with immediate practical consequences, OR offers a distinctive career path that rarely appears in searches for "AI jobs".

## What Operations Research Actually Is

Operations research uses mathematical models and algorithms to support decisions. Where machine learning predicts what will happen, optimisation decides what to do. Typical problem types include:

- **Scheduling:** assigning tasks, shifts or trains to time slots.
- **Routing:** determining efficient routes for vehicles or people.
- **Allocation:** distributing limited resources such as capacity, stock or staff.
- **Network design:** deciding where to locate warehouses, depots or charging stations.
- **Inventory control:** determining when and how much to order.
- **Pricing and revenue management:** setting prices and availability to maximise value.

Methods include linear and mixed-integer programming, constraint programming, heuristics and metaheuristics, stochastic optimisation, simulation and queueing theory.

## Why the Netherlands Has a Strong OR Tradition

**Railways.** **NS (Dutch Railways)** is internationally known for applying operations research to timetabling, rolling stock and crew planning; its work on the introduction of a new national timetable received international recognition from the operations research community. **ProRail**, the rail infrastructure manager, uses optimisation for capacity allocation and maintenance planning on one of Europe's busiest rail networks.

**Logistics.** As a logistics hub for Europe, the Netherlands has many companies in transport, warehousing and parcel delivery. **PostNL** plans daily parcel and mail logistics at national scale.

**Optimisation software.** **ORTEC**, a Dutch company founded in the early 1980s, develops optimisation software and analytics for routing, workforce scheduling and supply chains for clients worldwide.

**Academic strength.** Dutch universities have strong OR and econometrics programmes, including the Econometric Institute at **Erasmus School of Economics**, and operations research groups at **Eindhoven University of Technology**, Tilburg University, the University of Twente, VU Amsterdam, the University of Groningen and others. The **Dutch Network on the Mathematics of Operations Research (LNMB)** connects researchers and PhD education across universities.

## How OR Relates to Machine Learning

Optimisation and machine learning are increasingly combined. A forecast of demand, travel times or no-shows feeds into an optimisation model that decides on capacity or schedules. Machine learning can also speed up optimisation, for example by learning good heuristics or predicting which constraints are binding. Reinforcement learning has been explored for sequential decision problems, although classical optimisation often remains more reliable when constraints are strict.

For job seekers, the combination is valuable: data scientists who understand optimisation, and OR specialists who understand machine learning, are both in demand. Many vacancies titled "data scientist" in logistics or energy involve substantial optimisation work.

## Regulation Becomes Constraints

In optimisation, rules often appear directly in the model as constraints.

**Driving times and rest periods.** **Regulation (EC) No 561/2006** sets EU rules on driving times, breaks and rest periods for road transport drivers, which vehicle routing and scheduling models must respect.

**Working hours.** The Dutch Working Hours Act and collective labour agreements set limits on shifts, rest periods and night work for staff rostering.

**Rail capacity allocation.** Allocation of rail infrastructure capacity follows European and national rules, influencing how timetables are constructed.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Whether an optimisation system qualifies as an AI system under the AI Act depends on how it works; systems that infer outputs from input using techniques such as machine learning fall within scope. Systems used to allocate tasks to workers based on individual behaviour or to monitor performance can be high-risk in employment contexts.

**GDPR.** Workforce scheduling and routing systems often process personal data of employees, requiring careful design and, in companies with a works council, possibly consent for systems that monitor employees.

## A Concrete Scenario: Recovering From a Disruption

A signal failure blocks a busy rail corridor during the morning peak. Trains, rolling stock and crews are suddenly in the wrong places. Dispatchers must decide which trains to cancel, how to reroute others and how to reassign crews while respecting working hour rules and minimising passenger inconvenience.

An optimisation-based decision support tool proposes a recovery plan within minutes. The first versions produced mathematically optimal plans that dispatchers rejected, because they changed too much at once and were hard to communicate. The team adjusted the model to favour plans with fewer changes and clear explanations. The case illustrates a core truth of applied OR: the best mathematical solution is only useful if people can understand, trust and execute it under time pressure.

## Exact Methods, Heuristics or Learning? Choosing the Right Approach

A central skill in operations research is choosing the right solution approach for a problem, and that choice is rarely purely technical.

**Exact methods** such as mixed-integer programming can find provably optimal solutions or give a bound on how far a solution is from optimal. They work well for problems of moderate size and are valued when decisions must be defensible, for example in capacity allocation. Their limitation is computation time for very large or complex problems.

**Heuristics and metaheuristics** — such as local search, large neighbourhood search or genetic algorithms — find good solutions quickly for large problems like national parcel routing. They do not guarantee optimality, but in practice often deliver solutions within a small margin of the best achievable.

**Decomposition approaches** split large problems into smaller subproblems, for example planning rolling stock first and crews afterwards, sometimes iterating between them.

**Learning-based methods** use machine learning to predict inputs, guide search or learn policies for repeated decisions. They are promising for problems solved many times with similar structure, but require careful validation when constraints are strict.

Experienced OR professionals combine these approaches pragmatically: an exact model for a strategic plan, a heuristic for daily operations and machine learning for forecasting inputs.

## The Human Side: Planners and Dispatchers

Optimisation models rarely replace planners entirely. Planners know exceptions that are not in the data: a driver who cannot work certain routes, a customer with special delivery requirements, a maintenance activity scheduled last minute. Successful OR teams build tools that let planners adjust solutions, lock parts of a plan and understand why the model made certain choices. Adoption depends on trust, and trust grows when planners see their expertise reflected in the tool rather than overruled by it.

## Where to Find the OR Community in the Netherlands

The Dutch OR community is relatively close-knit. The LNMB organises national PhD education and the long-running annual Lunteren conference on the mathematics of operations research, where academic and industry researchers meet. University alumni networks in econometrics and industrial engineering are active, and optimisation software companies and consultancies regularly organise events and webinars. For professionals entering the field from data science, attending such events is an efficient way to understand where OR is applied and which employers are hiring.

## A Common Misconception About OR Careers

Many students and professionals believe OR is an old-fashioned discipline replaced by machine learning. In practice, optimisation remains the backbone of planning in transport, logistics, energy and healthcare, and demand for people who can formulate and solve real-world optimisation problems is strong. The misconception often arises because OR roles are titled differently — planning, supply chain, decision science — and are less visible in AI job searches.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Mathematical modelling.** Translating messy real-world problems into objective functions and constraints.
- **Solvers and tools.** Experience with commercial and open-source solvers and modelling libraries, such as Gurobi, CPLEX, OR-Tools or HiGHS.
- **Heuristics.** Designing algorithms for problems too large for exact methods.
- **Programming.** Python, Java, C++ or similar, with attention to performance.
- **Simulation and stochastic modelling.** Evaluating plans under uncertainty.
- **Data skills.** Forecasting inputs and analysing outcomes.
- **Communication.** Explaining trade-offs to planners, dispatchers and managers.

## How to Build Experience

Students in econometrics, applied mathematics, industrial engineering or computer science can take OR courses and thesis projects with companies. Professionals from data science can build optimisation skills by solving public benchmark problems, such as vehicle routing or scheduling instances, and by combining forecasts with optimisation in portfolio projects. Participating in optimisation competitions or contributing to open-source solvers also demonstrates skill.

## Career Growth in OR

OR careers often progress from analyst roles to lead optimisation engineer, planning system architect or decision science manager. Because optimisation is closely tied to operations, OR professionals frequently develop deep domain knowledge and move into strategic planning roles. Experience transfers across sectors: routing expertise from parcel logistics applies to field service planning, and scheduling expertise from rail applies to airlines and hospitals.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **Are optimisation results used for automated decisions or decision support?**
- **Which solvers and tools does the team use?**
- **How are planners and operators involved in model design?**
- **How are forecasts and optimisation combined?**
- **How is solution quality measured in practice, beyond objective values?**

- The Netherlands has strong OR traditions at NS, ProRail, PostNL and ORTEC, supported by universities and the LNMB.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: data scientist curious about OR) What is the difference between operations research and machine learning?
Machine learning mainly predicts outcomes from data, while operations research determines optimal decisions subject to constraints; they are often combined.

### (Scenario: candidate searching for jobs) Why are OR jobs hard to find with AI searches?
They are often titled planning specialist, logistics engineer, supply chain analyst or decision scientist rather than AI or optimisation.

### (Scenario: econometrics student) Which Dutch sectors hire operations research specialists?
Rail and public transport, logistics, airlines, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, retail, optimisation software vendors and consultancies.

### (Scenario: candidate interested in regulation) Do laws influence optimisation models?
Yes — driving time rules, working hours regulations and capacity allocation rules often become constraints in the models.

### (Scenario: engineer building skills) Which tools should I learn for optimisation roles?
Mathematical modelling, solvers such as Gurobi, CPLEX, OR-Tools or HiGHS, heuristics, programming and simulation.
