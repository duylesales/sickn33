In many international technology companies, the cover letter has quietly disappeared: applications consist of a CV, a profile link and perhaps a few screening questions. In the Netherlands, however, the motivation letter (motivatiebrief) remains a standard part of many application processes — particularly in the public sector, universities, research institutes, healthcare and at smaller companies. For AI and data candidates, this creates a dilemma. Is the letter actually read? And if so, what should it say that the CV does not already show? The answer is that a good motivation letter does something a CV cannot: it connects your experience to this specific role and organisation, and shows how you think.

## When the Motivation Letter Matters Most

| Employer type | Importance of the letter | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Government and public organisations | High | Selection committees often assess motivation and fit with public values explicitly |
| Universities and research institutes | High | Letters show research interests, fit with the group and communication skills |
| Healthcare organisations | Medium to high | Motivation for working in care and understanding of the context matter |
| Small and medium-sized companies | Medium to high | Owners or managers often read applications personally |
| Consultancies | Medium | Letters show communication skills and interest in client work |
| Large technology companies and scale-ups | Low to medium | Many rely on CVs, screening questions and technical assessments |

If a vacancy asks for a motivation letter, assume it will be read. If it is optional, a short, specific letter can still help, particularly when your background requires explanation — for example, a career switch, a gap or a move from another country.

## What a Motivation Letter Should Do

A strong motivation letter answers three questions clearly:

1. **Why this organisation?** What specifically attracts you — the problems it works on, its sector, its approach or its impact?
2. **Why this role?** How does the role fit your skills, interests and development?
3. **Why you?** Which experience and qualities make you likely to succeed in this role?

The letter should not repeat your CV line by line. Instead, select one or two experiences that best demonstrate your fit and explain them in more depth.

## A Structure That Works

**Opening paragraph.** State the role you are applying for and give a specific reason for your interest. Avoid generic openings such as "With great enthusiasm I am applying for..." — Dutch recruiters see this phrase (in Dutch: "Met veel enthousiasme solliciteer ik...") constantly.

**Middle paragraph or paragraphs.** Describe one or two relevant experiences in more detail: the problem, what you did, what you learned and why it matters for this role. For data roles, show how you think about data, evaluation and impact.

**Organisation paragraph.** Show that you understand what the organisation does and why your contribution would be relevant. Reference concrete aspects, such as a public project, a product, a research theme or a challenge mentioned in the vacancy.

**Closing paragraph.** Summarise your fit in one or two sentences and express interest in a conversation. Keep it brief.

**Length.** One page, typically three to five paragraphs and around 250 to 400 words, is enough.

## Examples of Weak and Strong Sentences

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| I am passionate about data and AI | During my thesis, I found that improving label quality helped more than changing the model — which is why your team's focus on data quality appeals to me |
| I have experience with machine learning | At my internship, I built a demand forecast for a regional distributor and learned to explain uncertainty to planners who were sceptical of models |
| Your company is a leader in innovation | Your work on predicting maintenance for public infrastructure connects directly to my interest in models that support long-term decisions |
| I am a team player | In a project with three faculties, I translated research questions into data requirements and kept a shared codebase usable for non-programmers |

Concrete details make the difference between a letter that sounds like hundreds of others and one that sounds like a person the reader wants to meet.

## Writing for Public Sector and Research Employers

**Public sector.** Government organisations often use selection committees that assess applications against defined criteria from the vacancy. Address each key requirement explicitly, preferably in the same order as the vacancy. Show understanding of public values such as transparency, equal treatment and careful handling of citizens' data — particularly relevant for AI and data roles after high-profile cases involving government algorithms.

**Universities and research institutes.** Describe your research interests and how they connect to the group's work. Mention relevant publications, methods or datasets, and show that you have read about the group's recent projects.

## The Open Application (Open Sollicitatie)

Dutch job seekers sometimes send an open application: a letter and CV to an organisation that has not advertised a vacancy. For AI and data roles, this can work well at smaller companies and organisations that do not recruit actively, or in regions where many opportunities are not publicly advertised.

An effective open application is short and specific. Explain what kind of role you are looking for, which problem in the organisation you could help with and why you chose this organisation. Address it to a relevant person, such as a data team lead or department manager, rather than a general mailbox where possible. Follow up once after a week or two if you receive no reply.

## Salary, Availability and Residence Status

**Salary.** Do not mention salary expectations in a motivation letter unless the vacancy explicitly asks for them. Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, as implemented, employers must provide information about the initial pay or pay range before the interview and may not ask about your pay history.

**Availability.** Mentioning your earliest start date can be useful, particularly for graduates and international candidates.

**Residence status.** International candidates can briefly mention their residence situation when relevant — for example, holding a residence permit for the orientation year after graduating in the Netherlands, or eligibility for the highly skilled migrant scheme. This helps employers understand whether sponsorship with the **Immigration and Naturalisation Service (IND)** is needed. Keep it factual and short.

## Language Choice

Write the letter in the language of the vacancy. For Dutch-language vacancies, a well-written Dutch letter is usually expected; if your Dutch is not yet strong enough for the role, an English letter may still be acceptable for English-speaking teams, but check the requirements. Poor-quality machine translations can harm your application more than a clear letter in English.

## Using AI Tools Without Producing a Generic Letter

Generative AI tools can help structure a letter, find clearer wording or correct language. The risk is that letters become generic, overly polished and similar to thousands of others. Recruiters increasingly notice typical AI-generated phrases and structures.

A practical approach:

1. Write your own draft with specific experiences and reasons, even if the language is rough.
2. Use an AI tool to suggest improvements in clarity and structure.
3. Remove generic phrases and check that every statement is accurate.
4. Read the letter aloud to ensure it sounds like you.

Remember that employers may also use tools in screening. Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems used to evaluate or filter job applications are classified as high-risk, and under the **GDPR** you have rights regarding your personal data and automated decision-making. The **NVP Recruitment Code** also sets expectations for transparent and careful recruitment procedures.

## Tailoring the Letter for Career Switchers and Returners

For candidates whose CV does not follow a straight line, the motivation letter is the best place to tell the story that connects the parts.

**Career switchers.** Explain the reason for the switch in one or two sentences and focus on how previous experience strengthens your contribution. A former teacher can highlight explaining complex topics; a former logistics planner can highlight understanding operational data. Show evidence of the steps you took to build data skills, such as a project or course, without overstating them.

**Returners after a career break.** A brief, factual mention of the break is sufficient. Focus on what you did to prepare for returning and why this role fits your next step.

**Candidates moving from academia.** Translate research experience into terms that matter for the employer: independent problem-solving, rigorous evaluation and communicating complex results.

In all cases, the letter should not apologise for your background. It should explain why your path makes you a good candidate for this particular role.

## Common Mistakes Recruiters Mention

- **Sending the same letter to every employer**, sometimes with the wrong company name.
- **Repeating the CV** instead of explaining motivation and fit.
- **Focusing only on what you want to learn**, without explaining what you will contribute.
- **Writing too long**, with several pages of general statements.
- **Ignoring the vacancy's requirements**, which makes it hard for selection committees to assess fit.
- **Spelling and grammar errors**, particularly in Dutch letters, which suggest a lack of care.
- **Generic AI-generated phrasing** that makes the letter indistinguishable from others.

## A Concrete Scenario: A Letter That Changed the Outcome

A recent master's graduate applies for a junior data scientist position at a municipality. Her CV is similar to those of other applicants: a data science degree, a thesis on forecasting and an internship at a consultancy.

In her motivation letter, she explains that her thesis on predicting demand for public services made her aware of how data can reinforce existing inequalities if historical patterns reflect unequal access. She connects this to the municipality's published ambition to use data responsibly in its social services and describes how she tested her model for differences across neighbourhoods. She addresses the vacancy's requirements in order and ends with a short, direct closing.

The selection committee invites her for an interview, noting that her letter demonstrated both technical ability and awareness of public values — something not visible in any of the CVs alone.

## A Common Misconception About Motivation Letters

A common misconception is that motivation letters are a formality nobody reads. In Dutch public organisations, research institutions and smaller companies, they are often read carefully and can determine who is invited. Another misconception is that a longer letter shows more motivation. In reality, a concise letter with specific evidence is more persuasive than a long letter full of general statements.

## A Final Checklist

- **Addressed to a person where possible, and the role clearly named.**
- **Specific reason for choosing this organisation.**
- **One or two experiences explained in depth, linked to the role.**
- **Key requirements from the vacancy addressed.**
- **No repetition of the entire CV.**
- **One page, clear language, no generic phrases.**
- **Salary only mentioned if requested; residence status briefly clarified if relevant.**
- **Written in the language of the vacancy and checked for errors.**

## Key Takeaways

- Motivation letters remain important in Dutch public organisations, research institutions, healthcare and smaller companies.
- A strong letter answers why this organisation, why this role and why you, using one or two concrete experiences.
- Keep it to one page, address vacancy requirements explicitly and avoid generic openings and phrases.
- Open applications can work well for AI and data roles at organisations that do not recruit actively.
- Use AI tools for editing, not for generating generic content; recruitment AI is high-risk under the AI Act and GDPR rights apply.

## Where to Start

For your next application, write a first draft by answering the three questions — why this organisation, why this role, why you — with one concrete example each, before polishing the language.
