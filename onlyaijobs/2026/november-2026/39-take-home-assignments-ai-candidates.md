For many AI and data roles, the interview process includes a take-home assignment: a dataset and a question, a small modelling task, a data pipeline to build or a case to analyse at home within a few days. When designed well, a take-home is one of the fairest ways to assess practical skills — it resembles real work more closely than whiteboard puzzles and gives candidates time to think. When designed poorly, it becomes an unpaid weekend project, a test of free time rather than skill, or even a way to obtain free consultancy. Candidates who understand the difference can invest their time wisely, set boundaries where needed and deliver work that genuinely stands out.

## Types of Practical Assessments in AI Hiring

| Type | What it involves | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|
| Take-home modelling task | Analyse a dataset, build and evaluate a model, report findings | A few hours of work, with several days to submit |
| Take-home engineering task | Build a small pipeline, API or deployment for a model | A few hours of work |
| Case study presentation | Analyse a business problem and present an approach | Preparation plus a presentation in the interview |
| Live coding | Solve programming or data tasks during an interview | Thirty minutes to two hours |
| Pair programming | Work with an interviewer on a realistic problem | One to two hours |
| System design discussion | Design an ML system verbally or on a whiteboard | About an hour |

Each has advantages. Take-homes allow thoughtful work; live exercises test reasoning under time pressure; design discussions show architectural thinking. Many employers combine a short take-home with a follow-up discussion.

## What a Reasonable Take-Home Looks Like

- **Clear scope.** The assignment defines the question, deliverables and evaluation criteria.
- **Limited time investment.** The employer indicates the expected effort, and it is proportional to the stage of the process — typically a few hours rather than days.
- **Realistic but artificial data.** Data is public, synthetic or anonymised, not live company data with a real business problem the company needs solved.
- **Flexibility.** Candidates can choose when to work on it within a reasonable deadline.
- **Follow-up conversation.** The work is discussed, giving candidates the opportunity to explain choices.
- **Transparency about tools.** The employer states whether AI coding assistants may be used.
- **Feedback.** Candidates receive at least brief feedback, especially after investing significant time.

The **NVP Recruitment Code**, used by many Dutch employers, emphasises that selection methods should be relevant to the job and that candidates should be informed about the procedure in advance.

## Warning Signs of an Unreasonable Assignment

- **Excessive scope.** Building a full product, dashboard and model with production-quality code, expected to take more than a day's work.
- **Real business problems.** Analysing the company's actual data to solve a current issue, or producing a strategy the company could implement directly.
- **No stated time limit or evaluation criteria.**
- **Assignments early in the process**, before any conversation, sent to large numbers of candidates.
- **Requests to transfer rights** to your work or to sign broad agreements before starting.
- **No feedback or conversation** after submission.

A single warning sign does not necessarily mean bad intent — many companies simply have not thought carefully about assessment design. But multiple signs suggest that the employer does not value candidates' time.

## Ownership of Your Work

Under the **Dutch Copyright Act**, the creator of a work generally holds copyright unless rights are transferred, for example through employment or an agreement. A candidate completing a take-home assignment is not an employee, so code and reports you create are generally yours unless you agree otherwise. Employers may, however, ask you to confirm that they may use your submission for evaluation purposes.

If an assignment includes a clause transferring all rights to the employer, read it carefully. For a short, artificial exercise, such a clause is unusual. For a task based on the company's real problem, it may indicate that the company intends to use the work.

## Trial Days and Productive Work

Some Dutch employers invite candidates for a trial day (proefdag) to experience the work environment. Short trial days aimed at assessment are common, but if a candidate performs productive work that the employer benefits from over a longer period, this may be considered work that should be paid. For take-home assignments, the same principle applies in spirit: assessment tasks should be designed to evaluate skills, not to obtain useful work for free.

## Data Protection and Candidate Data

Take-home submissions contain your personal data, and sometimes interviewers' evaluations of your work. Under the **GDPR**, employers must process candidate data lawfully and not keep it longer than necessary. The **Dutch Data Protection Authority** has indicated that application data should generally be deleted within a limited period after the procedure ends, unless the candidate consents to longer retention, for example to be considered for future vacancies. You can request access to your personal data, including evaluations, subject to certain limitations.

If an assignment involves datasets containing personal data, check whether the data is anonymised or synthetic. Reputable employers should not share real personal data with candidates.

## AI Tools During Take-Home Assignments

Generative AI coding assistants have changed practical assessments. Some employers explicitly allow them, because they reflect how work is done today. Others prohibit them to assess candidates' own skills. Many do not specify.

**Ask if unclear.** A short question about the policy shows professionalism.

**Be transparent.** If you use AI tools, mention how you used them — for example, for boilerplate code or debugging — and ensure you understand and can explain every part of the solution.

**Expect follow-up questions.** Interviewers increasingly ask candidates to explain or modify their code live, precisely to verify understanding.

On the employer side, if automated tools are used to evaluate submissions, the **EU AI Act** classifies AI systems used to evaluate candidates as high-risk, with requirements for human oversight and transparency.

## Accessibility and Reasonable Accommodations

Candidates with disabilities or chronic conditions may need adjustments, such as extended deadlines or alternative formats. Dutch equal treatment legislation includes an obligation for employers to make reasonable accommodations for people with disabilities or chronic illnesses, including in recruitment, unless this imposes a disproportionate burden. Candidates can ask for adjustments without disclosing more details than necessary.

## How to Deliver a Take-Home That Stands Out

**Start with the question.** Restate the business or research question in your own words and define what success looks like.

**Explore before modelling.** Show that you examined data quality, distributions, missing values and potential leakage.

**Establish a baseline.** Compare your model against a simple baseline. Many submissions skip this, which makes results hard to judge.

**Evaluate honestly.** Choose appropriate metrics, use proper validation and discuss limitations. Interviewers value honest assessment more than inflated accuracy.

**Keep it simple and readable.** Clean, well-organised code with clear structure is more impressive than complex techniques with messy notebooks.

**Document assumptions and trade-offs.** Explain what you would do with more time, more data or different constraints.

**Write a short summary.** Start the report with a one-paragraph conclusion that a non-technical stakeholder could understand.

**Respect the time limit.** Delivering a focused solution within the suggested time, and listing further ideas, shows good judgement.

## What Interviewers Actually Look For

| What candidates often focus on | What interviewers usually value more |
|---|---|
| Complex models | Sensible baseline and appropriate model choice |
| Maximum accuracy | Correct validation and honest discussion of limitations |
| Many visualisations | A few clear visualisations supporting conclusions |
| Long notebooks | Structured code and concise explanation |
| Covering every possible idea | Prioritisation and clear next steps |

## Presenting Your Submission in the Follow-Up Interview

The follow-up conversation about a take-home assignment is often as important as the submission itself. Interviewers use it to understand your reasoning, verify that the work is your own and see how you respond to questions and critique.

**Prepare a short walkthrough.** Be ready to summarise your approach in a few minutes: the question, the key data issues you found, your modelling choices, results and limitations.

**Know your weak points.** Identify parts of your solution that could be challenged — a validation choice, a missing feature, a simplifying assumption — and prepare to discuss alternatives.

**Welcome critique.** When an interviewer questions a choice, respond with curiosity rather than defensiveness. Explaining how you would adjust the approach given new information shows exactly the collaborative judgement teams need.

**Connect to the role.** Where possible, relate your solution to the problems the team works on. For example, explain how your approach would change with real-time data or stricter privacy requirements.

**Be ready to modify code.** Some interviewers ask candidates to make a small change live. Keeping your code clean and well organised makes this much easier.

## When to Decline an Assignment

Declining is reasonable if an assignment is excessive, if you are early in the process with little information about the role, or if you have several offers progressing. A polite response might explain that you are very interested in the role, that the expected time investment is difficult at this stage and ask whether an alternative is possible, such as a shorter task, a live exercise or discussing a previous project. Many employers are open to alternatives, particularly for experienced candidates.

## A Concrete Scenario: Two Submissions for the Same Assignment

A retailer asks candidates to predict product returns using an anonymised dataset, with an estimated time of four hours.

The first candidate spends a weekend building an ensemble of complex models, achieves slightly higher accuracy and submits a long notebook with dozens of charts but little explanation. The second candidate spends five hours, starts by identifying a data leakage issue in one column, builds a simple baseline and a gradient boosting model, evaluates results by product category and writes a one-page summary explaining which returns are predictable, what the model cannot capture and how the retailer could test it.

The second candidate is invited to the final round. In the discussion, interviewers mention that spotting the leakage and explaining limitations showed exactly the judgement they need.

## A Common Misconception About Take-Home Assignments

Many candidates believe that investing more time always improves their chances. In practice, interviewers often compare submissions against the expected time and value focus and judgement over volume. Another misconception is that candidates must accept any assignment to be taken seriously. Politely asking about scope or proposing alternatives is professional and often respected.

## Questions to Ask Before Starting

- **How much time do you expect this to take?**
- **What are the evaluation criteria?**
- **May I use AI coding assistants, and should I document their use?**
- **Will there be a follow-up discussion of my submission?**
- **Is the data synthetic or anonymised?**
- **Will I receive feedback?**

## Key Takeaways

- Well-designed take-home assignments have clear scope, limited time, artificial or anonymised data, stated criteria and a follow-up conversation.
- Warning signs include excessive scope, real business problems, rights transfers and no feedback.
- Candidates generally retain copyright in their submissions; GDPR limits retention of candidate data and gives access rights.
- Clarify the policy on AI tools and be able to explain every part of your solution.
- Standout submissions focus on the question, data quality, baselines, honest evaluation, clear code and concise summaries within the expected time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate receiving a large assignment) How long should a take-home assignment take?
A reasonable assignment usually requires a few hours of work, proportionate to the stage of the process; multi-day projects are excessive for most roles.

### (Scenario: candidate worried about free work) Who owns the code I write for a take-home assignment?
Generally you do, as you are not an employee, unless you agree to transfer rights; be cautious with assignments based on real business problems.

### (Scenario: candidate using coding assistants) Can I use AI tools during a take-home assignment?
Ask about the employer's policy; if allowed, be transparent about how you used them and make sure you can explain all your code.

### (Scenario: candidate with limited time) Can I decline a take-home assignment?
Yes — politely explain your situation and ask whether an alternative, such as a shorter task or discussing a previous project, is possible.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting to stand out) What makes a take-home submission stand out?
A clear question, data quality checks, a baseline, honest evaluation, readable code and a short summary of conclusions and limitations.
