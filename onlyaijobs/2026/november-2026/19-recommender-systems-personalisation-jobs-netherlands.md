When you open a travel site, a webshop, a food delivery app or a news website, much of what you see first was chosen by an algorithm. Recommender systems and search ranking determine which hotels appear on top, which products are suggested, which restaurants are shown and which articles are highlighted. The Netherlands, and Amsterdam in particular, is home to several technology companies operating these systems at European and global scale. For machine learning engineers and data scientists, recommendation and personalisation offer some of the most mature, data-rich and experimentation-driven AI work in the country — increasingly shaped by European rules on transparency and fairness.

## Why Recommendation Is One of the Most Valuable ML Applications

For platforms with large catalogues, users cannot browse everything. Recommendation and search determine what users discover, which directly affects conversion, customer satisfaction and revenue. Small improvements in relevance, applied to millions of sessions, have large business impact. That is why companies invest heavily in ranking teams, experimentation platforms and personalisation infrastructure.

The work also involves difficult trade-offs: relevance versus diversity, short-term clicks versus long-term satisfaction, personalisation versus privacy, and the interests of users versus those of sellers or partners on a platform.

## The Named Employers in the Dutch Market

**Booking.com.** Headquartered in Amsterdam, the travel platform is known for its large-scale experimentation culture and uses machine learning for ranking accommodations, personalisation and many other aspects of the customer experience.

**bol.** The Dutch and Belgian e-commerce platform, headquartered in Utrecht, uses search and recommendation to help customers find products among a very large catalogue, including products from partner sellers.

**Picnic.** The Amsterdam-based online grocery company uses data and machine learning across its operations, including personalisation of the shopping experience and demand forecasting.

**Coolblue.** The Rotterdam-based electronics retailer uses data to support customer decisions and online experience.

**Just Eat Takeaway.com.** The Amsterdam-headquartered food delivery company ranks restaurants and dishes and personalises the experience for users in many markets.

**DPG Media.** The media company, with major operations in Amsterdam, uses data for personalisation of news and media platforms.

Many other companies — broadcasters, streaming services, marketplaces, retailers and job platforms — also work on search and recommendation, often with smaller teams.

## Technical Challenges in Recommendation

**Cold start.** Recommending items or users with little history requires content-based methods, popularity signals or exploration strategies.

**Scale and latency.** Systems must retrieve and rank from large catalogues within milliseconds, typically combining fast candidate retrieval with more complex ranking models.

**Feedback loops.** Recommendations influence what users click, which influences training data, which can amplify popularity bias.

**Offline versus online evaluation.** Offline metrics often fail to predict online performance, making controlled experiments essential.

**Multiple objectives.** Balancing relevance, diversity, novelty, business value and fairness to sellers or content creators.

**Context and intent.** Understanding whether a user is browsing, planning or ready to buy.

**Generative and semantic search.** Language models and embeddings increasingly support semantic search and conversational discovery, adding new evaluation challenges.

## The Rules That Increasingly Govern Ranking

**Digital Services Act (EU) 2022/2065.** Online platforms using recommender systems must explain in their terms and conditions the main parameters used and any options users have to modify them. Very large online platforms must offer at least one option for recommendations not based on profiling and must assess systemic risks, including those related to their recommender systems.

**Platform-to-Business Regulation (EU) 2019/1150.** Online intermediation services and search engines must be transparent towards business users about the main parameters determining ranking.

**Consumer protection.** EU consumer law requires traders to inform consumers about the main parameters determining ranking in search results and to disclose paid placement. In the Netherlands, the **Authority for Consumers and Markets (ACM)** enforces consumer protection rules and has published guidance on online persuasion techniques and misleading design.

**GDPR.** Personalisation relies on personal data and profiling, requiring a lawful basis, transparency and respect for user rights, including objecting to direct marketing profiling.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** Most recommender systems are not high-risk, but transparency obligations and rules against manipulative practices are relevant, and systems used in areas such as employment matching can fall into high-risk categories.

## A Concrete Scenario: When Clicks Are Not Enough

An e-commerce platform introduces a new recommendation model that increases click-through rates in an A/B test. The team celebrates — until a follow-up analysis shows that return rates for purchased products increased and repeat visits declined slightly. The model had learned to promote attractive-looking products with lower satisfaction.

The team redefines success metrics, adding post-purchase signals such as returns and reviews, and introduces longer-running experiments to capture repeat behaviour. The next model shows smaller click improvements but better long-term outcomes. The case illustrates a central lesson in recommendation work: optimising the easiest metric can harm users and the business over time.

## Working in an Experimentation Culture

Several large Dutch platforms are known for running many controlled experiments. Working in such an environment changes how data scientists and engineers operate. Ideas are expected to be tested rather than debated; intuition matters less than evidence. Teams need reliable experimentation platforms, clear metric definitions and statistical rigour to avoid false conclusions from many simultaneous tests.

For new team members, this culture has advantages: decisions are transparent, and good ideas can prove their value quickly regardless of who proposed them. It also has challenges. Many experiments show no significant effect, which can be discouraging. Short-term metrics can dominate if long-term effects are not measured. And interactions between experiments running at the same time require careful design. Candidates who understand experimental design, statistical power and the limits of A/B testing are particularly valued.

## Two-Sided Marketplaces: Balancing Users and Sellers

Many Dutch platforms are marketplaces connecting consumers with sellers, restaurants, accommodations or content providers. Recommendation and ranking on these platforms affect not only user experience but also the income of businesses on the other side. Promoting only the most popular items can concentrate demand on a few large sellers, while newer or smaller businesses struggle to gain visibility.

Ranking teams therefore work on questions such as how to give new listings fair exposure, how to include quality signals beyond popularity and how to separate sponsored placement from organic ranking. These are business and ethical decisions as much as technical ones, and European rules on ranking transparency for business users make them more visible.

## Search Versus Recommendation: Two Related Careers

Search and recommendation are closely related but differ in important ways. In search, a user expresses intent through a query, and the system must understand that intent and rank relevant results. In recommendation, the system anticipates what a user might want without an explicit query, based on context and behaviour.

Search roles emphasise information retrieval, query understanding, spelling correction, synonyms, language-specific processing and relevance evaluation with human judgements. For Dutch-language platforms, handling compound words and Dutch-Flemish vocabulary differences is a practical challenge. Recommendation roles emphasise user modelling, collaborative signals, exploration and long-term engagement.

In practice, many teams work on both, and modern systems increasingly blur the boundary: semantic search uses embeddings similar to those in recommendation, and personalised search combines query relevance with user preferences. Professionals who understand both perspectives are well placed for senior roles in discovery and relevance teams.

## Privacy-Preserving Personalisation

Personalisation depends on data about users, but expectations and rules around privacy are strict. In the Netherlands, placing non-essential cookies and similar tracking technologies generally requires consent under the Telecommunications Act, and the GDPR requires a lawful basis for profiling. As a result, platforms increasingly rely on first-party data from logged-in users, contextual signals such as the current search or page, and session-based recommendations that do not require long-term profiles.

Techniques such as aggregation, data minimisation and on-device processing can reduce privacy risks. For engineers, designing recommendations that work well with limited personal data is a valuable skill — and one that becomes more important as regulation and user expectations evolve.

## A Common Misconception About Recommendation Careers

Many engineers assume recommendation work is mainly about training sophisticated deep learning models. In reality, much of the impact comes from problem framing, data quality, feature engineering, retrieval efficiency, experimentation design and metric definition. Another misconception is that recommendation is only relevant at big tech companies. Many mid-sized Dutch companies — retailers, publishers, job platforms and marketplaces — need search and recommendation expertise and often offer broader responsibility.

## Skills That Stand Out

- **Ranking and retrieval methods:** collaborative filtering, learning to rank, embeddings and two-stage retrieval-ranking architectures.
- **Experimentation:** A/B testing, statistical power, long-term effects and interference.
- **Feature engineering and data pipelines:** user behaviour, content features and real-time signals.
- **Search technology:** information retrieval, query understanding and semantic search.
- **Production engineering:** low-latency serving, caching and monitoring.
- **Metrics and product thinking:** defining success beyond clicks.
- **Regulatory awareness:** transparency, profiling and consumer protection rules.

## Building Relevant Experience

Public datasets for recommendation and search allow candidates to build portfolio projects, but the most convincing projects go beyond training a model. Show how you evaluated offline, discuss how you would test online, analyse popularity bias or diversity and explain trade-offs. Contributions to open-source recommendation or search libraries also demonstrate relevant skills.

## Career Growth

Recommendation careers can progress towards senior ML engineer or scientist roles, ranking or search team leadership and product-oriented roles such as personalisation product manager. Experience with large-scale experimentation is highly transferable across e-commerce, media, travel and fintech. Knowledge of platform regulation is increasingly valuable as European rules expand.

## Questions Worth Asking in an Interview

- **How are recommendation models evaluated offline and online?**
- **Which metrics define success, and how are long-term effects measured?**
- **How does the team handle transparency and non-profiling options?**
- **How are sponsored and organic results separated?**
- **What is the balance between research, engineering and experimentation?**
