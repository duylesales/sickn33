Drachten has no railway station, and most people in the Randstad would struggle to place it on a map. Yet millions of people around the world use products that were designed and made there. Philips has developed and manufactured personal care products such as shavers in Drachten for decades, and the site is known for a high degree of manufacturing automation. Around this anchor, a regional community of engineers, suppliers and technical education has grown. For data professionals in the north of the Netherlands, Drachten is a reminder that industrial AI does not only happen in Eindhoven.

## Why a Frisian Town Became a Manufacturing Technology Site

Philips established activities in Drachten in the mid-twentieth century, as part of a broader move to spread industrial employment across the country. Over time, the site specialised in personal care appliances and the precision manufacturing required to produce them at very high volumes. Shaving heads, for example, require extremely precise metal parts, and producing them in large numbers has driven investment in robotics, automated assembly and quality control.

Because labour costs in the Netherlands are high, competing with manufacturing elsewhere required automating as much as possible. That necessity turned Drachten into a location with deep experience in smart manufacturing — long before "Industry 4.0" became a common term.

## The Named Organisations Behind Drachten's Technology Market

**Philips.** The Drachten site combines product development and manufacturing for personal care products. Development involves mechanical engineering, electronics, software, industrial design and consumer research. Manufacturing involves precision metalworking, plastics, automated assembly and testing. Many modern personal care products are connected to apps, which adds consumer data and software development to the mix.

**Regional suppliers and engineering firms.** A large manufacturing site creates demand for machine builders, automation specialists, tooling companies and engineering service providers. Many of these are small and medium-sized companies in Friesland and Groningen that build and maintain automated production equipment.

**Knowledge institutions.** The **University of Groningen** and **Hanze University of Applied Sciences** in Groningen, **NHL Stenden University of Applied Sciences** in Leeuwarden and **Campus Fryslân**, the University of Groningen's faculty in Leeuwarden, supply engineers, data scientists and researchers to the region.

## Smart Manufacturing: Where Data Meets Micrometres

In high-volume precision manufacturing, small deviations add up quickly. A tool that wears slightly can produce thousands of parts just outside tolerance before a traditional inspection catches the problem. Data-driven manufacturing aims to detect such deviations earlier: sensors on machines, cameras on lines and measurement data from quality systems are combined to predict when a process is drifting.

This work is different from typical data science in an office. Data comes from machines with their own control systems, timestamps must be aligned across sensors, and a model is only useful if it can trigger action on the production floor in time. Engineers who combine data skills with understanding of machines, materials and control systems are particularly valuable.

## Connected Products: A New Data Stream

Many personal care products now connect to apps that provide guidance, track usage or give personalised tips. This creates a data stream that product teams can use to understand how products are used in daily life — which features matter, where users struggle and how products wear over time.

It also introduces responsibilities. Product usage data can be personal data, users must understand what is collected, and privacy expectations for personal care products are high. Data professionals working on connected products spend significant effort on consent, data minimisation and aggregate analysis instead of individual tracking.

## Regulation That Shapes the Work

**EU Data Act (EU) 2023/2854.** Applicable since September 2025, the Data Act gives users of connected products rights to access data generated by their use of those products and to share it with third parties. Manufacturers must design products and services with data access in mind, which affects data architecture for connected devices.

**EU Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230.** This regulation, replacing the Machinery Directive and applying from January 2027, includes requirements for machinery with self-evolving behaviour and safety functions based on software, which is relevant for AI in automated production equipment.

**EU Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853.** The revised directive explicitly covers software, including AI systems, as a product, which increases attention to software quality and documentation.

**Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (EU) 2024/1781.** Introduces requirements for product sustainability, repairability and information, including digital product passports for certain product groups over time. This creates data requirements across product development and manufacturing.

**GDPR.** Applies to app and usage data from connected products.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** AI used as a safety component in machinery can be high-risk, and consumer-facing AI features have transparency obligations.

## A Concrete Scenario: Inspection With Very Few Defects

A production line makes precision parts with an extremely low defect rate. A team wants to replace manual sample inspection with automated visual inspection on every part. The difficulty: there are almost no images of defective parts to train on, and new types of defects may appear that nobody has seen before.

The team switches from a classification approach to anomaly detection, training models on what good parts look like and flagging anything that deviates. Operators review flagged parts, and their decisions become new training data. The system catches defects that sample inspection used to miss, but it also flags harmless variations, so thresholds must be tuned carefully with quality engineers. The case illustrates a general truth in manufacturing AI: when defects are rare, the problem is less about accuracy and more about managing false alarms without missing the important exceptions.

## A Common Misconception About Manufacturing AI

Many data professionals assume manufacturing AI is mostly about predictive maintenance dashboards. In practice, some of the most valuable applications are in quality and process control, where models are integrated directly into production systems and influence what happens to each part. That requires reliability, latency guarantees and close cooperation with automation engineers — a very different engineering culture from analytics teams that deliver reports.

Experience in high-volume precision manufacturing and connected consumer products is valued across industry: in high-tech equipment manufacturing, automotive, medical devices and consumer electronics. Within the north of the Netherlands, careers can move between manufacturers, machine builders and technology consultancies. And because smart manufacturing skills combine data, automation and domain knowledge, professionals with this profile are among the harder-to-find candidates for industrial employers nationwide.

## Working at a Regional Site of a Global Company

A site that belongs to a multinational group works differently from an independent company. Strategic decisions about product portfolios, investment and sometimes even which products are manufactured where are made at group level. Data and IT platforms are often standardised globally, which means local teams work with group-wide tools and governance. Colleagues may sit in other countries, and meetings often take place in English across time zones.

For data professionals, this has advantages and disadvantages. On the positive side, there is access to large-scale data platforms, global expertise and the possibility to work on products used worldwide. Career paths can extend beyond the local site to international roles. On the other hand, local initiatives must fit group standards, and changes in global strategy can affect local priorities. Asking how decisions are made between the site and the group is one of the most useful questions in an interview.
