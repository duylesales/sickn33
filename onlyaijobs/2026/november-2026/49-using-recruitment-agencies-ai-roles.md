When an AI vacancy has been open for months, an agency's promise to deliver a shortlist within weeks is tempting. For some roles and organisations, agencies are genuinely the best option: they have networks, time and expertise that internal recruitment lacks. For others, the result is high fees for candidates who could have been reached directly, or a flood of CVs that do not match the role. Using agencies well requires understanding how they are paid, what their incentives are, which contract terms matter and which alternatives exist.

This article provides general information, not legal advice.

## Types of Agency Services

| Model | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency recruitment | The agency is paid only when a candidate it presented is hired | Most common for individual AI and data roles |
| Retained search | The employer pays part of the fee upfront for a dedicated search | Senior and leadership roles, rare specialists |
| Secondment or staffing | The agency or firm employs the professional and places them with the employer, charging an hourly or daily rate | Temporary capacity, projects, try-before-hire arrangements |
| Interim and freelance intermediaries | The intermediary connects employers with self-employed professionals | Short-term expertise and projects |
| Recruitment process outsourcing | An external party manages all or part of the recruitment function | Organisations with high hiring volumes |

For permanent hires, fees are commonly calculated as a percentage of the candidate's first-year salary, with the exact percentage depending on seniority, scarcity and exclusivity. Retained searches for senior roles are typically more expensive but come with dedicated effort.

## Understanding Agency Incentives

Contingency agencies earn nothing unless a candidate they presented is hired. This creates predictable behaviour: agencies prioritise vacancies where they expect to place quickly, present candidates to several clients at once and may send many CVs to increase their chances. None of this is inherently wrong, but it means employers should manage agencies actively rather than expecting them to act as neutral advisers.

Good specialist agencies counter these incentives with reputation: they know that presenting poorly matched candidates damages their relationship with clients and candidates in a small market.

## When Agencies Add Real Value

- **Senior or rare roles,** such as a head of data science or an ML engineer with specific domain experience, where passive candidates must be approached personally.
- **Urgent hiring** when internal recruitment capacity is lacking.
- **Confidential searches,** for example when replacing someone who has not yet left.
- **Unfamiliar markets,** such as hiring in a new region or country.
- **Organisations without recruitment expertise** in AI and data roles, where an agency can help define requirements and market salaries.

## When Other Channels Often Work Better

- **Junior and medior roles** with a reasonable candidate pool, where direct publication reaches many qualified applicants.
- **Roles that benefit from local candidates,** who search by distance and location.
- **Organisations with a strong network,** where employee referrals and community events generate candidates.
- **Employers with limited budgets,** such as SMEs, research institutions and public organisations.
- **Roles with clear, attractive propositions,** where candidates are likely to apply directly when they see the vacancy.

Specialist job boards for AI and data roles allow employers to reach candidates who are already filtering specifically for these roles, often at a fraction of agency costs.

## Contract Terms That Matter

**Fee structure.** Clarify the percentage or amount, the salary components on which it is calculated — base salary only or including bonuses and allowances — and when invoices are due.

**Guarantee or replacement period.** Many agencies offer a replacement or partial refund if the candidate leaves or is dismissed within a certain period. Check conditions carefully.

**Exclusivity.** Exclusive arrangements may bring more effort from the agency but limit your options. Consider time-limited exclusivity, such as a few weeks.

**Candidate ownership.** Agree how long a presented candidate "belongs" to the agency, to avoid disputes if the candidate applies directly later or through another channel.

**Double submissions.** Agree that agencies must obtain the candidate's explicit consent before presenting them, and define how conflicts are handled if the same candidate is presented by multiple parties.

**Non-solicitation.** Agencies should not approach candidates they placed with you, or your employees, for other roles within a certain period.

**Data protection.** Agree on roles, responsibilities and retention of candidate data.

## Legal Requirements for Labour Providers

**Waadi registration.** Under the **Placement of Personnel by Intermediaries Act (Waadi)**, companies that make workers available to others must be registered as such in the trade register of the **Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK)**. Employers can check registration in the KVK register. This is particularly relevant for secondment and staffing arrangements.

**Mandatory admission system.** The Netherlands is introducing a mandatory admission system for labour providers through the **Labour Provider Admission Act (Wet toelating terbeschikkingstelling arbeidskrachten)**. Once fully in effect, companies may only hire workers through admitted labour providers. Employers using secondment or staffing arrangements should check the current implementation timeline.

**Certification and associations.** Certification by **Stichting Normering Arbeid (SNA)** indicates that a labour provider has been checked for compliance with obligations such as paying wages and taxes. Membership of associations such as **ABU** or **NBBU** involves codes of conduct.

**Liability.** In certain situations, hiring companies can be liable for wage or tax obligations of labour providers, which makes due diligence important.

## Data Protection and AI Tools

Agencies process large amounts of candidate data. Clarify whether the agency acts as an independent controller or as a processor for the employer, how candidates are informed and how long data is retained. Under the **GDPR**, candidates must be informed about processing, and data should not be kept longer than necessary.

Many agencies use AI tools for sourcing, matching and screening. Under the **EU AI Act**, AI systems used to filter applications or evaluate candidates are high-risk. Ask agencies which tools they use, how human oversight works and how candidates are informed — as the employer, you have an interest in fair and lawful processes, even when outsourced.

## Pay Transparency Applies to Agencies Too

Under the **EU Pay Transparency Directive**, candidates must receive information about initial pay or its range before the interview, and pay history questions are prohibited. When agencies recruit on your behalf, ensure they provide the required information and do not ask candidates about their current salary. Agencies that routinely ask "what are you earning now?" will need to change practices.

## Managing Agencies Effectively

- **Brief the agency thoroughly** on the role, team, challenges, salary range and selection criteria.
- **Limit the number of agencies** to avoid duplicate candidates and diluted effort.
- **Give fast feedback** on presented candidates, so the agency can calibrate.
- **Evaluate performance** on quality of shortlists, time to hire, retention of placed candidates and candidate experience.
- **Maintain your own channels** in parallel, such as direct publication and referrals, unless exclusivity has been agreed.

## Comparing Total Costs

| Channel | Direct costs | Indirect costs | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contingency agency | Placement fee | Time reviewing many CVs, risk of duplicates | Scarce or urgent roles |
| Retained search | Upfront and completion fees | Management of search | Senior leadership and rare specialists |
| Specialist job board | Posting costs, sometimes free first listing | Internal screening time | Most AI and data roles with a reasonable pool |
| Generalist job board | Posting and promotion costs | High volume of irrelevant applications | Broad roles, high-volume hiring |
| Referrals | Referral bonus | Risk of homogeneous hiring | Roles in connected communities |
| Secondment | Hourly margin over time | Dependence on external firm | Temporary or try-before-hire needs |

## Writing an Effective Agency Briefing

The quality of candidates an agency presents depends heavily on the quality of the briefing. A good briefing for an AI or data role covers:

- **The problem the role solves,** such as improving forecast accuracy or bringing models into production.
- **The team and data context,** including tools, data maturity and who the hire will work with.
- **Must-have versus nice-to-have requirements,** clearly separated.
- **The salary range and total package,** including hybrid policy and working hours.
- **The selection process,** including assessments, timeline and decision-makers.
- **What makes the role attractive,** in terms the agency can use when approaching passive candidates.
- **Deal-breakers,** such as language requirements or location.

A thirty-minute briefing call with the hiring manager — not only HR — usually improves shortlist quality more than any contract clause.

## Evaluating Agencies After the Hire

Recruitment costs are only justified if the hire succeeds. Evaluate agencies not only on speed but also on outcomes:

- **Shortlist quality:** how many presented candidates reached interviews and final rounds.
- **Retention:** whether placed candidates stayed and performed well after the first year.
- **Candidate experience:** feedback from candidates about how the agency represented the company.
- **Compliance:** whether the agency respected data protection, consent and pay transparency practices.
- **Market insight:** whether the agency provided useful information on salaries and candidate availability.

Sharing this evaluation with agencies helps good partners improve and makes it easier to decide which agencies to work with next time.

## A Concrete Scenario: Three Agencies, One Candidate

A mid-sized manufacturer opens a vacancy for a data scientist and informs three agencies. Within a week, it receives twelve CVs. Two agencies present the same candidate, each claiming a fee. Several candidates have no manufacturing or production data experience. The hiring manager spends hours reviewing CVs and resolving the duplicate dispute.

For the next vacancy, the manufacturer changes approach. It publishes the role on a specialist AI job board and in regional networks, with a clear description and salary range, and engages one specialist agency under a time-limited exclusive agreement only for a senior role that remains difficult to fill. The data scientist role is filled through direct applications from candidates living within commuting distance; the senior role is filled through the agency. Total recruitment costs decrease, and the hiring manager spends less time on irrelevant CVs.

## A Common Misconception

A common misconception is that agencies always find better candidates than employers can reach directly. For scarce senior roles, good agencies often do. For many junior and medior AI roles, the same candidates are actively searching and applying directly — and are sometimes approached by several agencies at once. Another misconception is that using more agencies increases the chance of success. In practice, multiple agencies often compete for the same candidates, causing duplicates, confusion and a poor candidate experience.

## Key Takeaways

- Agencies offer contingency, retained, secondment and interim models, each with different incentives and costs.
- Agencies add most value for senior, rare, urgent or confidential roles; direct and specialist channels often work better for junior and medior roles.
- Check fees, guarantee periods, exclusivity, candidate ownership, double submissions and data protection terms.
- Verify Waadi registration for labour providers, follow developments on the mandatory admission system and consider SNA certification.
- Agencies must also respect GDPR, AI Act and pay transparency rules; manage agencies actively and compare total costs across channels.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer considering an agency) How are recruitment agencies paid for AI roles?
Most contingency agencies charge a fee, often a percentage of first-year salary, when a presented candidate is hired; retained searches involve upfront payments.

### (Scenario: employer receiving duplicate CVs) What happens if two agencies present the same candidate?
Agree in contracts that agencies need candidate consent before presenting them and define how conflicts are resolved, typically based on who presented first with consent.

### (Scenario: employer using secondment) Do labour providers need to be registered?
Yes — companies that make workers available must be registered under the Waadi in the KVK trade register, and a mandatory admission system is being introduced.

### (Scenario: SME with a limited budget) Is an agency necessary for hiring a data scientist?
Often not — for many roles, a clear vacancy on specialist and regional channels reaches qualified candidates at much lower cost.

### (Scenario: employer outsourcing recruitment) Do pay transparency rules apply when an agency recruits for us?
Yes — candidates must receive pay information before interviews and must not be asked about pay history, regardless of who conducts recruitment.
