🏥 Healthcare is one of the most sought-after destinations for ML engineers in the Netherlands and one of the hardest to enter. Not because the technical bar is higher — in places it's lower. Because of data access.

🔒 You cannot demonstrate capability on real clinical data before having an institutional affiliation. Access requires an approved purpose and usually ethical review. So the normal route of building a portfolio on public data and applying simply doesn't exist here, which is why the sector looks closed from outside.

🚪 But six entry routes exist, and four of them need no academic background:
· Hospital data and IT functions — capacity planning, reporting, integration. Less glamorous, far more accessible, and being inside gives you the context and relationships that enable clinical projects later
· Medical device and health software companies — engineering standards often higher than research, because the output is a regulated product
· Health insurers — claims data at population scale, supporting different questions from clinical records
· Consultancies serving healthcare

Candidates who assume a PhD is required exclude most of what's available.

⚠️ The technical trap that catches capable people: clinical data records what clinicians did as much as what happened to patients. If a measurement exists, someone decided to take it. So models trained on records frequently learn clinical judgement rather than physiology — a deterioration model may be detecting that a nurse increased monitoring, which is genuinely predictive and completely useless because it only tells you what the nurse already knew.

Worse, it validates beautifully retrospectively and fails entirely in prospective use. Raising this unprompted in an interview signals real experience.

📋 And regulation shapes the work more than technique does. Clinical decision support is a regulated medical device — documentation, risk analysis and clinical validation absorb a large share of effort.
