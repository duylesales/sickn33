🔧 "Semiconductor AI" in the Netherlands covers two different industries, and candidates conflate them constantly.

Equipment development — building the machines that make chips — is measurement and inverse inference at nanometre scale. Chip manufacturing is yield attribution across hundreds of process steps. Both are hard. The skills only partly overlap.

📉 Yield is the organising concept in manufacturing, and it produces a distinctive analytical situation. A wafer passes through hundreds of steps over weeks. At the end some proportion of devices work. That proportion determines profitability directly, because processing cost is largely independent of how many good devices come off.

So when yield drops, the cause sits somewhere in a long sequence — possibly in an interaction between steps, possibly in one tool, possibly in a material lot. With substantial confounding. And weeks of delay between cause and observation, during which material keeps being processed.

🗺️ A nice detail: where on a wafer the failures occur is diagnostic. Edge effects, radial patterns, repeated patterns matching equipment geometry — each points to different causes. Reading those patterns is a vision problem with unusually strong domain priors.

📊 Why the statistical bar is genuinely high: you're often chasing a yield improvement of a fraction of a per cent, on data that was expensive to generate, with hundreds of candidate process parameters. Screen them all against yield and you'll find apparently significant results by chance, guaranteed. Handling multiple comparisons properly isn't optional — failing to is how expensive investigations get launched on noise.

And a correlation without a plausible physical mechanism is treated with suspicion, correctly. Engineers who can propose and test mechanisms are valued far above those who can only find patterns. Your work will often be reviewed by physicists.

🚪 Entry point if your background isn't physics: the precision supplier network and engineering services. Real technical content, considerably less competition than the largest employers.
