Brainport Eindhoven gets cited constantly as a model for regional tech development, and just as often, regional organizations elsewhere try to replicate it by copying the visible parts — a branded regional identity, a cluster-marketing campaign — while the parts that actually took decades to build go unaddressed. That's not a criticism of the ambition; it's a caution about which parts of the model are genuinely portable.

## What Doesn't Transfer Easily

Brainport's foundation rests on a specific, long-established anchor institution and a multi-decade industrial history that produced a dense supplier network around it. That combination isn't something a different region can build in a few years through marketing alone — it reflects a genuinely different starting point that took generations to form.

## What Actually Does Transfer

**The visibility principle, at a much smaller scale.** Regardless of whether a region has a comparable anchor institution, the underlying problem — local opportunity existing but not being visible to local talent — applies everywhere, and the fix (making existing local jobs checkable, not aspirational) doesn't require decades of industrial history to implement.

**Coordinated relationships between education, employers and local government.** The specific mechanism of connecting career services, employers and municipal economic development doesn't require an anchor institution — it requires deliberate coordination, which is a matter of organizational will rather than industrial history.

**Starting from what already exists rather than what you wish existed.** Brainport's own narrative, honestly told, is about compounding an existing industrial base rather than manufacturing one from nothing. The transferable lesson is to inventory and make visible what a region's economy already has, rather than assuming success requires an equivalent anchor institution first.

## Where to Start

Rather than asking "how do we become the next Brainport," a more useful question for most regions is narrower: what employers already exist here doing AI/ML/Data-adjacent work, and how visible is that work to the local talent who might otherwise leave.

## A Framework for Separating the Portable From the Non-Portable

**Non-portable: the anchor institution and its multi-decade supplier network.** This took roughly half a century to form around a specific industrial history and cannot be replicated on any policy timeline.

**Non-portable: the specific industrial specialization itself.** A region without Eindhoven's semiconductor and precision-manufacturing history cannot simply decide to have one — its actual industrial base, whatever that is, is the correct starting point instead.

**Portable: the visibility mechanism.** Making existing local opportunity checkable rather than assumed is a general-purpose fix that doesn't depend on any particular industry.

**Portable: the coordination habit.** Deliberately connecting education, employers and local government, as a standing practice rather than an occasional project, transfers regardless of what the regional economy actually specializes in.

## Comparing What a Region Should and Shouldn't Try to Copy

| Element of the Brainport story | Should other regions copy this directly? | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Decades-long industrial specialization | No | Inventory and build on your own region's actual existing base |
| Anchor-institution supplier network | No | Identify your own region's closest equivalent, however modest |
| Visibility of existing local opportunity | Yes | Directly transferable at small cost |
| Cross-institution coordination habit | Yes | Directly transferable, mostly a matter of organizational will |

## Why Starting From Envy Produces Worse Strategy Than Starting From Inventory

Regions that begin by asking "how do we become the next Brainport" tend to import assumptions about scale and industry type that don't match their actual starting conditions, which produces strategies calibrated to the wrong reality. Regions that begin by inventorying what already exists locally — however modest — end up with a strategy that's smaller in ambition but far more likely to be actionable within existing budgets and existing institutional relationships.

## A Walkthrough: What a Realistic Regional Strategy Looks Like Instead

Rather than a region announcing an ambition to become "the next Brainport," a more realistic strategy starts narrower: a regional economic office spends a few weeks cataloguing which local employers already do anything resembling AI, ML or data-adjacent work — however modest, however unlabeled as "AI" — using the same kind of pattern-recognition discussed elsewhere on this site (looking past job titles to underlying work). This inventory alone, completed in weeks rather than years, often reveals more existing local capacity than anyone in the regional office previously realized, simply because that capacity was never surveyed using the right lens.

## A Misconception About What Makes a Regional Strategy "Ambitious Enough"

Regional leaders sometimes feel pressure to announce a big, headline-grabbing ambition — matching or exceeding a well-known success story — to justify political or budgetary support for an initiative. This pressure can push a region toward strategies that sound ambitious but are calibrated to the wrong starting conditions, when a narrower, more honestly-scoped strategy built on an actual inventory of existing local capacity is both more achievable and, ultimately, more likely to produce real results worth announcing later.

## Why Modesty in Initial Framing Often Produces Bigger Results Over Time

Counterintuitively, a region that starts with a modest, inventory-based strategy and executes it well tends to build more durable, compounding results than a region that starts with a grand comparison to an established success story and struggles to match expectations calibrated to a fundamentally different starting point. Success stories like Brainport's own trajectory, if examined honestly, typically began with exactly this kind of modest, incremental approach rather than an announced ambition to become a global hub — the ambition, where it exists now, was earned over decades, not declared at the outset.

## What a Realistic Timeline Looks Like for the Portable Elements

While the non-portable elements of Brainport's success took decades to build, the genuinely portable elements — visibility mechanisms and coordination habits — can show meaningful results within a much shorter timeframe, often within a single year of consistent effort. Setting this expectation explicitly at the outset of a regional initiative helps prevent the common failure mode where a region announces a Brainport-inspired initiative, expects Brainport-scale results within a similarly short window, and then abandons the effort as a perceived failure when the (unrealistic) timeline isn't met — when in fact the portable elements were working exactly as expected, just at a modest, honest scale appropriate to what they can actually deliver.

## Why Documenting and Sharing Results Honestly Matters for Other Regions

A region that successfully implements the portable elements of this approach — even at modest scale — contributes valuable, honestly-scoped evidence that other regions can learn from, in a way that comparisons to Brainport's full, decades-long success story cannot. Regional organizations considering this kind of initiative benefit from actively seeking out and learning from other regions' honest accounts of what worked at a similarly modest scale, rather than only studying the aspirational, headline success stories that set an unrealistic bar for what a first year or two of effort can reasonably achieve.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: regional organization trying to directly replicate Brainport) Can another Dutch region realistically replicate the Brainport model?
Not the full model — its foundation rests on a specific, multi-decade industrial history and anchor institution that can't be quickly recreated. But specific elements, particularly the visibility and coordination principles, are transferable at a smaller scale.

### (Scenario: organization wondering what's actually achievable without an anchor institution) What can a region without a comparable anchor institution actually do?
Focus on making existing local AI/ML/Data-adjacent employment visible to local talent, and on coordinating between education, employers and local government — neither requires an equivalent industrial history to start.

### (Scenario: organization considering a branding-first approach) Is a regional branding campaign enough to build a Brainport-like ecosystem?
Branding alone typically addresses only the visible surface of the model, not the underlying industrial depth and coordination that took decades to build. It can be part of a strategy, but not a substitute for the underlying substance.

### (Scenario: organization unsure where to start) What's a more useful starting question than "how do we become the next Brainport"?
What employers already exist locally doing AI/ML/Data-adjacent work, and how visible is that work to local talent who might otherwise leave — a narrower, more actionable question than replicating an entire ecosystem.

### (Scenario: organization wondering about timeline expectations) How long should a region expect this kind of work to take?
