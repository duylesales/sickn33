📈 Most technical career advice covers getting hired. Far less covers what happens once you're competent and the next step is unclear — and in the Dutch market that ambiguity is sharper, because the senior individual contributor track is less established here and many organisations don't have one at all.

❓ So ask one question, and ask it before accepting a role if progression matters: does a senior technical path exist separately from management, and who currently occupies it?

The second half matters more. Plenty of organisations describe a technical track in their career framework without anyone actually being on it. If nobody a level above you is a non-manager, the track is aspirational and advancement means managing people, whatever the document says.

🔄 What genuinely changes at this level, and it isn't technical depth:
· Choosing the problem becomes the work. A senior engineer solves the given problem well. Beyond that, the contribution is recognising that the requested model wouldn't help, or that the real constraint is data quality
· You're accountable for outcomes you don't personally produce, so influence runs through other people's work
· Writing becomes your main output — documents that people who weren't in the room act upon
· You own the problems nobody else will touch
· Saying no becomes a core function

🇳🇱 Three Dutch specifics worth knowing. Flat hierarchy means a competent senior person already has influence beyond their title — genuinely good, but it also means formal recognition can lag actual standing by years. Directness makes advocating an unpopular technical position cheap. Consensus culture makes moving an organisation slow, so patience beats authority.

And in public sector, healthcare and research, collective agreements cap what any technical role pays regardless of contribution. Recognition then takes other forms — autonomy, problems, training budget, a four-day week. Those are genuinely available here in a way they aren't in most markets.

💡 Fastest route to standing: solve something visible that nobody else will touch. Reliability, evaluation, a legacy system, a year-old disagreement. Unattractive problems are unclaimed problems.
