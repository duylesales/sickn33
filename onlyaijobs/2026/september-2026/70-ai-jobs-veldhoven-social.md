🔬 Veldhoven is the global centre of semiconductor lithography equipment. The machines built here define the physical limit of how small a transistor can be made — one of the few places where the constraint is fundamental physics rather than engineering effort.

🔄 And it inverts the usual framing of applied ML. In most commercial settings you have abundant measurements and the difficulty is predicting from them. Here the quantities you need are at or beyond the limit of what can be measured at all. Feature sizes are smaller than the wavelength of light used to image them. You're inferring physical structure from signals that don't directly show it.

⚗️ Which is why physics-informed methods are standard practice rather than an academic curiosity. Purely data-driven models fail unpredictably outside their training distribution — and here that failure means scrapping material worth a great deal. Calibrated uncertainty isn't a refinement; a prediction without it is often useless because the decision it feeds needs to know whether to trust it.

🚪 The thing candidates get wrong: assuming the only way in is through the dominant employer. The precision supplier network and the engineering service layer have real technical content and far less competition. Match your entry point to your background — the cluster isn't closed, it's stratified.

💡 One durable advantage: physical measurement limits don't get easier. Competence acquired here holds its value in a way that expertise in a specific software ecosystem generally doesn't.
