A regional economic development office and a job board have almost nothing in common operationally. One writes policy briefs and courts investment; the other displays listings and takes emails from employers. But both are, in practice, trying to answer the same underlying question from opposite ends: is the work that would keep someone in this region actually visible to the person deciding whether to leave it?

Most regional talent-retention efforts approach that question from the outbound side — surveys of graduates who left, exit interviews, labor-market reports published a year after the fact. That work is necessary and OnlyAIJobs does not replace it. But it answers "what happened," not "what was visible at the moment someone decided." Those are different questions, and the second one is where a job board's data structure — not its scale — becomes useful to a region rather than just to a job seeker.

## The Visibility Gap Is the Policy-Relevant Part

OnlyAIJobs exists because of a specific, named pattern: graduates from Noord-Brabant moving to the Randstad for work, not primarily because better roles exist there, but because they often don't know which companies near them are hiring. That is the platform's own founding observation, and it is worth stating precisely what kind of claim it is — not a labor-market statistic, but a visibility problem. The jobs may already exist. The awareness of them, at the moment a graduate is choosing where to build a career, often does not.

That distinction matters for anyone doing regional economic development work, because the two problems have completely different solutions. A shortage of local opportunity is fixed by attracting employers. A visibility gap is fixed by making existing opportunity legible — which costs a fraction of what an investment-attraction campaign costs, and can start with the employers a region already has.

## What Address-Level Listings Make Visible That a Labor Market Report Doesn't

A regional labor market report tells you sector composition and rough headcounts. It does not tell you what a 22-year-old graduate can actually see when they open a job search the week they're deciding whether to look locally or in Amsterdam. An address-level, distance-sorted listing does exactly that: it shows, concretely, what "close to home" contains for AI, ML and Data roles specifically, rather than as an abstraction in a policy document.

This is a narrow but concrete thing a regional partner can point candidates toward without having to build it themselves: not a claim that the region has "more jobs than people think," but a way to make the jobs that already exist checkable in under a minute.

## Where This Intersects With What Regional Organizations Already Do

**University and graduate programs.** Career services at technical universities already tell students to "look locally first." An address-level board gives that advice something concrete to point to, rather than a general encouragement that competes with a national job board's much larger marketing presence.

**Employer engagement.** Regional development organizations already maintain relationships with local employers as part of investment and retention work. Encouraging those employers to list open AI/ML/Data roles — which currently costs nothing for a first posting — is a low-effort extension of a relationship that already exists, not a new program to fund.

**Public reporting.** A region making the case to graduates, or to prospective employers, that "the jobs are here" benefits from being able to point to a visible, checkable list rather than an aggregate statistic that requires trusting a report's methodology.

## What This Is Not

This is not a claim that a job board can measure or reverse regional brain drain on its own, and it should not be presented to a board or a council as if it were. It is a narrow, concrete tool that addresses one identifiable part of the problem — visibility of existing local opportunity for AI/ML/Data roles specifically — alongside the harder, longer-term work of attracting and retaining employers that any regional strategy still has to do.

## Where to Start

If your organization already works on regional talent retention, the lowest-effort next step is not a new initiative — it's a conversation about whether pointing graduates and job seekers toward a visible, address-level list of current local AI/ML/Data vacancies fits into outreach you're already doing, whether that's a careers-office resource, a newsletter mention, or an employer-engagement conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: policy officer skeptical that a job board is relevant to retention strategy) Isn't talent retention a much bigger problem than job visibility?
Yes — retention depends on housing, wages, career growth and quality of life, and a job board addresses none of those directly. What it addresses is narrower: whether the jobs that already exist locally are visible to someone deciding whether to leave. That's one input into a much larger strategy, not a substitute for it.

### (Scenario: economic development office deciding whether this is worth engaging with) What would our organization actually need to do to make use of this?
Very little to start: pointing existing channels — a careers office, a newsletter, an employer-engagement conversation — toward a resource that already exists, rather than building or funding something new. The lowest-effort version is simply a conversation to see whether it fits what you're already doing.

### (Scenario: university career services deciding how to advise students) How is this different from telling students to "check LinkedIn" or a national job board?
National boards rank listings partly by employer advertising spend and show only a city name, not an actual commute. A student advised to "look locally" needs something concrete to check that shows real distance from home for AI/ML/Data roles specifically — otherwise "look locally first" remains a suggestion with nothing to act on.

### (Scenario: regional employer relationship manager wondering if this duplicates other channels) Does this compete with or duplicate the employer relationships our organization already manages?
No — it's additive. Encouraging an employer you already work with to post an open AI/ML/Data role costs them nothing for the first listing and takes an email. It doesn't require them to change how or where else they recruit.

### (Scenario: reader wondering what evidence underlies the "graduates leave for the Randstad" claim) What is the basis for the claim that graduates leave the region due to visibility, not lack of opportunity?
That is OnlyAIJobs' own stated founding observation, based on prior research into vacancy-seeker behavior in the region — it is presented here as the platform's founding premise, not as new data this article is generating. Regional organizations evaluating whether to engage should treat it as a hypothesis worth testing against their own local data, not as an established statistic.
