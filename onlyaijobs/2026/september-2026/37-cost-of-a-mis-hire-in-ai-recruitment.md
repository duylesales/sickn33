A wrong hire for an AI role is usually measured in the salary paid before it stopped working. That is the smallest cost. The larger costs lie in what didn't happen while that person was employed: the project that fell behind, the team that spent time on supervision without results, and the next recruitment round that has to start again from zero.

## Where the Real Costs Come From

**Delayed projects have a knock-on effect.** An AI project that depends on one specialist stalls as soon as that person turns out not to have the right skills — and other teams waiting on that project fall behind too.

**Your existing team's supervision time isn't free.** Senior colleagues who spend time onboarding and correcting a mis-hire do so at the expense of their own work. That time is real, even if it isn't recorded anywhere separately.

**A second recruitment round doesn't start at zero, but below it.** On top of the time of a new search, trust within the team in the organisation's ability to fill this role well has often been damaged.

## Why Specialist Recruitment Structurally Reduces This Risk

A broad pool of applicants doesn't filter itself for fit with a specific AI/ML/Data role — that filtering only happens after you have gone through the CVs yourself. An audience that is already specifically looking for AI/ML/Data roles doesn't reduce the chance of a mis-hire to zero, but it does reduce it structurally, simply because the applicants who come in are already more targeted.

## A Timeline of How the Costs Pile Up

**Months 1–2:** The new hire is onboarded; the first signs of a mismatch are often already visible but are attributed to a normal learning curve.

**Months 3–4:** The project the hire is working on falls behind; senior colleagues spend more time on correction and supervision than planned.

**Months 5–6:** The mismatch is explicitly acknowledged; a decision about continuing or stopping is made — often later than it could have been, because nobody wanted to name the problem out loud earlier.

**Month 7 onwards:** A new recruitment round starts, now with less trust within the team and often under greater time pressure than the first round.

| Cost item | Often included | Often forgotten |

| Salary during employment | Yes | — |
| Recruitment costs (advertising, agency) | Yes | — |

## Why Early Signals Should Be Taken More Seriously

A mismatch that is already visible after two months but only acknowledged after six costs the company four months of avoidable delay and supervision time. The reason this happens so often is not a lack of signals — it is the social awkwardness of naming a problem early with someone who has just been hired. An explicit, scheduled check-in after two months, in which these kinds of signals are discussed structurally, prevents an early-recognisable problem from simmering for six months.

## A Concrete Scenario That Makes the Creeping Costs Visible

A company hires a data scientist for a project with a deadline four months away. After six weeks, the manager notices the approach isn't working, but says nothing — it feels awkward to raise it so early. After ten weeks, the deadline is at risk, and a senior colleague steps in to salvage what can be salvaged, at the expense of their own ongoing work. After fourteen weeks, well past the deadline, the mismatch is finally acknowledged explicitly and a difficult conversation follows. Had the manager said something after six weeks, eight weeks of delay and the associated supervision time of the senior colleague would have been avoided — a real difference that can be traced entirely to postponing an awkward conversation.

## A Common Misconception About Why Early Intervention Doesn't Happen

Managers often postpone naming a mismatch because they are afraid of discouraging the new employee or feel they haven't yet allowed enough time to settle in. This misconception confuses "naming a signal early" with "giving up prematurely" — a good conversation after six weeks doesn't have to be a final judgement; it can just as well be an opportunity to adjust course before the problem has piled up into something irreversible.

## Why a Fixed Checkpoint After Six to Eight Weeks Solves This Structurally

Instead of relying on a manager's gut feeling to intervene at the right moment, a pre-scheduled, mandatory check-in after six to eight weeks works better: it normalises the conversation about progress and concerns, regardless of whether there actually is a problem, so that discussing a mismatch no longer feels like an exceptional, loaded action but a routine part of every new hire.

## How to Include These Costs in a Business Case for Better Recruitment

If you have to argue within an organisation for more time or budget for a more careful recruitment process, it is more effective to name these costs explicitly than to rely on a general feeling that "good recruitment matters." Calculate concretely: how many weeks of delay did the last mis-hire cost, how many hours of a senior colleague's time were lost to it, and how long did the second recruitment round take compared with the first? These concrete figures, even if approximate, are more convincing to a decision-maker than an abstract call for care.

## Why This Topic Is Rarely Discussed Explicitly Within Organisations

The costs of a mis-hire are rarely named out loud within an organisation, because doing so implicitly criticises the hiring decision made earlier — nobody likes to point out that a previous decision turned out to be expensive. That makes the topic uncomfortable to discuss, which means the lessons from a mis-hire are often not captured structurally for future hiring decisions, unless someone explicitly takes the trouble to have that conversation despite the discomfort.

## Dutch Employment Law Makes Early Detection Extra Important

In the Netherlands, ending an employment contract after the probation period is legally and financially more drastic than in many other countries. That makes the moment a mismatch is recognised a direct cost factor.

**The probation period is short.** A probation period must be agreed in writing and may last at most two months for a permanent contract, and at most one month for a fixed-term contract of more than six months but less than two years. No probation period is allowed for a fixed-term contract of six months or less. The check-in after six to eight weeks recommended above therefore coincides exactly with the end of a two-month probation period — not a coincidence, but a reason not to postpone that conversation.

**After the probation period, dismissal is not simple.** Dismissal for poor performance in the Netherlands in principle goes through the subdistrict court. The employer must show that the employee was told about the poor performance in good time, had sufficient opportunity to improve — often through an improvement plan — and that the poor performance is not the result of insufficient attention to training or working conditions. Without a documented file, such a dissolution has little chance of success.

**The settlement agreement as the usual way out.** In practice, many mis-hires end with a settlement agreement: termination by mutual consent, often with a payment based on the statutory transition payment and an agreement about the notice period. That payment comes on top of the costs this article already describes.

**Fixed-term contracts and the notification obligation.** For a fixed-term contract of six months or longer, the employer must inform the employee in writing at least one month before the end whether the contract will be extended. Forgetting this can lead to a payment being owed. So even with a fixed-term contract, a timely, explicit evaluation is indispensable.

## A Realistic Cost Breakdown

Because amounts vary widely per organisation, your own calculation is more meaningful than a general figure. Use this breakdown:

| Cost item | How to calculate it |
|---|---|
| **Wage costs during employment** | Gross salary plus employer contributions, holiday allowance and pension premium, times the number of months |
| **Recruitment costs** | Advertising, agency or recruiter fees, interviewers' time |
| **Onboarding** | Hardware, licences, access management, training time |
| **Supervision time** | Hours of senior colleagues times their internal hourly cost |
| **Project delay** | Missed or postponed revenue or savings from the project, per week of delay |
| **Termination costs** | Payment under a settlement agreement, legal costs, notice period paid out |
| **Second recruitment round** | All of the above recruitment costs again, usually under greater time pressure |

For AI roles, project delay is often the largest item, because AI projects depend on scarce specialist knowledge and other teams are waiting on the results.

## What Research Says About Preventing a Mis-Hire

Most mis-hires originate in selection, not onboarding. Research on the predictive value of selection methods offers clear guidance.

**Structured interviews predict better than unstructured ones.** The well-known 1998 meta-analysis by Frank Schmidt and John Hunter, and later re-analyses by Paul Sackett and colleagues, among others, show that structured interviews — with fixed questions and assessment criteria for all candidates — predict job performance better than unstructured conversations based on gut feeling.

**Work samples are valuable, provided they are realistic.** An assignment that resembles the real work, such as analysing a representative dataset or discussing an existing modelling problem, provides more information than abstract puzzles. Keep the scope reasonable: a take-home assignment of several days actually puts strong candidates off.

**Ask references targeted questions.** Ask references about concrete situations similar to the challenges of the new role, not about general impressions.

**Be careful with automated screening.** AI systems that filter applications or assess candidates are classified as high-risk under the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act**. Anyone using such tools to select faster takes on obligations around human oversight and transparency — and runs the risk that an opaque system lets exactly the wrong candidates through.

## A 30-60-90-Day Plan That Makes Mis-Hires Visible

An explicit onboarding plan makes expectations measurable and prevents a mis-hire from going unnamed for months.

- **Day 30:** access to data and systems arranged, a first analysis or exploration delivered, the team's way of working understood.
- **Days 45–55:** a formal evaluation conversation, well before the end of any probation period, with concrete observations from the supervisor and team members.
- **Day 60:** a first independent contribution to the project, such as a baseline model or a data pipeline.
- **Day 90:** a defined result delivered and discussed with stakeholders outside the team.

If the first two milestones are not met, that is the signal to adjust — with extra supervision, an adapted assignment or an honest conversation about fit.

## Key Takeaways

- Because of the short probation period and strict dismissal rules, early detection is extra important in the Netherlands.
- Schedule an evaluation well before the end of the probation period; if problems persist, build a careful improvement plan and file.
- Calculate the cost of a mis-hire yourself, including supervision time, project delay and termination costs.
- Structured interviews and realistic work samples demonstrably reduce the chance of a mis-hire.
- Automated AI screening is high-risk under the AI Act and not a quick fix for better selection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: employer who only sees salary as a cost) What does a wrong AI hire actually cost, besides the salary paid?
Delays to projects that depended on that person, supervision time of existing team members, and the time and trust a second recruitment round requires. Salary is usually the smallest of these costs.

### (Scenario: employer unsure whether specialist recruitment reduces the risk) Does a specialist platform really reduce the risk of a wrong hire?
Not to zero, but structurally — an audience already specifically looking for AI/ML/Data roles is more targeted than a general audience, which reduces the chance of a fundamental mismatch before the screening process even starts.

### (Scenario: employer who wants to hire quickly to avoid delaying a project) Isn't hiring quickly better for avoiding delay?
In the short term perhaps, but a quick wrong hire often causes more delay than a slightly longer, more careful search — plus the cost of a second round if it goes wrong.

### (Scenario: employer who underestimates the cost of supervision time) Why does my existing team's time count as a cost?
Because senior colleagues who spend time onboarding and correcting a mis-hire do so at the expense of their own work — a real cost, even if it never appears on an invoice.

### (Scenario: employer who wants to know how to reduce this risk) What can I concretely do to reduce this risk for my next AI vacancy?
Recruit through a channel where the audience is already specifically looking for AI/ML/Data roles, and write the vacancy around a concrete, current problem instead of a broad, aspirational description — both reduce the chance of a fundamental mismatch.
