An AI hiring process with a take-home assignment, a live coding round, a system-design interview, a case study presentation and a culture-fit conversation isn't unusual — and each stage individually seems reasonable to whoever proposed it. The problem is cumulative: a strong candidate evaluating three or four offers in parallel will often withdraw from the longest process, not because they weren't interested, but because someone else moved faster.

## Why Each Additional Stage Seems Reasonable in Isolation

Every stakeholder in an AI hiring process has a legitimate reason to want their own assessment — engineering wants to verify coding ability, the hiring manager wants a case study, another team wants a culture conversation. No single addition looks unreasonable when proposed. The person accountable for the total number of stages is often nobody in particular, which is exactly why the process quietly grows.

## What Actually Drives Drop-Off

**Elapsed calendar time, not just number of stages.** A five-stage process completed in ten days loses fewer candidates than a three-stage process that stretches across six weeks because of scheduling delays between rounds.

**Redundant assessment of the same skill.** A take-home assignment followed by a live coding round that tests overlapping skills reads to a candidate as inefficiency, not thoroughness, and it's a reasonable read.

**Silence between stages.** A week of no communication after a strong interview signals low priority to a candidate, regardless of whether that's true internally — and candidates weighing multiple offers act on signals, not on your actual internal urgency.

## A Practical Fix

Audit your current process for what each stage actually tests, and consolidate any two stages that test overlapping skills. Set an internal maximum elapsed time from application to offer, and communicate proactively between stages even when there's no update — silence is read as a signal, so replace it with one you control.

## Where to Start

If you're currently losing strong candidates mid-process, the highest-leverage fix is usually reducing elapsed time and redundant stages, not adding new assessment methods.

## An Audit Template for Your Current Process

For each stage in your current hiring process, write down: what specific skill or trait it tests, how it differs from what the previous stage already tested, and how many days typically pass before the candidate hears back. Doing this exercise once, honestly, for a role you're currently hiring for, usually reveals at least one redundant stage or one avoidable delay that nobody had previously flagged because no one owned the process end-to-end.

## Comparing a Bloated Process to a Tightened One

| | Common unaudited process | After a redundancy audit |
|---|---|---|
| Number of stages | 5-6, added incrementally over time | Often reducible to 3-4 without losing signal |
| Total elapsed time | 4-6 weeks, often longer with scheduling gaps | 1.5-3 weeks achievable with intent |
| Overlap between stages | Common, undetected | Explicitly checked and removed |
| Communication during gaps | Often silent | Proactive, even without news |

## Why Speed Itself Becomes a Competitive Signal to Candidates

A strong candidate evaluating multiple opportunities reads process speed and communication quality as a proxy for how the company operates more broadly — a slow, disorganized hiring process is often, fairly or not, taken as a preview of a slow, disorganized way of working. Tightening your process isn't just about winning this one candidate; it's about what the process itself signals before you've said a word about your actual culture.

## A Walkthrough: Auditing a Real Five-Stage Process

A company's current AI hiring process: application review, a take-home assignment, a live coding round, a system-design interview, and a final culture-fit conversation. Auditing each stage: the take-home and live coding round both test similar algorithmic and coding ability, with meaningful overlap — a candidate strong on one is very likely strong on the other, meaning one of these two stages is largely redundant. The system-design interview tests something genuinely distinct (architectural judgment) and should stay. The culture-fit conversation, scheduled last, often happens two weeks after the system-design interview due to calendar conflicts — an avoidable gap that costs candidates during exactly the period they're most likely to be evaluating competing offers. This audit alone suggests: drop either the take-home or the live coding round, and schedule the culture-fit conversation within days of the technical rounds rather than weeks later.

## A Misconception About What "Thorough" Actually Means

Hiring teams often equate more stages with more thoroughness, assuming each additional stage adds proportional signal. In practice, signal from additional stages diminishes quickly once the first two or three stages have covered the core competencies — a sixth stage testing something similar to the third rarely adds meaningful new information, it just adds calendar time and candidate fatigue, both of which carry real costs that a longer process doesn't offset with genuinely better hiring decisions.

## Why Fixing This Is Ultimately a Leadership, Not a Recruiting, Problem

Because a bloated process usually accumulated through many individually reasonable additions by different stakeholders over time, no single recruiter or hiring manager typically has the authority to unilaterally cut a stage that someone else insisted on adding. Solving this durably requires someone with cross-functional authority — often a hiring or engineering leader — to periodically audit and approve the process as a whole, rather than leaving each addition to accumulate indefinitely without anyone ever being responsible for the total.

## How to Communicate a Necessary Delay Without Losing the Candidate

Sometimes a genuine delay is unavoidable — a key decision-maker is on leave, a budget approval is pending. In these cases, the specific way a delay is communicated matters enormously: a proactive message explaining the specific reason for the delay and a realistic new timeline is received very differently than silence followed eventually by an apologetic update. Candidates generally understand that organizations have real constraints; what erodes their patience is not the delay itself but the uncertainty of not knowing whether they've simply been forgotten, which silence does nothing to resolve.

## Why Fixing This Process Pays Off Beyond Just This Round of Hiring

An efficient, well-communicated hiring process doesn't just help you win this specific candidate — it shapes your company's reputation among the broader pool of AI/ML/Data candidates in the Netherlands, a genuinely small and networked community where candidates often discuss their interview experiences with peers considering the same company. A reputation for a respectful, efficient process compounds as a recruiting asset over time, while a reputation for a slow, disorganized one can quietly cost you candidates in future searches who heard about a bad experience secondhand before ever applying themselves.
