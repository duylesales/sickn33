📡 Assen itself is a provincial market. What makes Drenthe worth writing about sits in the countryside around it: the Dutch radio astronomy institute, where data rates and processing problems exceed almost anything in commercial technology.

🌌 A distributed radio telescope spans many antenna stations across the Netherlands and beyond. Signals from all of them must be correlated with precise timing. The raw data rate is so large it cannot all be stored — so a substantial part of the engineering problem is deciding what to keep and processing the rest in real time as it arrives.

🔊 The problems are genuinely distinctive. Detecting extremely faint natural signals in an environment saturated with human radio transmission — broadcasting, aviation, mobile networks, electric fences, badly shielded equipment. Calibrating a distributed instrument where the ionosphere distorts signals differently by time, direction and frequency. Reconstructing images from incomplete sampling, where your choice of regularisation determines what you see.

🔄 And it transfers better than candidates assume. Inverse problems, distributed sensor calibration, streaming detection and petabyte-scale data management apply directly to medical imaging, remote sensing, geophysics and semiconductor metrology. The caveat: you have to describe your work in general problem terms, because a commercial hiring manager may not know what a calibration pipeline is or why it was hard.

💡 Practical note — engineering roles there don't need a physics degree, and the institute's software is public. Read it before interviewing and be able to discuss it. Few signals of genuine interest are stronger.

⚠️ It's small: a few dozen relevant technical roles nationally. Treat it as a specific pursuit, not a market.
