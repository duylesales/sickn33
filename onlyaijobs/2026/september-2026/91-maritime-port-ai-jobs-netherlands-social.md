⚓ Most sectors optimise within one organisation. Maritime rarely can — and that's what makes the technical work distinctive.

📦 A container arriving in Rotterdam involves a shipping line, terminal operator, customs authority, barge or rail or road carrier, freight forwarder, inland terminal and consignee. Each independent, each with its own systems and objectives, none obliged to share more than the contract requires.

So the largest inefficiencies aren't inside any one party's operation — they're between them. A truck waiting four hours at a gate. A barge sailing half empty. A container sitting on a quay because nobody knew it arrived. Coordination failures that no single organisation can fix by optimising internally.

Which produces an unusual technical shape: you build systems that must function without knowing what other parties know. Estimation under partial observability isn't a research nicety here, it's the working condition. And a globally optimal solution may disadvantage a party whose cooperation you need — so understanding who gains and loses determines whether it can be implemented at all, regardless of the numbers.

🔧 The insight modelling-focused candidates undervalue: agreeing common data definitions and exchange formats often creates more value here than better algorithms. A coordination failure caused by incompatible data cannot be fixed with a better model.

🔍 Why almost nobody competes for these roles: the vocabulary hides them completely — postings say "operations analyst", "planner", "engineer". The industry is family-owned, long-established, and communicates with its own sector rather than the technology labour market. And it looks old-fashioned from outside, though the optimisation problems at a large container terminal are as hard as anything in commercial data work.

💡 Most efficient preparation available: spend a few hours understanding how a container actually moves through a port. Domain credibility is weighted heavily here, and this industry has watched external technologists arrive with proposals that ignored operational reality.
