🏛️ Dutch public sector AI work is shaped decisively by one thing: automated decision-making in public administration caused serious, documented harm to thousands of people. Parliamentary inquiry, government resignation, a remediation programme running for years.

Understanding that history isn't optional context for a candidate. It's the single most useful thing you can bring to an interview here, because it determines which proposals are viable.

📋 What it means in practice:
· Human review must be substantive, not formal — a system whose recommendation the official always accepts is automated decision-making with a signature attached
· Explainability means explicable to the affected citizen, not interpretable to a data scientist
· Contestability is a design requirement, and the challenge must be capable of succeeding
· Disparate impact is examined whether or not a protected characteristic was an input, because proxies exist

🔄 The most useful practical insight: assistive framing beats decisive framing. A system that classifies a citizen's case faces the full weight of the governance requirements. A system that helps an official find information, summarise a file or flag something worth checking is much more acceptable. Same technology, framed differently, moves from unbuildable to valuable.

✅ What's genuinely good and under-advertised: national registers at full population coverage maintained for decades with legal authority — no commercial equivalent exists. Questions that are concrete and consequential. And you are not optimising engagement, which for some people is a real relief.

⚠️ What it costs: pace, tooling, a bounded compensation ceiling, thin senior technical tracks, and exposure of your work to political contestation. Also worth naming — risk aversion blocks beneficial systems too, because a visible failure costs far more institutionally than a missed improvement.

💡 Interview signal that works: volunteer the assistive-not-decisive framing before being asked.
