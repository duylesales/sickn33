A senior AI candidate has already read many job ads that promise a lot and say little that is concrete. That experience makes them more sceptical than a junior candidate, so they screen a text differently — not for enthusiasm, but for signals that the team knows what it is doing.

## What Senior Candidates Specifically Look for in a Job Ad

**A concrete technical problem, not a mission statement.** Seniors immediately recognise the difference between "we want to use AI to innovate" and "we have a model that does X and struggles with Y." The second signals that the team already understands the problem, which builds trust.

**Honesty about technical debt.** A senior candidate knows every company has technical debt. Not mentioning it creates distrust, not trust — it sounds as if the job ad was written by someone who doesn't know the system.

**Who actually wrote the vacancy.** A text that was clearly co-written by a technical team member, rather than by HR alone, reads differently to someone who has been in the field for years — the details reveal who the author is.

## Why Generic Language Puts Senior Candidates Off Faster Than Juniors

Junior candidates have less of a frame of reference to recognise a generic job ad as generic. Senior candidates do have that frame of reference, and often interpret vague language as a sign that the team itself doesn't know exactly what it is looking for — a red flag, not a neutral signal.

## A Checklist Before Publishing a Vacancy for Senior Candidates

**Is a concrete, current problem mentioned?** Not a mission, but a specific thing the team is trying to solve right now.

**Is technical debt or an existing limitation honestly named?** A senior candidate expects this and appreciates it when it's there.

**Has a technical team member co-written or reviewed the text?** Check this explicitly with HR if you're not sure yourself.

**Does it contain something a senior candidate wouldn't read in ten other vacancies?** If the answer is no, rewrite the text.

## Comparison Table: Signals That Do and Don't Convince Seniors

| Signal | Convinces senior candidates | Doesn't convince senior candidates |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete, current technical problem | Yes | — |
| Honesty about technical debt | Yes | — |
| Generic language ("innovative," "dynamic") | — | Yes, backfires |
| Long list of requirements without prioritisation | — | Yes, creates distrust |

## Why This Weighs More Heavily for Senior Candidates Than for Juniors

A senior candidate has often experienced several times that a job ad promised something practice didn't deliver, and is therefore quicker to interpret vague language as a warning sign rather than neutral marketing. This isn't cynicism — it is learned behaviour based on previous experience, and it means that investing in an honest, specific job ad pays back faster with this audience than with junior candidates.

## A Concrete Scenario: How a Senior Candidate Actually Reads a Vacancy

A senior data scientist with ten years of experience doesn't read a job ad linearly from top to bottom — they first scan for specific technical details and skip generic opening lines. When the text starts with "We are an innovative company looking for a passionate data scientist," there is a good chance this candidate drops out before even reaching the actual job description — not out of unwillingness, but because experience has taught them that such opening lines are rarely followed by anything substantial.

## A Common Misconception About What Seniors "Deserve" to Hear

Employers sometimes think senior candidates mainly need to be convinced with status and prestige — an impressive-sounding company, a high salary, a big name. While these factors certainly play a role, this assumption underestimates how much senior candidates specifically look for evidence of technical substance, precisely because they have often been disappointed by vacancies that promised a lot and delivered little.

## Why This Approach Also Improves the Quality of Your Interviews

A job ad written specifically and honestly doesn't just attract better candidates — it also makes the first conversation deeper and more relevant, because the candidate already has a concrete picture of the problem and can ask targeted questions, instead of starting with basic clarification questions a more specific job ad could have prevented.

## How to Use Feedback From Previous Applicants to Improve Your Text

If you have used job ads before and received little response from senior candidates, it is worth asking, where possible, candidates who did apply but eventually dropped out what initially appealed to them or held them back in the text itself. This direct feedback, however uncomfortable to ask for, is more valuable than internal guesswork about what works, and can provide concrete leads for the next version of your job ad.

## Why This Is an Ongoing Process, Not a One-Off Exercise

A job ad that works well for senior candidates today may no longer do so in a year — as the market changes and candidates develop different expectations, what is experienced as "honest and specific" shifts too. So treat writing a convincing senior job ad as something that is periodically reviewed based on current feedback, not as a template that, once written well, can remain unchanged.

## The Six Questions a Senior Candidate Wants Your Vacancy to Answer

Senior AI professionals usually decide whether to apply based on a limited number of concrete points. A vacancy that answers these questions is taken seriously; a vacancy that avoids them is skipped.

| The senior candidate's question | What to state concretely | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| **Which decisions can I make myself?** | Decision rights on architecture, tooling and priorities | "You decide on the model architecture and evaluation approach; roadmap choices you make together with the product owner." |
| **How mature is the production environment?** | Whether models are already running, how they are deployed and monitored | "Three models run in production; deployment is automated, drift monitoring is still missing." |
| **Who are my peers?** | Team size, seniority, reporting line | "You work with two data engineers and a mid-level ML engineer and report to the Head of Data." |
| **What will I earn?** | Salary range or scale, bonus, pension | "€X–€Y gross per month, excluding 8% holiday allowance; pension scheme with employer contribution." |
| **How will I grow?** | Specialist and management tracks, training budget | "Growth to lead or principal without management tasks is possible; annual conference budget." |
| **How seriously does the organisation take responsible AI?** | Governance, documentation, AI Act | "For customer-facing models we produce model documentation and a risk classification under the AI Act." |

**On salary.** For senior candidates, a missing salary indication is one of the quickest reasons to scroll on. The **EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970** will also require employers to give applicants information about the starting salary or salary range before the interview. Employers who already do so gain trust and are ahead of legislation.

**On production maturity.** Seniors are familiar with the MLOps maturity levels described by major cloud providers, from manual training and deployment to fully automated pipelines. An honest positioning ("we are somewhere between manual and automated") is more informative to them than any promise.

**On responsible AI.** Because of the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act**, experienced candidates increasingly ask how an organisation handles risk classification, documentation and human oversight. A vacancy that says nothing about this for a role affecting customers, credit, healthcare or staff is read as immature.

## Employment Conditions That Weigh More Heavily for Seniors

Senior candidates compare offers on more than the monthly salary. Mention these points in the vacancy or, at the latest, in the first conversation:

- **Total package.** Holiday allowance, any thirteenth month or bonus, pension scheme and employer contribution, training and conference budget.
- **Notice period.** Seniors often have a notice period of one or two months themselves. Flexibility on the start date is a concrete plus.
- **Non-compete clause.** A broadly worded non-compete clause puts experienced specialists off. A non-compete clause must be agreed in writing and in a fixed-term contract is only valid with a written justification of compelling business interests. Limit it to what is truly necessary, or leave it out.
- **Contract type.** A permanent contract from the start is often an important condition for seniors.
- **International candidates.** State whether you are a recognised sponsor with the **IND**, and whether you apply for the **30% ruling** for eligible candidates.
- **Work pattern.** Exact office days and the office address; seniors with a family often weigh travel time heavily.

## Red Flags Seniors Recognise Immediately

| Red flag in the vacancy | What a senior candidate reads into it |
|---|---|
| "Rockstar," "ninja" or "AI guru" | The company doesn't understand the field |
| Twenty tools and five programming languages as requirements | Nobody has decided what the role really is |
| "Competitive salary" without a range | The salary is probably lower than expected |
| "Senior" with tasks such as updating dashboards | Title inflation; the role is mid-level or junior |
| No information at all about the team | You may be the only one, without peers |
| "You'll build our AI platform from scratch" without budget or support | High expectations, few resources |
| No mention of data, infrastructure or production | Possibly nothing is running yet |

## The Selection Process Itself Is Also a Signal

For senior candidates, the assessment of your organisation starts with the selection process.

- **Let a senior counterpart conduct the first substantive interview.** A senior candidate wants to spar with someone at their level, not only with HR.
- **Replace puzzle assignments with a realistic conversation.** Discussing a real problem from your team — or a previous project of the candidate — says more to both sides than an algorithm puzzle.
- **Offer a conversation with future colleagues.** Seniors want to know who they will be working with.
- **Keep the process short.** Senior candidates often have several options at once; a six-week process loses them.

## An Opening Line Seniors Actually Read

> "Our fraud detection models have been running in production for two years, but we're seeing more and more false positives with new payment methods. We're looking for someone to redesign the evaluation and retraining approach and make the technical choices in it."

An opening like this names an existing system, a concrete problem and the role's decision-making scope — exactly the three things a senior candidate scans for in the first seconds.

## Key Takeaways

- Senior candidates look for evidence of technical substance: a concrete problem, honest technical debt and the hand of a technical team member.
- Answer six questions: decision rights, production maturity, peers, salary, growth and responsible AI.
- State a salary range; the EU Pay Transparency Directive is also making this increasingly the norm.
- Be restrained with non-compete clauses and clear about contract type, notice period, IND sponsor status and the 30% ruling.
- Avoid red flags and make the selection process itself evidence of maturity.

## Where to Start

Have a technical team member co-write the job ad, be specific about the current problem and existing technical debt, and avoid language that would fit a hundred other vacancies just as well.
