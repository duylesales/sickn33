Post a Machine Learning Engineer role on a general job board and you will get applications within hours. Look closer and a large share of them will be from candidates whose CV contains the word "Excel" more often than "model," because the board's matching logic optimises for volume, not relevance, and a generic "engineer" or "data" keyword pulls in everyone adjacent to the term.

This is not a failure of the candidates applying — it is a predictable result of how generalist boards are built. Understanding the mechanism matters more than complaining about the symptom, because it tells you what actually needs to change about where you post, not just how you write the listing.

## The Structural Problem, Not a Writing Problem

A generalist job board's business model depends on matching as many job seekers as possible to as many postings as possible, because that is what keeps both sides — employers paying for visibility and candidates browsing for free — on the platform. Relevance is a secondary objective to volume. The result is a matching algorithm that treats "AI" as a keyword to broaden reach with, not a filter to narrow it.

Combine that with pay-to-rank visibility, and a second effect compounds the first: an employer's posting is ranked partly by how much they spend, not by how well it matches the candidates viewing it. A five-person AI team with a modest budget gets outranked by a large company's generic "Software Engineer" posting that happens to also mention machine learning in the fifth bullet point — and both postings are competing for the same pool of applicants, most of whom are not looking for either role specifically.

## What This Actually Costs You

The visible cost is time: screening a pile of applications where a small fraction is relevant. The less visible cost is worse — qualified candidates who specifically want AI/ML/Data work often don't bother scanning a generalist board at all, because their experience of it is the same noise from the other direction: scrolling past hundreds of irrelevant IT roles to find the handful that match. If your posting is competing for attention with backend, DevOps, and IT support roles in the same results page, you are not just fishing in a noisy pond — the fish you actually want have often stopped fishing there themselves.

## What a Specialist Board Does Differently

**The audience has already filtered itself.** Everyone browsing a board that lists only AI, ML and Data roles is there because they specifically want one of those roles. You are not paying to reach a broad audience and hoping relevance sorts itself out — the relevance is upstream of your posting.

**Visibility isn't determined by budget.** On OnlyAIJobs, every employer's listing is shown on the same terms regardless of company size or spend. A small team with a genuinely interesting AI problem is not out-ranked by a large company's bigger recruitment budget for an unrelated role that happens to mention the same keyword.

**Location precision does real filtering work.** Listings show the exact address and the distance from a candidate's home, not just a city tag. That means the candidates who engage with your posting have already self-selected on commute distance before they click through — fewer applications from people who would decline the moment they see the office address.

## The Dutch Recruitment Channel Landscape for AI Roles

Most Dutch employers hiring for AI, machine learning or data roles use a mix of the same handful of channels. Each has a distinct audience and a distinct commercial model, and understanding both explains the quality of what comes back.

| Channel | Commercial model | Audience | Realistic role for AI hiring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LinkedIn Jobs** | Free basic posts; promoted posts paid per view or click | Very broad professional audience, strong for passive senior candidates | Good for outreach and employer visibility; noisy for inbound applications |
| **Indeed** | Free organic listings; sponsored jobs paid for extra visibility | Broadest job-seeker audience in the Netherlands | Volume channel; weak relevance filtering for specialist roles |

## Compliance Points Every Employer Hiring AI Talent Should Know

Hiring for AI roles in the Netherlands now sits at the intersection of several legal frameworks. None of this is legal advice, but a hiring manager who is unaware of these points is carrying unnecessary risk.

**The EU AI Act classifies recruitment AI as high-risk.** AI systems intended to be used for recruitment or selection — for example to filter or rank applications, or to evaluate candidates — are listed among the high-risk use cases in the regulation. Employers that deploy such tools take on obligations around human oversight, use in line with the provider's instructions, and informing affected people, as those obligations come into application. If your applicant tracking system uses automated ranking, find out exactly what it does before your screening depends on it.

**The AVG sets limits on how long you keep applicant data.** Under the GDPR (in Dutch, the *AVG*), personal data may not be kept longer than necessary. The **Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens** has long advised that applicant data should be deleted no later than four weeks after a recruitment procedure ends, unless the candidate consents to a longer retention period — commonly up to one year — so that you can contact them for future roles.

**Equal treatment rules apply to vacancy texts.** The Dutch *Algemene wet gelijke behandeling* and related legislation prohibit discrimination on grounds including age, sex, origin and religion. The **Netherlands Institute for Human Rights** (College voor de Rechten van de Mens) regularly assesses complaints about recruitment practices. Phrases such as "young and dynamic team" or an unnecessary "native Dutch speaker" requirement are common and avoidable risks.

**Pay transparency is coming.** The **EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970** requires employers to give applicants information about the initial pay or pay range before the interview stage and prohibits asking about salary history. Member states had until June 2026 to transpose it; check the current status of the Dutch implementing legislation. Specialist candidates already expect a salary indication, so treating this as a best practice now costs little.

**Sponsorship status matters for international hires.** If you intend to hire non-EU candidates under the highly skilled migrant scheme, your organisation needs recognised sponsor status with the **IND**. Stating this clearly in the vacancy saves time for both sides.

The **NVP Sollicitatiecode**, a widely used Dutch code of conduct for recruitment published by the Dutch association for HR professionals, bundles many of these good practices into a single checklist and is a sensible reference for any team formalising its process.

## A Simple Framework for Comparing Channels

The cleanest way to evaluate a specialist board is to measure it against your existing channels on the same role, over the same period, with the same criteria.

1. **Define "qualified" before posting.** Write down the three or four non-negotiable criteria for the role — for example production ML experience, a specific modelling domain and the ability to work on-site two days a week.
2. **Track the source of every application.** Use a "how did you find this vacancy?" field or distinct application links per channel.
3. **Measure qualified applicants, not total applicants.** A channel with fifty applications and two qualified candidates performs worse than one with eight applications and three qualified candidates.
4. **Record time-to-first-qualified-candidate.** For roles that have been open for weeks, speed of the first strong candidate often matters more than total volume.
5. **Compare downstream conversion.** Track how many candidates from each channel reach a second interview and an offer, and how many withdraw after learning about the location or salary.
6. **Review after 30 days.** Keep the channels that produce qualified candidates; drop the ones that only produce screening work.

This turns the choice of channels from a matter of habit into a matter of evidence — and it protects your recruitment budget from the channels that are merely the most familiar.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: hiring manager who just posted on a generalist board and got hundreds of irrelevant applications) Why do I get so many unqualified applications when I post an AI role on a general job board?
Generalist boards are built to maximise matches across their whole audience, not to filter for relevance to a specific role. Their algorithms treat a keyword like "AI" or "data" as a way to broaden reach rather than narrow it, and ranking is influenced by advertising spend rather than fit. The result is a large volume of applications from candidates who are only loosely adjacent to what you actually need.

### (Scenario: small AI team competing against enterprise recruitment budgets) Can a small company's AI vacancy actually compete with large employers on a specialist board?
Yes, structurally more so than on a generalist board. On a specialist board, listings are shown on equal terms regardless of company size or advertising spend, so a five-person team's posting is not systematically outranked by a large company paying for placement. Visibility depends on the listing being there, not on outbidding competitors.

### (Scenario: recruiter deciding whether a niche board is worth the time to try) Is it worth posting on a specialist AI job board if I already use LinkedIn and Indeed?
It is a low-cost test, not a replacement decision. The audience composition is fundamentally different — everyone browsing a specialist board is specifically looking for AI, ML or Data roles, which changes the ratio of relevant to irrelevant applications you receive. Since the first posting is free, the only cost of testing it is the time to send a link.

### (Scenario: employer unsure what information to provide when posting) What do I actually need to provide to post a vacancy?
A link to the job listing on your own website or careers page, and the city or exact address where the role is based. The address is what powers distance-from-home search for candidates, so being specific improves the quality of the match rather than just the reach.

### (Scenario: hiring manager evaluating why location precision matters for applications) Why does showing an exact address instead of just a city change the applications I get?
Because candidates can see the real commute before they apply rather than discovering it during a first interview. That pre-filtering means the people who do apply have already accepted the commute, which reduces late-stage drop-off from candidates who would have declined once they learned exactly where the office is.
