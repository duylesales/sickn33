A job ad for a "junior data scientist" often lists twelve tools, three programming languages and "an affinity with machine learning, statistics and business." Read literally, almost nobody qualifies — not even an experienced senior. That is not a contradiction; it is a list of what a team might find useful at some point, not a hard requirement for every applicant.

## What Employers Weigh More Heavily Than the Tool List

**Being able to reason about a problem, not just use a tool.** An employer wants to see that you can translate a question into an approach — what data you need, what assumptions you make, where the limits of your conclusion lie. Which specific Python library you use along the way is usually secondary.

**Communicating results.** A model nobody understands doesn't get used. Employers often test this deliberately in an interview: can you explain a technical result to someone who isn't a data scientist?

**Willingness to learn within the team.** A junior is rarely hired because they can already do everything — but because they demonstrably pick up what's needed quickly. That shows more in how you talk about previous projects than in a CV full of tools.

## How to Use This When Applying

Don't be put off by a job ad in which you "only" master six of the twelve tools listed. Focus your cover letter and interview on how you think about a problem, not on a checklist of skills — that is usually also how the employer looks at the vacancy, even if the text doesn't say so.

## Why Job Ads So Often Seem to Ask Too Much

A job ad is rarely written by one person with a sharp picture of the complete, final profile. More often it is an accumulation: the hiring manager adds what they consider important, HR adds generic language to make it attractive, and nobody removes anything. The result is a document that reflects more how a vacancy comes about internally than what a junior actually needs to be able to do on day one.

## Comparison Table: What the Text Says vs. What Counts in Practice

| What the job ad asks for | What happens in practice |
|---|---|
| "Experience with ten specific tools" | You learn most tools on the job in the first months |
| "Independently building complex models" | Junior work is usually supervised, with growing independence |
| "Excellent communication skills" | Mainly: being able to ask when something is unclear |
| "An affinity with business" | Showing interest in why a project matters |

## How to Read an Overwhelming Job Ad

Instead of ticking off the list, read a job ad looking for the two or three things that are actually repeated or described with emphasis — that is usually where the real priority lies. The rest is often padding with what "might be useful someday." A vacancy that uses the phrase "communicating with stakeholders" three times tells you more about what really matters than the twelfth Python library listed.

## A Concrete Scenario: How a Job Ad Comes About

Imagine a hiring manager who needs a junior data scientist. They write a short, reasonable list of core skills themselves. Then an HR colleague reads along and adds "an affinity with business" and "excellent communication skills," because that appears in almost every job ad. Then someone copies a section of tools from a previous, different vacancy, "just to be safe." The end result — the text an applicant actually reads — is an accumulation of three different people with three different goals, none of whom has read the whole thing again with the question: is this realistic for one junior person?

## A Common Misconception About What "Junior" Means to an Employer

Some applicants assume that "junior" in a job ad means the employer expects a low level on every point listed. In practice, it usually means the employer expects a high learning capacity on most points, combined with a few core skills you must already have. The distinction between "must already be able to" and "must be able to learn" rarely appears explicitly in the text, which means you have to make that distinction yourself when reading, or ask about it in an interview.

## Why This Realisation Changes Your Application Strategy

Once you understand that a job ad is an accumulation rather than a carefully considered, fully representative document, it changes how you deal with it: instead of checking yourself against every line, you can ask more specifically what weighs most heavily in practice — a question most hiring managers are happy to answer, precisely because they know their own job ad isn't perfect.

## How to "Dissect" a Job Ad Before You Apply

A practical exercise before applying for a junior vacancy: mark which sentences in the text describe a concrete task ("you will work on...") versus which describe a general personality trait ("you are proactive and communicative"). The first category tells you something concrete about the role; the second appears in almost every job ad in the world and says little that is distinctive. Focus your cover letter on the first category, and use concrete examples from your own experience that connect to it, instead of repeating in general terms that you are "communicative" or "proactive" — words no recruiter takes seriously any more without a concrete example.

## Why These Kinds of Job Ads Are Unlikely to Improve Soon

It is tempting to hope that companies will write better, sharper job ads over time as they gain more recruitment experience. In practice this changes little, because each new vacancy is often written by a slightly different combination of people — a new hiring manager, a new HR colleague — who reinvent the wheel without knowing the history of earlier, possibly better-written job ads. This means the skill of reading and interpreting an overwhelming job ad remains relevant throughout your career, not something that gradually becomes less necessary.

## Decoding Common Phrases in Dutch Job Ads

Many vacancies in the Netherlands are published in Dutch, even for teams that work in English. International graduates regularly encounter a set of fixed phrases. A short glossary:

| Phrase in the job ad | English meaning | What it usually implies |
|---|---|---|
| "WO-werk- en denkniveau" | University-level working and thinking | A university degree is preferred, but a demonstrably comparable level through work or an applied sciences degree is sometimes accepted |
| "HBO/WO-niveau" | Applied sciences or university level | Both education levels are welcome; the role is often applied |
| "Startersfunctie" / "0–2 jaar ervaring" | Entry-level role / 0–2 years' experience | Graduates without work experience are explicitly welcome; internships and graduation projects count |
| "Affiniteit met" | An affinity with | Interest and basic knowledge are enough; not a hard requirement |
| "Kennis van … is een pré" | Knowledge of … is a plus | Nice-to-have, not a reason not to apply |
| "Beheersing van de Nederlandse taal in woord en geschrift" | Command of Dutch, spoken and written | The working language is Dutch; often a hard requirement in government and client-facing roles |
| "Een VOG is vereist" | A Certificate of Conduct is required | You must apply to Justis for a Certificate of Conduct, common in government, healthcare and financial institutions |
| "Assessment" or "case" | Assessment or case | A test or take-home assignment follows in the selection process |

The most important lesson: distinguish hard requirements ("required," "you have") from wishes ("a plus," "affinity," "preferably"). Most long tool lists fall into the second category.

## Which Skills Really Recur in Junior Vacancies in the Netherlands

Although every vacancy is different, a core set of skills recurs in almost all junior data science roles in the Netherlands:

**Python and SQL.** Python for analysis and modelling, SQL to retrieve data independently. Anyone who doesn't master SQL depends on colleagues for every dataset in practice — which is why this is often the real hard requirement, even if it appears as one line among ten others.

**Statistical foundations.** Hypothesis tests, regression, validation and recognising overfitting. Employers test this more often with a case than with theory questions.

**Visualisation and reporting.** Many Dutch organisations use Microsoft environments, which is why Power BI is often mentioned. Understanding how to visualise a result clearly matters more than the specific tool.

**Version control.** Basic knowledge of Git is increasingly expected, even for analyst roles.

**Awareness of privacy and responsible AI.** Because of the GDPR and the **EU Artificial Intelligence Act** — which since February 2025 has required organisations to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff working with AI systems — juniors are increasingly expected to understand basic concepts such as personal data, bias and explainability.

## Where Junior Data Scientists End Up in the Netherlands

Junior roles are not evenly distributed across sectors. It helps to know which types of organisations structurally hire starters:

| Type of employer | What it means for starters |
|---|---|
| Banks and insurers | Often traineeships, strong supervision, strict governance and Certificate of Conduct requirements |
| Government and implementing agencies | Public salary scales, Dutch often required; organisations such as **Statistics Netherlands (CBS)** have large data departments |
| Consultancies and secondment agencies | Many starters, quick exposure to different clients, varying supervision |
| Retail, e-commerce and logistics | Applied work on forecasting and customer data, often in smaller teams |
| Industry and high-tech | Sensor data and quality control, often with collective labour agreement conditions |
| Start-ups and scale-ups | Broad responsibility, little structure, fast learning |

## The Selection Process: What to Expect

A typical Dutch selection process for a junior data scientist consists of a CV screening, a first interview (often with the hiring manager), a technical case or take-home assignment, and a second interview with the team. Many organisations follow the **NVP Recruitment Code**, which calls for a transparent procedure and careful handling of your data, among other things.

A few practical points:

- **Take-home assignments** should be proportionate to the role. Ask in advance how much time is expected.
- **Employment conditions** include, besides salary, holiday allowance (at least 8% of gross salary), pension, travel allowance and often a training budget. Compare offers on the total package.
- **Probation periods** are legally limited: at most one month for a fixed-term contract of up to two years and at most two months for a permanent contract, and only if agreed in writing.
- **International graduates** can benefit from a lower salary threshold for highly skilled migrants who start work shortly after their studies or orientation year; check the current conditions with the **IND**.

## Key Takeaways

- Long tool lists are usually wishes, not hard requirements; watch the difference between "required" and "a plus."
- Python, SQL, statistical foundations, visualisation and Git form the real core of junior data science vacancies.
- Knowledge of the GDPR and AI literacy are increasingly expected.
- Banks, government, consultancies, retail, industry and start-ups each offer a different kind of starter position.
- Prepare for a case, compare the total employment package and know the rules on probation periods.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: student who doesn't meet all the required tools) Do I have to meet all the tools in a junior vacancy before I apply?
No — such a list usually describes what a team might find useful at some point, not a hard requirement per applicant. Apply if you recognise the core of the requested profile, even without mastering every tool listed.

### (Scenario: student unsure what really counts in an interview) What weighs more in a job interview: tools or way of thinking?
Usually way of thinking — how you translate a question into an approach, what assumptions you make and how you explain a result. Specific tools are often easier to learn than that way of thinking.

### (Scenario: student afraid of not knowing enough) Am I expected to know everything already as a junior?
No, a junior is usually hired on demonstrable learning capacity, not on completeness of knowledge. That shows more in how you talk about previous projects than in a perfect match with the job ad.

### (Scenario: student who doesn't know how to show communication skills) How do I show that I can communicate well about technical results?
Practise explaining a project to someone without a technical background, and use that kind of explanation in your cover letter too. Employers often test this deliberately in an interview.

### (Scenario: student unsure whether to apply) Should I refrain from applying if the job ad is overwhelmingly long?
Not necessarily — a long, broad list is often a sign that the vacancy hasn't been sharply written yet, not that the bar for a junior is extremely high. Apply if the core of the profile fits you.
