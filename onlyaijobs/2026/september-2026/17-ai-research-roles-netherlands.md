Research roles are the smallest category on almost any Dutch AI job board, and that scarcity is easy to misread as absence. In reality, research-adjacent employers — university groups, research institutes, and the handful of corporate labs operating in the Netherlands — hire less frequently and less publicly than product companies, not because the work doesn't exist, but because the hiring model is different.

## Why Research Roles Behave Differently in the Market

Product-company hiring is driven by roadmap and headcount planning: a team needs another engineer this quarter, so it posts a role. Research hiring is often driven by grant cycles, specific project funding, or a group's need for a specific specialization — conditions that don't map onto a predictable quarterly posting rhythm. A research group may not hire for a year, then post two highly specific roles in the same month.

**Specificity over generality.** A product ML role often lists broad requirements ("Python, TensorFlow or PyTorch, 3+ years"). A research role frequently names a sub-field explicitly — a specific class of models, a specific application domain — because the group needs someone who can contribute to a particular line of work, not a generalist.

**Publication and network-driven discovery.** Many research hires happen through academic networks, conference contacts, or direct outreach before a role is ever posted publicly. A posting that does appear on a general job board is often a formality alongside a candidate who is already known to the group.

## What This Means for a Job Search

If you're specifically looking for research work, treat scarcity on a job board as a signal to widen your search method, not your ambition — check institutional pages directly, and treat a specialist board's research-category listings as a supplement to, not a replacement for, staying connected to the relevant academic and professional networks.

## Where to Start

Filter by the research category on a specialist board to catch postings as they appear, but don't rely on it exclusively — for AI research specifically, direct outreach and network visibility still matter more than they do for product engineering roles.

## The Entity Landscape of Dutch AI Research

**University research groups** remain the largest single source of AI research roles, typically tied to specific professors' grant portfolios rather than a department-wide hiring plan — which means the actual opening you want might depend on one specific grant being renewed, not on the university's general health.

**Corporate research labs**, where they exist inside larger Dutch or multinational companies operating in the Netherlands, tend to be small, tightly scoped, and hire rarely but for very specific expertise — a corporate lab posting for "Research Scientist" is almost never a generalist role.

**Independent and semi-public research institutes** sit between the two, often funded by a mix of government and industry money, with hiring cycles tied to specific funded programs rather than either academic terms or product roadmaps.

## Comparing Research Hiring to Product Hiring, Structurally

| | Research-adjacent hiring | Product-company hiring |
|---|---|---|
| What triggers a posting | Grant award or specific project funding | Roadmap decision, headcount approval |
| How far in advance you can predict it | Hard — tied to funding cycles | Easier — tied to fiscal quarters |
| How specific the requirements are | Very — often a named sub-field | Usually broader |
| How much of the hiring happens off-board | A lot — network and conference-driven | Less — most roles reach a board eventually |

## A Practical Search Strategy for This Market

Because research hiring doesn't follow a predictable calendar the way product hiring roughly does, the single most effective thing a candidate can do is build standing visibility rather than searching only when actively looking. Following specific research groups' publication activity, attending relevant Dutch academic or industry events even when not job-hunting, and maintaining light contact with people in the field all increase the odds of hearing about an opening before — or instead of — it ever reaching a public board. This is slower and less satisfying than a keyword search, but it's the actual mechanism by which most research hiring happens in this country.

## A Walkthrough: The Life Cycle of a Grant-Funded Research Position

A typical research position begins roughly a year before you'd ever see it advertised: a professor or research lead drafts a grant proposal specifying a research direction and, often, a rough headcount plan tied to that funding. The proposal goes through review, which can take six months to a year depending on the funding body. Once awarded, the group has a specific, often narrow window to hire — sometimes just a few months — before the funding clock starts running against project deliverables. This explains why research postings can seem to appear suddenly and demand oddly specific qualifications: the group isn't being arbitrarily picky, they're hiring against a grant proposal's specific promises, written a year earlier, that they now have limited time to fulfill.

## A Misconception About What Determines Whether You're a Good Fit

Candidates sometimes read a highly specific research posting and assume that not matching every listed sub-specialization disqualifies them entirely. In practice, because these postings are often written against grant language rather than genuinely fixed requirements, there's frequently more flexibility than the posting suggests — particularly if a candidate can demonstrate strong foundational skills and genuine enthusiasm for the research direction, even without the exact named sub-specialization. This is worth testing with a direct inquiry rather than self-selecting out based on the literal text alone.

## What This Means for How You Should Plan a Multi-Year Research Career

Because research hiring follows funding cycles rather than a predictable calendar, building a research career in the Netherlands rewards patience and diversified relationship-building over a narrow, single-target job search. Maintaining light contact with several research groups whose work interests you, rather than fixating on one specific lab's current opening, increases the odds that when a grant is eventually funded and a position opens, you're already a known quantity to the people making the hiring decision — often more valuable than the strongest cold application to a posting that's already been informally decided before it's ever published.

## Building Visibility Without an Existing Academic Pedigree

Candidates without a traditional academic pipeline into research — for instance, industry practitioners who've developed a genuine research interest later in their career — sometimes assume this path is closed to them without a PhD or an existing publication record. In practice, visible, high-quality independent work (a well-documented technical blog exploring a specific research question, contributions to open-source projects connected to a research area, active and substantive participation in relevant online research communities) can build the same kind of recognition that a traditional academic pipeline provides, just through a different, less credentialed route. Research groups evaluating potential collaborators or hires increasingly care more about demonstrated ability to engage seriously with a specific research question than about the formal pedigree of how that ability was developed, particularly for applied research positions that value practical engineering skill alongside theoretical understanding.

## What Long-Term Financial Planning Looks Like in This Career Path

Because research funding cycles are inherently less predictable than a product company's roadmap-driven hiring, professionals building a career in this space benefit from financial planning that explicitly accounts for potential gaps between grant-funded positions — maintaining a larger financial buffer than might be typical advice for someone in a more stable product-company career track, and being realistic about the possibility of a multi-month gap between one grant-funded position ending and the next beginning. This isn't a reason to avoid this career path, but it is a practical planning consideration that's rarely discussed explicitly alongside the more commonly cited advice about building academic networks and staying visible within the relevant research community.

### (Scenario: candidate discouraged by how few research listings they see) Why are there so few AI research job postings compared to product ML roles?
Research hiring is often tied to grant cycles and specific project funding rather than predictable quarterly headcount planning, so postings appear less frequently and less evenly over time. Scarcity reflects hiring rhythm, not absence of work.

### (Scenario: candidate applying broadly to research postings) Why do research job postings often require such a specific sub-specialization?
Because a research group typically needs someone who can contribute to a particular existing line of work, not a generalist who could plausibly do many kinds of ML. Specificity is a feature of how these teams hire, not an unusually narrow one-off requirement.

### (Scenario: candidate wondering why some roles never appear on job boards) Do all AI research roles in the Netherlands get posted publicly?
No — many are filled through academic networks, conference contacts, or direct outreach before or instead of a public posting. A public listing is sometimes a formality alongside a candidate already known to the group.

### (Scenario: candidate deciding how to search for this kind of role) How should I search for AI research roles if job boards show so few?
Check institutional and university pages directly in addition to job boards, and treat a specialist board's research-category filter as a supplement to staying visible in relevant academic and professional networks, not a replacement for it.

### (Scenario: candidate wondering if the scarcity means low demand) Does a small number of listed research roles mean weak long-term demand for AI research talent in the Netherlands?
Not necessarily — it reflects how research groups hire, which is driven by funding cycles and specific project needs rather than steady headcount growth. It's a different rhythm, not automatically a smaller total opportunity over time.
