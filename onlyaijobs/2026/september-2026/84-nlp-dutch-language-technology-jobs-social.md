🇳🇱 NLP work in the Netherlands has one defining characteristic: the language isn't English. Dutch has ~25 million speakers, so available text, annotated datasets and international research attention are all a fraction of what exists for English. That constraint shapes nearly every role.

⚠️ What it means concretely: multilingual models include Dutch but their Dutch capability is weaker — and unevenly so. General text may be fine while specialised domain language is poor. An English benchmark result tells you nothing about Dutch behaviour. Annotation requires Dutch speakers, a much smaller and more expensive labour pool. And standard benchmarks are limited, so you often build your own evaluation set. That's part of the job, not preliminary work.

🔀 Also: code-mixing is the norm, not an edge case. Dutch professional text routinely embeds English technical vocabulary. Any system assuming monolingual input fails on real material.

💡 The counterintuitive part — this constraint is an advantage. Working in English means competing with an enormous international talent pool on problems well-resourced organisations have already attacked. Working in Dutch means approaches are less explored, datasets often don't exist yet, and far fewer people can do the work competently. And the skills transfer to most of the world's languages, which are also low-resource.

🗣️ On the language question, answered honestly: you need Dutch to evaluate output on Dutch text — no amount of metric watching substitutes for reading a sentence and judging whether the output is good. You don't need it for infrastructure and platform work. And reading comprehension sufficient to evaluate is a much lower bar than fluency, achievable in months.

🎯 Highest-return skill in this field: building evaluation sets deliberately. Most people do it carelessly — convenient sampling, hard cases underrepresented, annotator agreement unchecked. When you can't rely on benchmarks, your conclusions are only as good as the set you built.
