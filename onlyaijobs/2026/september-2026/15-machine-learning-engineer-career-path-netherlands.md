A "Senior Machine Learning Engineer" title at a five-person startup and the same title at a large enterprise can describe genuinely different jobs — different scope, different ownership, different expectations of what "senior" means in practice. Title inflation is real, and it means the title on a job posting is a weaker signal than most candidates treat it as.

## What Actually Separates Levels, Independent of Title

**Scope of ownership.** A junior engineer typically owns a well-defined piece of a pipeline. A senior engineer owns the tradeoffs: which architecture, which failure modes to design against, when a simpler model beats a more sophisticated one. That shift — from executing a defined task to deciding what the task should be — is the real marker, and it doesn't always track with years of experience.

**Production accountability.** Whether a role includes on-call responsibility, whether a model's failure is your problem at 2am or someone else's, changes the day-to-day reality of a job far more than the title does. This is worth asking about directly in an interview rather than inferring from the title.

**Breadth vs. depth.** Smaller Dutch companies often need a "senior" engineer who can do data engineering, modeling and deployment in the same week. Larger, more specialized teams separate these into distinct roles. Neither is objectively better — they select for different working styles.

## A Practical Way to Evaluate a Posting

Instead of anchoring on the title, look at three things in the job description: what decisions the role is expected to make independently, whether production incidents are explicitly part of the role, and how many other specialists the role would work alongside. Those three answers tell you more about actual seniority than the word before "Engineer."

## Where to Start

If you're evaluating your next move by title alone, you're comparing labels that different companies define differently. Look at scope, accountability and team structure instead, and treat the title as a starting point for questions rather than an answer.

## A Framework for Reading Any ML Engineer Posting

Rather than relying on the title, it helps to score a posting against four concrete axes before deciding how "senior" it really is.

**Ownership axis.** Does the posting describe a defined task ("implement feature X") or an open problem ("figure out how we should handle Y")? The second is a stronger seniority signal than any years-of-experience number.

**Accountability axis.** Is on-call, incident response or production monitoring mentioned at all? Its absence in a "senior" posting is itself informative — either the team has a dedicated platform function, or nobody has thought about it yet.

**Breadth axis.** Does the role span data engineering, modeling and deployment, or is it scoped to one of the three? Neither answer is wrong, but they demand different skill profiles.

**Collaboration axis.** How many other specialists does the posting mention the role working alongside? A role that never mentions a team is either a true solo-generalist position or a posting written without much thought about how the work actually gets done.

## Comparing Three Common Levels in Practice

| | Junior | Mid-level | Senior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical ownership | Executes a defined task | Owns a module or pipeline | Owns architecture-level tradeoffs |
| On-call expectation | Rare | Sometimes, with support | Often, expected to lead incident response |
| Independent decisions | Few, closely reviewed | Some, reviewed at milestones | Most, reviewed after the fact |
| Mentoring expectation | None | Occasional, informal | Explicit, sometimes formal |

## Why Dutch Employers in Particular Vary So Much Here

The Netherlands' AI hiring market spans everything from five-person startups to enterprise teams inside insurers and logistics companies, and title conventions haven't converged the way they have in a more mature, single-dominant-employer market. This is actually good news for candidates willing to read past the title: it means a "Medior ML Engineer" posting at a smaller, less title-inflated company can carry more real scope than a "Senior" posting at a company that inflates titles to compete for attention — the frameworks above are how you tell the difference before accepting an offer, not after.

## A Walkthrough: How the Same Title Plays Out at Three Different Companies

Picture three postings for "ML Engineer" landing in the same week. At a five-person startup, the role means owning everything from data collection through deployment, with the founder as your only technical sounding board — you'll make more independent architectural decisions in month one than a peer at a larger company might make in a year, simply because there's no one else to make them. At a fifty-person scale-up, the role sits within a small but real team, with a senior engineer to review your decisions and an existing platform to build on top of rather than from scratch — more support, less blank-page ownership. At a large enterprise, the role is likely one of several ML Engineers on a team with defined specializations, where your actual scope might be narrower than either of the other two, but with far more infrastructure, mentorship and process supporting you. None of these is a "better" version of the title — they're three different jobs that happen to share a name.

## A Misconception About What "Senior" Actually Signals

Candidates sometimes assume a "Senior" title guarantees broader scope, higher pay, and more autonomy uniformly. In a market with as much title inflation as this one, "Senior" sometimes signals nothing more than "we wanted to seem more attractive to candidates" or "this is our second ML hire, so relatively speaking, they're senior to the first." Treating the word "Senior" as a reliable, portable credential — the way it might function in a more standardized profession — leads to real disappointment when the actual scope of the role doesn't match the title's connotation.

## Why Building Your Own Career Framework Beats Chasing Titles

Rather than optimizing your job search around acquiring progressively more senior-sounding titles, a more durable career strategy is to track your own growth against the four axes discussed above — ownership, accountability, breadth, and collaboration — regardless of what any given employer calls the role. An engineer who has genuinely grown along these axes over several years will be recognized as senior by any competent hiring manager during a real conversation, even if their most recent title was, on paper, something more modest. The title is a proxy; the actual axes are the substance, and building your career around the substance rather than the proxy protects you from both underselling yourself and overestimating your own readiness for a role that superficially matches an inflated title elsewhere.

## How to Negotiate Around Title Ambiguity When You Have an Offer

When an offer arrives with a title that doesn't clearly reflect the scope you were promised verbally during interviews, it's worth raising this explicitly before signing rather than assuming the written offer is a formality that doesn't matter. Ask specifically whether the title can be adjusted to reflect the actual scope discussed, or, if the company's internal title conventions are fixed, ask instead for the specific responsibilities and reporting structure to be documented in writing as part of the offer itself. This written documentation becomes valuable later — both as a reference point during your own performance reviews, and as a concrete artifact you can point to if the role's actual scope drifts from what was originally promised, a pattern discussed in more detail elsewhere on this site regarding role and career satisfaction over time.

## Why This Title Confusion Is Unlikely to Resolve Through Individual Effort Alone

It's tempting to think that if enough individual candidates push back on inflated or ambiguous titles, the market will eventually self-correct. In practice, title conventions are set at the company level based on internal competitive pressures — a company competing for talent against employers with inflated titles feels pressure to inflate its own titles to remain competitive in how the role reads to a candidate scanning multiple postings quickly. This is a collective-action problem that no single candidate's negotiation can solve, which is exactly why the practical response has to be personal and ongoing (evaluating each role by its actual substance) rather than waiting for the broader market to standardize on your behalf. Treat every future job search, for the remainder of your career, as requiring this same substance-over-title evaluation, rather than assuming the problem will have resolved itself by your next move.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two offers with the same title) Why do two "Senior ML Engineer" roles look so different in practice?
Because the title is set by each company independently, without a shared industry standard. What separates levels in practice is scope of ownership, production accountability, and whether the role is broad (data-to-deployment) or narrow (modeling only) — none of which the title guarantees.

### (Scenario: candidate deciding whether to accept on-call responsibility) Does seniority always come with on-call responsibility?
Not always, but it's common at smaller teams without a dedicated platform function. It's worth asking directly rather than assuming, since it materially changes the day-to-day reality of the role.

### (Scenario: candidate choosing between a startup and an enterprise role) Is a broader role at a small company "better" experience than a narrower role at a large one?
Neither is inherently better — a broad role builds versatility, a narrow one builds depth. The right choice depends on what kind of expertise you're trying to build next, not on which sounds more impressive.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure how to evaluate a posting) What should I actually look for in a job description to judge real seniority?
What decisions the role makes independently, whether production incidents are part of the job, and how many other specialists you'd work alongside — these tell you more than the title.

### (Scenario: candidate applying across many Dutch companies) Why does title inflation happen more at some companies than others?
Smaller or newer companies sometimes use inflated titles to compete for attention against larger employers with stronger brand recognition. It's not universal, but it's common enough that the title alone shouldn't be treated as a reliable signal.
