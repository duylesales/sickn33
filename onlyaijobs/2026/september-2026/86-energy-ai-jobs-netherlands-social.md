⚡ The Dutch electricity system is in an active operational crisis, and it's the best explanation for why energy has become one of the more attractive sectors for technical work here.

🔌 Connection capacity across large areas is fully allocated. Industrial sites cannot be built. Housing cannot be connected. A company wanting to electrify its process to cut emissions sometimes can't. These aren't deferred inconveniences — they're blocked investments with identifiable costs. And physical reinforcement takes years.

📊 Which produces an unusually short causal chain: a congestion forecast that's better by a meaningful margin lets a network operator offer conditional capacity where it previously had to refuse. Very little analytical work has a path to economic outcome that direct.

📈 And forecasting here is genuinely harder than elsewhere, because historical patterns are actively breaking. Electrification of heating and transport changes load shapes. Distributed solar changes the daily profile. Heat pumps make demand temperature-sensitive in ways the historical record doesn't capture. You're forecasting a system whose behaviour changes while you model it.

🧩 One thing candidates misjudge: congestion management is as much mechanism design as optimisation. You're coordinating independent parties with their own objectives — and non-discriminatory access is a legal obligation, so solutions that would be efficient but unfair are simply unavailable regardless of their numbers.

🚪 Entry without energy experience is realistic. Forecasting, constrained optimisation, anomaly detection and stochastic decision-making all transfer. Employers expect to teach the physics and market structure — but they screen hard for whether you're curious about the physical system or treating grid data as an abstract time series. A few hours of reading materially changes how you interview.

🛡️ Unusually secure sector, too: the work follows from a physical constraint and legislated obligations, not a product strategy. Corollary — teams are often stretched, so ask about size relative to scope.
