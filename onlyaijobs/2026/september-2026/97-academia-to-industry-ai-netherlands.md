The Netherlands produces far more research-trained people than its academic system can absorb, and the transition into industry is consequently well-trodden. It is also routinely handled badly, in both directions: researchers undersell the things that genuinely distinguish them and oversell the things that do not, while some employers screen out excellent candidates for lacking experience they could acquire in weeks.

This guide is about closing that gap from your side.

## What Genuinely Transfers and Is Genuinely Scarce

Being specific matters, because "research skills" as a phrase means nothing to a hiring manager.

**Designing an investigation that can answer a question.** Most people in industry have never been trained to do this. They test what is convenient, conclude from insufficient evidence, and do not notice. A researcher who can say what evidence would distinguish two hypotheses, and design the work to obtain it, is doing something scarce.

**Knowing when a result is not real.** Multiple comparisons, selection effects, insufficient power, confounding, and the difference between a finding and an artefact. The sector analyses in this series describe these problems in semiconductor yield work, clinical prediction, drug discovery and marketing measurement. Researchers trained to be sceptical of their own results are valuable precisely where the cost of being wrong is high.

**Quantifying uncertainty honestly.** Producing a calibrated statement about what you do and do not know, rather than a point estimate with unwarranted confidence. Several sectors — semiconductor metrology, flood risk, energy forecasting, medical imaging — treat this as a requirement rather than a refinement.

**Reading and using literature.** Finding out whether a problem has been solved, and whether the solution actually applies to your situation. Many industrial teams reinvent things unnecessarily because nobody is comfortable in the literature.

**Working for a long time on something hard without external structure.** A doctorate is largely an exercise in sustained self-directed work through periods when nothing is going well. That resilience is real and it is rarer than it should be.

**Writing an argument.** As the finance, public sector and consultancy analyses in this series all note, the written document frequently determines the outcome of technical work. Researchers have been trained in this for years.

## What Does Not Transfer and What Actively Hurts

Equally direct, because these are the reasons transitions fail.

**Novelty is not a value in industry.** A published contribution must be new. An industrial solution must work, be maintainable, and be understood by colleagues. Choosing a sophisticated approach where a simple one suffices is a negative signal, not evidence of capability. This is the single most common mistake.

**Perfectionism becomes an impediment.** Academic work is judged when finished. Industrial work is judged partly on whether it arrived in time to be useful. A good answer now frequently beats a better answer in three months, and learning to tell which situation you are in is the central adjustment.

**Solo working habits do not survive.** Research is often substantially individual. Industrial work is not, and code that only you can maintain is a liability regardless of its quality.

**Comfort with ambiguity about the question.** In research you usually own the question. In industry the question is often poorly specified, changes, or turns out to be the wrong question. Researchers who need the problem defined before starting struggle; those who can help define it are valuable.

**Status signals do not carry over.** Where you published, who supervised you, and the prestige of your institution matter far less than researchers expect. Nobody in an industrial interview will be impressed by a venue name they do not recognise.

**Treating engineering as beneath you is fatal.** If you regard writing solid, tested, maintainable code as a lesser activity, it will be visible within a week and it will end your credibility. The engineers around you have skills you do not have, and recognising that is the fastest route to being accepted.

## How to Translate Your Record

Concrete reframing, because this is where most candidates lose ground.

**Lead with the problem, not the method.** Not "I worked on variational inference for hierarchical models" but "I built a method to estimate effects where data was sparse at the group level, which let us draw conclusions from a much smaller sample." The second is the same work described in terms of what it achieved.

**Quantify where you can.** How much data, how many experiments, how long, what the outcome was, who used it. Researchers are trained to be modest about impact and it reads as having none.

**Foreground the engineering you did.** Almost every research project involves substantial software, data pipelines and infrastructure. You built it and you probably do not mention it. Mention it.

**Describe collaborations accurately.** Supervising students, coordinating with other groups, working with domain experts in another field — these are the teamwork and stakeholder-management experiences employers ask about, and researchers routinely fail to recognise them as such.

**Name failures and what you learned.** Industrial interviews ask about this, and a research career contains many honest examples. A candidate who can describe an approach that did not work and what they concluded is more credible than one presenting an unbroken record.

**Do not apologise for your background.** A surprising number of researchers open by explaining that they lack industry experience. It frames the conversation as a deficit. State what you can do.

## The Interview Is Testing Something Different Than You Expect

Researchers frequently prepare for the wrong thing, so it is worth describing what industrial technical interviews are actually assessing.

**Whether you can scope a problem.** A common format presents a vague business situation and watches how you narrow it. Strong candidates ask what decision the work informs, what data exists, what accuracy would be sufficient, and what the cost of being wrong is. Weak candidates start proposing methods. Researchers tend to do the latter because in academia the question usually arrives already specified.

**Whether you choose proportionate solutions.** If a question can be answered with a well-designed query and a simple model, that is the correct answer. Candidates who reach for something elaborate are demonstrating that they optimise for interest rather than for the problem.

**Whether you can be wrong gracefully.** Interviewers often push back to see what happens. A researcher used to defending a thesis may over-defend. The behaviour that reads well is engaging with the objection, conceding what is valid, and being clear about what you would need to know to resolve it.

**Whether you can communicate with a non-specialist.** Expect at least one interviewer without a technical background. Their assessment carries real weight, and a candidate who cannot explain their work to them is a hiring risk regardless of technical strength.

**Coding, at a level lower than researchers fear.** Industrial coding interviews usually test whether you write clear, working, reasonably structured code — not competitive programming. Researchers who write code daily are generally fine, and those who panic about this typically underestimate themselves. The genuine gap is usually in testing, version control discipline and code review practice, all of which are learnable quickly and worth acquiring before you start.

## What the First Six Months Actually Feel Like

Useful to know in advance, because the experience follows a predictable pattern and people who expect it handle it better.

**Weeks one to four: disorientation.** You will not know where the data is, who to ask, why things are done as they are, or what your actual job is. This is normal and is not evidence you made a mistake. The most useful thing you can do is map the organisation — who knows what, who decides what — rather than trying to produce output.

**Months two and three: the competence dip.** You will feel less capable than you were in research, because you are. Your accumulated knowledge is partly inapplicable and your new environment rewards skills you have not developed. Almost everyone experiences this and almost nobody mentions it.

**Months three to five: the first real contribution.** Usually something smaller than you expected, often infrastructural or analytical rather than modelling, and often valued more than you anticipated. This is where confidence returns.

**Month six onward: reassessment.** By now you can judge whether the work suits you, whether the organisation is good, and whether the sector was what you expected. This is the right moment to decide about staying, and a much better basis for that decision than anything you knew when you accepted.

Two things help throughout. Find one person who will answer stupid questions without making you feel stupid, early, and use them. And write down what confuses you in the first month, because you will lose access to that perspective within weeks and it is exactly what makes you useful for improving onboarding later.

## Which Sectors Value Research Training Most

This series has covered enough ground to answer specifically, and the answer is more useful than a general encouragement.

**Highest value.** Semiconductor metrology and equipment development, where physics and inverse problems are the work. Drug discovery and materials informatics, where the data is small and expensive and experimental design is central. Medical imaging and clinical research, where validation standards are the constraint. Official statistics, where methodology is the product. Radio astronomy and instrumentation, where the problems are research problems.

**Moderate value.** Energy, water and infrastructure, where physical modelling matters but engineering pragmatism matters more. Finance, where methodological rigour is valued but conventions are prescriptive. Automotive and aerospace, where validation is central but the pace is industrial.

**Lower value, and honest about it.** Retail, e-commerce and general consumer technology. The problems are well-understood, the premium is on delivery speed and breadth, and a doctorate is not obviously an advantage over three years of production experience. Many researchers enter here because it is accessible, and some thrive, but it is the sector where your training is least differentiating.

The practical implication is to target the sectors where your specific training is the scarce input rather than an incidental credential.

## The Applied Research Institute Route Is Underused

One option deserves separate mention because it suits researchers particularly well and is frequently overlooked in the binary framing of academia versus industry.

The Netherlands has a substantial applied research sector — organisations working on real problems for external partners, with methodological standards closer to academia than to commercial practice. Several appear throughout this series: the applied research organisation working across defence, mobility, energy and health; the water and subsurface institute; the aerospace institute; the agricultural research organisation; the radio astronomy institute.

For someone leaving academia, these have specific advantages.

**The methodological culture is familiar.** Rigour is expected, results must be defensible, and documentation is taken seriously. You are not asked to abandon the standards you trained under.

**Publication is usually possible.** Most publish on the unclassified, non-proprietary portion of their work, which means you need not give up academic visibility entirely.

**Problems come from outside.** Unlike academia, the questions are posed by partners who need answers, which supplies the relevance that many researchers find missing in purely curiosity-driven work.

**The transition is smaller.** You move from one research environment to another, rather than from research to product delivery. That makes it a gentler first step for people uncertain whether they want industry at all.

The trade-offs are real. Compensation is bounded, often by collective agreement. Fixed-term contracts tied to project funding are common. Pace is slower than commercial work and the bureaucracy is comparable to a university. And the senior technical ceiling exists but is not high.

The honest positioning is that applied research institutes are an excellent second step and a slightly risky terminal destination. Many people use them to build applied credibility and a portfolio of externally validated work, then move into industry from a much stronger position than they would have had directly from academia. Others stay for a career and are satisfied. Both are reasonable; the mistake is drifting there without deciding which you are doing.

## Key Takeaways

- What transfers and is scarce: designing investigations, recognising when a result is not real, honest uncertainty quantification, using literature, sustained hard work, and writing arguments.
- What hurts: valuing novelty over sufficiency, perfectionism, solo working habits, needing the question pre-defined, expecting academic status to carry, and treating engineering as beneath you.
- Choosing a sophisticated method where a simple one suffices is the single most common and most damaging mistake.
- Translate your record by leading with the problem rather than the method, quantifying impact, and foregrounding the engineering you almost certainly did but do not mention.
- Do not open by apologising for lacking industry experience; it frames the conversation as a deficit.
- Target sectors where research training is the scarce input — semiconductor, drug discovery, materials, medical imaging, official statistics — rather than the most accessible ones.

## Where to Start

Rewrite your CV so that every entry leads with a problem and an outcome rather than a method, and add the engineering work you have been omitting. Then target two or three sectors where your specific training is scarce rather than applying broadly, because broad applications from research backgrounds are filtered out by people who cannot tell what you would contribute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: researcher unsure what to emphasise) What actually distinguishes a research background?
The ability to design an investigation that can answer a question, and to recognise when a result is an artefact rather than a finding. Most people in industry have never been trained in either, and the sectors where being wrong is expensive value both highly.

### (Scenario: researcher whose interviews go badly) What is the most common mistake?
Choosing a sophisticated approach where a simple one suffices. In research novelty is required; in industry a solution must work and be maintainable, so unnecessary complexity reads as poor judgement rather than capability.

### (Scenario: researcher writing applications) How should I describe my work?
Lead with the problem and the outcome rather than the method, quantify what you can, and include the software and data infrastructure you built — which almost every research project involves and which candidates routinely omit.

### (Scenario: researcher choosing a sector) Where is my training worth most?
Semiconductor metrology, drug discovery, materials informatics, medical imaging, official statistics and instrumentation, where small expensive data, experimental design and validation standards are central. Retail and consumer technology value it least.

### (Scenario: researcher worried about a skills gap) Should I explain my lack of industry experience?
No. Opening with that frames the conversation as a deficit. State what you can do. Most of the gap concerns tooling and conventions that you would acquire in weeks, and employers who screen for those rather than for capability are not the ones to persuade.
