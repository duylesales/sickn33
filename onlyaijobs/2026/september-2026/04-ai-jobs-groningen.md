Every "best cities for AI talent in the Netherlands" list runs through the same four names in the same order, and Groningen is never one of them. That is not a verdict on the work available there. It is a side effect of how those lists get built — usually from employer-brand spend and conference sponsorships, both of which are Randstad-concentrated by default, regardless of where the interesting engineering actually happens.

Groningen has a genuine, self-sustaining reason to have an AI labor market: a research university that has been producing data science and AI graduates for longer than most Dutch scale-ups have existed, sitting in a region undergoing one of the more consequential data-and-infrastructure transitions in the country. Neither of those facts shows up in a generic "top tech cities" listicle, because neither one is a marketing budget.

## Why the City Gets Skipped, Structurally

Job search behavior in the Netherlands is disproportionately anchored to Amsterdam, Eindhoven and Utrecht, and generalist boards reinforce that anchor rather than correct it. A candidate types "AI jobs" without a city, gets a results page weighted toward wherever employers spend the most on visibility, and treats that page as a map of the country's opportunity. It isn't. It is a map of advertising budgets, and Groningen employers — many of them research institutes, midsize energy and agri-tech firms, and university spinouts — are structurally under-represented in that kind of budget competition.

The consequence is a market that is real but quiet: fewer listings than Amsterdam in absolute terms, but also far less competition per listing, because the candidate pool searching specifically for "Groningen" is a fraction of the pool searching "Amsterdam."

## What Kind of AI Work Happens in Groningen

**Energy-transition data.** The province has spent the past decade managing the shift away from its legacy gas industry, which created sustained demand for the unglamorous but well-paid work of modeling grid load, forecasting renewable output, and managing infrastructure risk at a regional scale. This is applied statistics and time-series modeling with real operational stakes, not a research exercise.

**University-adjacent and spinout work.** A research university produces founders and research staff who stay in the region rather than relocate, which means a steady trickle of small, technically serious AI teams that never appear on a national "top employer" list because they have never needed to advertise beyond their own network.

**Agriculture and logistics data.** The wider Groningen and Drenthe region has a substantial agri-food and logistics base, both of which have quietly become data-heavy — yield prediction, cold-chain optimization, route planning — without ever being described using the word "AI" in a press release.

None of this is visible to someone searching "machine learning engineer" nationally, because none of these employers are competing for that keyword. They are competing for people who already live in or near Groningen and search locally.

| Housing cost relative to salary | Increasingly strained | Materially lower |

The honest reading of this table is not "Groningen is better." It is that the two markets select for different priorities, and a candidate who only ever sees the Randstad column has not actually made a choice — they have only seen one option.

## Why "Fewer Listings" Doesn't Mean "Fewer Opportunities Relative to You"

The number that matters to an individual candidate is not total listings in a city. It is listings divided by realistic competition for each one. A market with a hundred listings and ten thousand applicants per posting can be structurally worse for an individual than a market with fifteen listings and forty applicants per posting — especially once a candidate accounts for the fact that they can only work one job, not a market share of jobs.

This is the same logic OnlyAIJobs applies at the level of a single street address rather than a city: raw volume is a weak proxy for whether a specific opportunity is good for a specific person. Distance, competition, and fit matter more than a city's position on a national ranking that was never measuring what you actually need to know.

## Where to Start

Do not use a national "best cities" ranking as your search strategy — it is measuring marketing spend, not opportunity density relative to competition. Look directly at what is currently listed in and around Groningen, filter by category, and treat the smaller number of results as a signal about competition, not about the quality of the work.

The market is real but structurally under-advertised rather than small. Groningen combines a research university that has produced AI and data science graduates for years with a regional economy going through an active energy-transition data problem, plus a quiet agri-food and logistics sector doing serious applied modeling. None of this competes for national advertising budgets, so it looks smaller on generalist boards than it actually is.

### (Scenario: Candidate comparing a Groningen offer to an Amsterdam offer) Why would I consider a market with fewer total listings?
Because the number that matters is listings relative to competition, not listings in absolute terms. A smaller regional pool of applicants competing for each Groningen vacancy can produce better realistic odds than a larger Randstad market with proportionally far more applicants per posting.

These are less visible nationally because the employers involved rarely need to advertise outside their own region.
