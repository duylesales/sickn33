Ask five Dutch companies to define "AI Engineer" and you'll get five different answers — one means someone who fine-tunes and deploys models, another means someone who builds the surrounding infrastructure, a third means a data scientist whose manager wanted a more modern-sounding title. This isn't sloppiness; it's a predictable side effect of a field that professionalized faster than its job titles could standardize.

## Why Standardization Hasn't Happened

Established engineering disciplines have had decades for titles to converge around shared meaning — a "Backend Engineer" means roughly the same thing everywhere. AI as a mainstream hiring category is young enough that companies are still independently inventing their own internal taxonomy, often based on whichever team happened to own the first AI hire, rather than an industry-wide convention.

## The Overlaps That Cause the Most Confusion

**AI Engineer vs. Machine Learning Engineer.** Often synonymous, sometimes distinguished by whether the role emphasizes integrating existing models (AI Engineer) versus training and fine-tuning custom ones (ML Engineer) — but not consistently.

**Data Scientist vs. Applied Scientist.** "Applied Scientist" sometimes signals a more research-adjacent data science role, but plenty of companies use it interchangeably with "Data Scientist" simply because it sounds different.

**ML Engineer vs. MLOps Engineer.** Sometimes the same role split by which department wrote the posting — see the separate discussion of MLOps titling for more on this specific overlap.

## A Practical Way to Search Past This

Search by category and by the actual responsibilities listed, not by matching a single expected title. Keep a mental checklist of the questions that actually matter to you — modeling versus infrastructure emphasis, research versus applied focus — and evaluate each posting against that checklist regardless of what it calls itself.

## Where to Start

Stop filtering exclusively by title, and start reading the first three bullet points of a posting's responsibilities before deciding whether it matches what you're looking for.

## A Practical Checklist to Use Instead of a Title

Since the title itself carries limited information, build a short personal checklist and apply it to every posting regardless of what it's called:

1. **Modeling emphasis**: does the role build models from scratch, integrate existing ones, or a mix?
2. **Infrastructure emphasis**: is deployment and monitoring part of the role, or handled by someone else?
3. **Research emphasis**: is there an expectation of novel methodology, or purely applied work?
4. **Seniority signals**: independent decision-making, on-call, mentoring — present or absent?
5. **Team context**: how many specialists does the posting mention working alongside this role?

## Mapping Common Titles to What They Usually — But Not Always — Mean

| Title | Usually leans toward | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| AI Engineer | Integration, orchestration, foundation models | Sometimes identical to ML Engineer |
| Machine Learning Engineer | Training, fine-tuning, deployment | Scope varies hugely by company size |
| Data Scientist | Analysis, modeling, experimentation | Can include heavy engineering at smaller companies |
| Applied Scientist | Research-adjacent, applied | Sometimes just a fancier Data Scientist title |
| MLOps Engineer | Deployment, monitoring, infrastructure | Overlaps heavily with Platform/DevOps titles |

## Why This Confusion Is Actually Temporary, Historically Speaking

Every engineering discipline goes through a period where titles are inconsistent before converging on shared meaning — "Full Stack Developer," "DevOps Engineer" and "Product Manager" all went through similar phases before their current, fairly stable definitions emerged. AI hiring is simply earlier in that process. This is a useful thing to keep in mind: the checklist approach above isn't a permanent workaround, it's a temporary necessity while the industry's vocabulary catches up to how fast the underlying roles have proliferated.

## A Walkthrough: Applying the Checklist to a Real Posting

Take a posting titled "AI Engineer" at a mid-sized Dutch fintech. Running it through the checklist: modeling emphasis — the description mentions "integrating large language models into our customer service flow," suggesting integration over from-scratch training. Infrastructure emphasis — no mention of deployment or monitoring, suggesting this sits with a separate team. Research emphasis — none apparent, purely applied. Seniority signals — mentions "you'll make architectural decisions independently," suggesting real autonomy despite no "Senior" in the title. Team context — mentions working alongside two backend engineers and a product manager. After five minutes with this checklist, you have a much clearer picture of the actual role than the two-word title ever provided, and you can walk into a first conversation with specific, informed questions rather than generic ones.

## A Misconception About Why This Confusion Persists

It's tempting to assume this inconsistency reflects carelessness on employers' part — that companies simply haven't bothered to standardize their titles. In most cases, the more accurate explanation is that no external standard yet exists for them to converge toward; each company is making a locally reasonable decision without the benefit of an established convention, which is a different problem from carelessness and one that won't be solved by any individual employer trying harder.

## Why Your Own Vocabulary Matters More Than the Industry's

While waiting for industry-wide title conventions to stabilize — which may take years — the most practical response is to develop your own precise vocabulary for describing what you actually do and want to do, independent of any title. Being able to say "I build and deploy production models, with moderate infrastructure ownership, minimal research component" is more useful in a job search than waiting for the industry to agree on what to call that combination of responsibilities.

## How Recruiters and Hiring Managers Can Reduce This Confusion Themselves

Employers frustrated by receiving mismatched applications due to title ambiguity have more control over this than they might assume. Rather than relying on a single, ambiguous title, a posting can explicitly state the checklist-style dimensions discussed here directly in the job description itself — modeling emphasis, infrastructure involvement, research component, seniority signals, team context — turning what's currently an implicit, candidate-side research burden into explicit, employer-provided clarity. Companies that do this consistently tend to receive better-matched applications precisely because they've done the disambiguation work themselves rather than leaving candidates to guess, a theme connected to the broader discussion elsewhere on this site about writing job descriptions that filter rather than merely attract.

## Why This Confusion Creates a Genuine Opportunity for Careful Candidates

Precisely because most candidates apply based on title-matching rather than doing the deeper analysis described in this article, candidates willing to invest the extra effort to read past titles and ask clarifying questions gain a real, if modest, competitive advantage — they're more likely to end up in roles that actually fit them, reducing early attrition risk, and they demonstrate a level of diligence in the interview process itself that reflects well on them as a candidate. The confusion in the market isn't just an obstacle to navigate around; for a sufficiently careful candidate, it's also a filter that works in their favor against less diligent competition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate confused by overlapping titles) What's the actual difference between "AI Engineer" and "Machine Learning Engineer"?
There isn't a consistent industry-wide difference. Some companies distinguish integrating existing models (AI Engineer) from training custom ones (ML Engineer), but usage varies enough that you have to check the actual responsibilities each time.

### (Scenario: candidate wondering why this inconsistency exists) Why hasn't the AI industry standardized its job titles yet?
Because AI as a mainstream hiring category is still young relative to established engineering disciplines, and companies are largely inventing their own internal taxonomy independently rather than converging on shared conventions.

### (Scenario: candidate deciding how to filter search results) Should I search using multiple title variants to avoid missing relevant roles?
Yes, but more importantly, filter by category and read the actual responsibilities rather than relying on any single title term, since the same underlying role can appear under several different names.

### (Scenario: candidate applying to a role with an unfamiliar title) Should I skip a posting if the title doesn't match what I'm looking for?
Not without reading the responsibilities first — a title like "Applied Scientist" or "Data Scientist" might describe exactly the modeling-heavy or infrastructure-heavy work you want, despite the unfamiliar label.

### (Scenario: candidate trying to build a consistent evaluation method) What's a reliable way to compare postings with different titles?
Build a short checklist of what actually matters to you — modeling vs. infrastructure emphasis, research vs. applied focus, seniority signals — and evaluate every posting against that checklist regardless of its title.
