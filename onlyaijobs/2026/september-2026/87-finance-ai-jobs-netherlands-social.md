🏦 The thing that surprises technology people most about finance isn't the modelling difficulty. It's that a model here is a governed artefact rather than a piece of code — it has an owner, a documented purpose, independent validation, specified monitoring, and often an explainability requirement.

📋 Which means: you can't repurpose a model informally because it's available. Documentation is a deliverable, not a README. And someone whose job is not to build models will assess your data, assumptions, methodology and limitations — adversarially, by design.

That last one is the most common cultural failure. Engineers who experience independent validation as an attack struggle. Engineers who use it to find their own weaknesses improve faster than they would alone.

🔍 A technically distinctive area worth knowing: fraud detection has the opposite cost asymmetry from most classification work. False positives are numerous and expensive in investigation effort; false negatives carry regulatory consequences. And the adversary adapts as your detection improves.

🇳🇱 Why the Dutch market is stronger than its size suggests: a pension system exceptionally large relative to population, creating long-horizon modelling needs few countries match. Mature competitive insurance with actuarial functions actively integrating modern methods. Advanced payments infrastructure. A real Amsterdam fintech layer. And English as the working language nearly throughout — one of the more accessible sectors for international candidates.

⚖️ The honest trade-off: high compensation, abundant well-structured data and genuinely transferable governance experience — against slower iteration and constrained methodological freedom.

🚪 If you're entering from a general ML background, target fraud or claims document processing rather than credit risk. Skills transfer more directly, governance is lighter while still teaching you the practice, and claims processing is under-served relative to its value.
