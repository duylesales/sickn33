Every guide to working in the Netherlands mentions that Dutch people are direct. Almost none explains what that means concretely, which leaves international arrivals to work it out through a series of small misunderstandings. This guide is about the specifics, with emphasis on the misreadings that cause actual problems in technical teams.

## What Directness Is and Is Not

**It is about content, not tone.** A Dutch colleague will tell you your approach has a flaw. They will say it plainly, in a meeting, possibly to a room. They are describing the approach, not evaluating you, and the distinction is real even though it takes time to feel real.

**It is not aggression.** Raised voices, personal remarks and hostility are as unwelcome here as anywhere. What differs is the absence of cushioning language around substantive disagreement.

**It is not permission to be rude.** International arrivals sometimes over-correct into bluntness that Dutch colleagues themselves would consider unpleasant. The style is direct about substance while remaining perfectly civil about people.

**It applies upward.** A junior engineer can tell a director that a plan will not work, and this is expected rather than brave. If you come from a culture where that is career-limiting, this is the single largest adjustment and also the largest gain.

**Silence does not mean agreement.** In some cultures a lack of objection signals assent. Here it more often means people have not yet formed a view or are waiting for someone to ask them. Asking directly whether anyone disagrees is normal and produces answers.

## The Misreadings That Cause Real Problems

Six specific patterns, each of which regularly damages a working relationship.

**Reading criticism of your work as criticism of you.** This is the most common and the most costly. Someone saying your model will not work is not saying you are not good. Responding defensively signals that substantive discussion with you is expensive, and colleagues will route around you.

**Assuming warmth means friendship and its absence means dislike.** Dutch professional relationships are typically cordial and somewhat reserved at first, and this is not a judgement. Relationships develop slowly and then last, as the relocation analysis in this series notes.

**Hearing a proposal as a decision.** In consensus cultures, a manager stating a direction may be opening a discussion rather than closing one. If you disagree and say nothing because you heard a decision, you have lost the moment where your objection was wanted.

**Treating consultation as indecision.** A Dutch team may discuss something for longer than you expect before acting. This is not weakness; the resulting decision is usually better supported and more durable. But it does mean you cannot expect to be given a mandate — you have to build agreement, as the progression analysis describes.

**Interpreting respected working hours as low ambition.** Colleagues leaving at five are not less committed. Long hours signal poor planning here rather than dedication, and an international arrival who visibly overworks is often read as struggling rather than as diligent.

**Assuming part-time means less serious.** A four-day week is normal at every level including senior and including management. Someone working Monday to Thursday is not on a reduced career track.

## How Decisions Actually Get Made

Consensus culture is mentioned constantly and explained rarely. The mechanics matter, because they determine how you get anything done.

**Agreement is sought, not manufactured.** A Dutch team genuinely wants the people affected by a decision to have been heard. This is not a formality to be endured before someone decides anyway; if you raise a serious objection, the direction may actually change.

**Everyone affected expects to be asked.** Deciding something that touches another team without consulting them is a genuine breach, and it will damage your standing more than a technically poor decision would. The cultural requirement is consultation rather than permission.

**The discussion is where influence happens.** Once a direction has been settled through discussion, reopening it is much harder than shaping it beforehand. Arrivals who stay quiet through the deliberation and then object afterwards are working against the grain.

**Written proposals move things faster than meetings.** A short document circulated in advance lets people form views before the conversation, which is how consensus cultures reach decisions efficiently. This is the practical reason writing matters so much in Dutch organisations, as the progression analysis notes.

**Small disagreements are resolved directly, not escalated.** Going to your manager about a technical dispute with a colleague, before you have had the conversation yourself, reads badly. Speak to the person.

**Once agreed, execution is expected.** The flip side of extensive consultation is that continued objection after a decision is settled is not welcome. You had your opportunity; the decision now has collective ownership.

The practical summary for someone from a directive culture is that you trade speed for durability. It takes longer to start and much less often falls apart later.

## Small Practical Things Nobody Mentions

A collection of specifics that cause disproportionate confusion in the first months.

**Birthdays matter and you are expected to bring something.** Your own birthday, not other people's — you provide the cake. Missing this is a small but genuine social misstep.

**Lunch is short, communal and modest.** Often half an hour, frequently at desks or a shared table, usually bread. It is a social occasion rather than a meal, and joining it is how you get to know colleagues.

**Coffee is a ritual and an invitation.** "Shall we get coffee?" frequently means "I want to discuss something informally." It is the closest Dutch equivalent to an unscheduled conversation and accepting is worthwhile.

**Agenda-setting is polite, not presumptuous.** Sending an agenda for a meeting you requested is appreciated rather than officious.

**Cycling is transport, not recreation.** Colleagues arrive by bike in all weather. Meetings are scheduled assuming this.

**Titles are rarely used.** First names apply to everyone including senior leadership. Using formal address can read as distancing rather than respectful.

**Directness about money is normal.** Splitting a bill precisely, asking what something cost, discussing salary bands openly. None of this is impolite here.

**Sickness is taken at face value.** If you are ill you stay home, and nobody expects a performance of resilience. Working while visibly unwell is regarded as inconsiderate rather than dedicated.

These are minor individually. Collectively they are most of what makes the first few months feel unfamiliar, and knowing them shortens that period considerably.

## How Feedback Actually Works

Worth a section because the mechanics differ enough to cause confusion.

**It arrives unprompted and in the moment.** You will not necessarily wait for a review cycle. If something is wrong, you will hear about it when it is noticed, which is more useful and initially more startling.

**It is specific rather than general.** Dutch feedback tends to be about a particular thing you did rather than a global assessment of your abilities, which makes it actionable.

**Positive feedback is sparser than in some cultures.** Absence of praise does not mean dissatisfaction. If something is fine, it may simply not be mentioned. International arrivals from cultures with more effusive affirmation sometimes conclude they are performing badly when they are not.

**You are expected to give it too.** Including to your manager, and including when it is unwelcome. A colleague who never disagrees is regarded as either disengaged or dishonest, neither of which is good.

**Asking for it works.** "What would you have done differently?" is a normal question and will be answered honestly. This is one of the most efficient tools available for learning quickly in a new environment.

## Meetings, Planning and Time

**Punctuality is genuine.** A meeting at ten begins at ten. Arriving at five past is noticed.

**Agendas are real and diaries are protected.** Meetings are scheduled in advance, including informal ones. Dropping in on a colleague unannounced is less welcome than in more spontaneous cultures, and asking for fifteen minutes tomorrow is the normal approach.

**Social planning follows the same logic.** An invitation for drinks in three weeks is not coldness; it is how people organise their lives. Spontaneous socialising happens but is not the default.

**Holiday is taken and respected.** People disappear for two or three weeks in summer and are genuinely unavailable. Planning around this is normal, and contacting someone on holiday about anything short of an emergency is poor form.

**The end of the day is the end of the day.** Messages sent in the evening are frequently not read until morning, and this is fine rather than a service failure.

## The Language Situation in Practice

Covered in the relocation analysis, but worth restating from inside a team.

**Colleagues switch to English readily and immediately.** This is kindly meant and is also the main reason international staff do not learn Dutch: you never have to.

**Meetings may start in Dutch and switch when someone asks.** Asking is entirely acceptable and nobody minds. Not asking and understanding nothing is worse for everyone.

**Informal conversation defaults to Dutch.** Lunch, corridors, the kitchen. This is where you miss context, relationships and the informal information that determines how organisations actually work, and it is the strongest practical argument for learning the language even when your work does not require it.

**Written internal material is often Dutch.** Policies, administrative communication, some documentation. Reading ability, as the relocation analysis argues, delivers more practical benefit per unit of effort than conversational fluency.

## What Dutch Colleagues Find Difficult About International Teams

Almost all writing on this subject addresses adaptation in one direction. It is worth knowing what the friction looks like from the other side, both because it is fair and because understanding it makes you easier to work with.

**Indirect communication is genuinely hard to read.** A Dutch colleague hearing "that's interesting, I'll think about it" may take it literally, when it meant "no". They are not being obtuse; they lack the decoding convention. If you mean no, saying no is kinder than leaving them to guess.

**Deference is confusing and sometimes counterproductive.** An engineer who will not contradict a senior person is, to Dutch colleagues, withholding information the team needs. Silence in a meeting may be read as having nothing to contribute rather than as respect.

**Overpromising creates real problems.** In cultures where declining a request is difficult, people sometimes agree to timelines they cannot meet. Dutch colleagues take stated commitments literally and plan around them, so an optimistic estimate causes more damage here than in environments where padding is assumed.

**Constant availability is unwelcome rather than impressive.** A colleague who answers messages at eleven at night creates pressure on others and signals poor boundaries. Several Dutch managers regard it as a warning sign about workload rather than as commitment.

**Hierarchy imported into a flat structure confuses people.** Treating a manager as an authority to be deferred to, rather than a colleague with a coordinating role, produces awkwardness in both directions.

The reciprocal point is worth stating plainly: Dutch colleagues frequently underestimate how much effort international staff are expending on cultural translation on top of their actual work, and how isolating the default to Dutch in informal settings is. Good teams notice this. If yours does not, saying so directly is both appropriate and likely to be received well, because directness cuts in every direction.

## What This Culture Is Genuinely Good For

Worth saying, because guides of this kind can read as a list of things to tolerate.

Technical disagreement is cheap. You can argue that a design is wrong without political consequence, which is an enormous advantage for engineering quality and something people from more hierarchical environments find genuinely liberating.

Work and life are actually separable, and the boundary is respected institutionally rather than only aspirationally.

Competence carries more weight than seniority in technical discussions, so a well-argued position from a junior person gets taken seriously.

Part-time and flexible arrangements are available without career penalty, which materially changes what a technical career can accommodate.

And the directness, once you adjust, means you generally know where you stand. Ambiguity about whether your work is valued is rarer here than in cultures where criticism is muted.

## Key Takeaways

- Directness is about content rather than tone: substantive disagreement without cushioning language, while remaining civil about people.
- It applies upward, which is the largest adjustment for arrivals from hierarchical cultures and the largest gain.
- The most costly misreading is hearing criticism of your work as criticism of you; defensive responses make colleagues route around you.
- A manager stating a direction may be opening a discussion rather than closing it, so silence loses you the moment your objection was wanted.
- Long hours read as poor planning rather than commitment, and a four-day week is normal at every level including senior.
- Informal conversation defaults to Dutch, which is where you lose context and relationships — the strongest practical argument for learning the language.

## Where to Start

For your first month, ask more questions than feels comfortable and treat every piece of critical feedback as information rather than judgement. Then ask one colleague directly what you are getting wrong culturally — you will get an honest answer, which is the point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: arrival who felt attacked in a meeting) Was that hostility?
Almost certainly not. Dutch directness describes the approach rather than evaluating the person, and substantive disagreement arrives without cushioning language. Responding defensively is the response that causes actual damage, because colleagues learn that discussion with you is expensive.

### (Scenario: arrival who said nothing and regretted it) Why did my objection come too late?
Because a manager stating a direction is often opening a discussion rather than closing one. If you hear a decision where a proposal was intended, you miss the moment your disagreement was actively wanted.

### (Scenario: arrival worried about appearing uncommitted) Should I work longer hours to show dedication?
No. Long hours read as poor planning here rather than commitment, and visible overwork is often interpreted as struggling. Leaving at a normal time is normal at every level.

### (Scenario: arrival receiving little praise) Does absence of positive feedback mean I am doing badly?
Usually not. Positive feedback is sparser than in some cultures, and if something is fine it may simply not be mentioned. If you are unsure, ask directly — you will get an honest answer.

### (Scenario: arrival whose colleagues all speak English) Do I still need Dutch?
For the work, often not. For everything informal — lunch, corridors, the conversations where context and relationships form — yes, and that is where the practical case for learning it is strongest even when your job does not require it.
