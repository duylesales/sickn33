A job posting is written before you ever speak to anyone at the company, which makes it one of the few honest, unfiltered artifacts you get in a hiring process — nobody has coached the wording for your specific interview yet. Read carefully, it tells you a surprising amount about how prepared a team actually is for the work it's hiring for.

## What a Posting Reveals, If You Read Past the Requirements List

**Whether the problem is defined or aspirational.** A posting that describes a specific model in production today, with a specific known limitation the hire is expected to address, reflects a team with real, lived experience of the problem. A posting that describes only ambitions — "leverage AI to transform our business" — without a concrete current state often means you'd be the first person defining what that even means in practice.

**Whether the role has already failed once.** A very specific, unusually detailed list of requirements sometimes indicates a team that hired the wrong person for this role before and is now overcorrecting. That's not necessarily a red flag — it can mean a company that learned from a mistake — but it's worth asking about directly.

**Whether engineering and business stakeholders wrote it together.** A posting heavy on business outcomes with no mention of technical stack, data infrastructure or evaluation approach suggests the requisition may not have had real technical input yet, which often means the role's scope is still being figured out — sometimes an opportunity to shape it, sometimes a sign of disorganization.

## Where to Start

Read a posting twice: once for what it's asking for, once for what it reveals about how the team thinks about the problem. Bring specific questions from that second reading into your first conversation rather than waiting to discover the answers after accepting an offer.

## A Reading Checklist for Any AI Job Posting

**Concreteness check.** Does the posting name a specific system, dataset or metric, or does it stay entirely at the level of ambition? Concreteness suggests something already exists; abstraction suggests you might be building the foundation yourself.

**Authorship check.** Does the language sound like it was written by someone who understands the technical work (specific tools, specific data challenges) or purely by HR (generic requirements, no technical texture)? This predicts how much technical support you'll have day to day.

**History check.** Is the list of requirements unusually long or specific in a way that suggests a lesson learned from a past hiring mistake? Not disqualifying, but worth a direct question.

**Structure check.** Does the posting mention who you'd report to and how big the team is? Absence of this information isn't necessarily bad, but it's worth clarifying before an offer stage.

## Comparing a "High-Signal" and "Low-Signal" Posting Side by Side

| Element | High-signal posting | Low-signal posting |
|---|---|---|
| Problem description | Names a specific system and its current limitation | Describes only an ambition ("leverage AI to transform X") |
| Technical detail | Mentions specific tools, data volumes, architecture choices | Generic buzzwords only |
| Team context | States team size and reporting line | Silent on both |
| Requirements list | Focused, tied to the actual problem described | Long, generic, seemingly copy-pasted |

## Why This Reading Skill Compounds Over a Job Search

The more postings you read this way, the faster you get at spotting the pattern — and the faster you can screen out roles that would waste your time in an interview process, while prioritizing conversations with teams that clearly know what they're building. This is a skill worth developing deliberately early in a search, not something to figure out only after a few disappointing interview processes.

## A Walkthrough: Reading Two Real-Sounding Postings Side by Side

Posting A: "We're building the future of AI-powered customer engagement. Join our passionate team of innovators." Posting B: "Our recommendation model currently has a 12% cold-start problem for new users in their first week — you'd own improving this, working with our two backend engineers." Posting A tells you nothing concrete; it could describe almost any company at any stage. Posting B tells you the team has already shipped something, has identified a specific, measurable problem, and knows roughly who you'd work with. Even without knowing anything else about either company, posting B alone justifies more optimism about organizational clarity than posting A does — the specificity itself is the signal, independent of how exciting either company's mission statement sounds.

## A Misconception About Vague Postings Always Being a Red Flag

It would be a mistake to conclude that every vague posting indicates a bad opportunity — some genuinely excellent, well-funded teams write poor job postings simply because whoever drafted it wasn't given much time or guidance, not because the underlying opportunity is weak. The reading skill described here is about calibrating your questions and expectations for the interview, not about screening out every vague-sounding posting before you've had a chance to ask.

## How This Skill Changes What You Ask For in a First Conversation

Once you've identified a posting as low-signal on concreteness, your first conversation should explicitly seek the missing information rather than assuming either the best or the worst: ask directly about the specific current system, if any, that the role would work on, and treat the quality and specificity of the answer — not just its content — as data about how mature the underlying organization actually is.

## Applying This Same Lens to the Interview Process Itself, Not Just the Posting

The reading skill described in this article doesn't stop being useful once you move past the job posting into actual interviews — the same signals of concreteness versus abstraction, technical authorship versus generic HR language, apply to how interviewers themselves talk about the role. An interviewer who can speak fluently and specifically about a current technical challenge is giving you the same positive signal a concrete job posting gives; an interviewer who can only repeat the same generic language from the posting itself, without adding any new specific detail, suggests the posting's vagueness reflects genuine organizational uncertainty rather than just a poorly drafted document.

## Why This Skill Becomes More, Not Less, Valuable as You Gain Seniority

Junior candidates often have less latitude to be selective about which roles they pursue, making this evaluation skill feel like a luxury they can't fully act on yet. As you gain seniority and options, the ability to quickly and accurately triage which opportunities are worth deeper investigation becomes increasingly valuable — senior candidates typically field more inbound opportunities than they have time to seriously pursue, and the fast, reliable filtering this reading skill provides becomes a genuine time-management asset, not just a way to avoid one bad interview process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate comparing two vague postings) What does it mean if a job posting describes only ambitions with no concrete current state?
It often means the team is at the very early stages of figuring out its AI strategy, and you might be the first person defining what the role actually looks like in practice. That's not automatically bad, but it's a different job than joining an established team with a working system.

### (Scenario: candidate seeing an unusually detailed requirements list) Why would a job posting have an oddly specific, long list of requirements?
Sometimes this reflects a team that hired the wrong fit for this role previously and is overcorrecting. It's worth asking directly what prompted the specificity rather than assuming it's simply thoroughness.

### (Scenario: candidate noticing a posting is all business language, no technical detail) What does it mean if a posting has no mention of the actual tech stack or data infrastructure?
It may suggest the requisition was written without much technical input yet, which can mean the role's scope is still being defined — potentially an opportunity to shape it, potentially a sign the hiring plan isn't fully thought through.

### (Scenario: candidate wanting a systematic approach) How should I read a job posting to extract this kind of signal?
Read it twice — once for the stated requirements, and once specifically for what it reveals about whether the problem is concretely defined, whether technical stakeholders were involved, and whether the specificity suggests a past hiring mismatch.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure whether to bring this up in an interview) Should I ask about these signals directly in an interview?
Yes — questions like "what's the current state of the system this role would work on" or "what prompted this specific list of requirements" are reasonable and often reveal more than the posting itself.
