Most AI job descriptions are optimised, intentionally or not, to attract as many applicants as possible: broad requirements, aspirational language, a generic list of tools. That approach makes sense on a platform charging per view, but it works against you once your actual constraint is screening time, not application volume.

## What to Include That Actively Filters, Not Just Attracts

**A specific current problem, not a mission statement.** "You'll help improve our recommendation system's cold-start performance for new users" tells a candidate exactly what to expect and lets the wrong-fit candidates self-select out before applying. "Help us leverage AI to drive innovation" filters nothing.

**The actual data and infrastructure reality, including its rough edges.** Naming the real state of your data — messy, well-documented, siloed across systems — attracts candidates who specifically enjoy that kind of problem and repels those who wanted a green field, before either side wastes time in an interview discovering the mismatch.

**A real distinction between required and aspirational skills.** A list where everything is phrased as a requirement filters out qualified candidates who don't check every box, even though most listed skills were never truly non-negotiable. Separating "must have" from "nice to have" explicitly does real filtering work.

## What to Leave Out

Generic language about "passion for innovation" or "fast-paced environment" filters nothing — every company uses it, so it carries no information a candidate can act on. Space spent on it is space not spent on the specifics that would actually help the right candidate recognise themselves in the posting.

## Where to Start

Rewrite your next AI job posting around one specific, current problem the hire would work on in their first month, and separate genuine requirements from nice-to-haves explicitly. Fewer, better-matched applications save more time than a larger unfiltered pool ever will.

## A Before-and-After Comparison of the Same Role

| Element | Attracts-everyone version | Filters-for-fit version |
|---|---|---|
| Opening line | "We're looking for a passionate AI Engineer to join our innovative team" | "You'll spend your first month improving our recommendation system's cold-start performance for new users" |
| Requirements | Long list, everything marked required | Short list, explicitly split into must-have and nice-to-have |
| Data description | Not mentioned, or described as "clean and modern" | Honest: "our event data is well-logged but our user profile data has gaps we haven't fully mapped yet" |
| Team context | Absent | "You'd work alongside two backend engineers and report to our Head of Data" |

## A Simple Three-Question Test Before Publishing a Posting

**Would a strong candidate reading this know, specifically, what they'd work on in week one?** If not, add the specific problem.

**Does the requirements list distinguish what's actually non-negotiable from what would be nice?** If everything is marked required, some of it probably isn't.

**Could this posting be published by ten other companies with only the company name changed?** If yes, the generic language is doing no filtering work and should be replaced with specifics.

## Why This Investment Pays Off Even Under Time Pressure

Writing a specific posting takes longer than reusing a template, which feels like a cost when you're trying to fill a role quickly. But the alternative cost — screening a much larger pool of mismatched applications — is almost always larger than the upfront writing time, especially for a specialized AI/ML/Data role where mismatches are expensive to discover late in a process. Treat the extra twenty minutes of writing as the cheapest screening tool you have, not an optional nicety.

## A Walkthrough: Rewriting a Real Posting Live

Start with a generic draft: "We're looking for a passionate AI Engineer to join our innovative, fast-growing team. You'll work with cutting-edge technology to build the future of our product." Apply the framework: replace the opening with the actual current problem ("You'll spend your first month improving how our system handles ambiguous customer queries that our current rules-based approach mishandles about a third of the time"). Replace generic requirements with a genuine split ("Must have: production experience with NLP models. Nice to have: experience with retrieval-augmented generation specifically"). Add an honest data note ("Our conversation logs are extensive but inconsistently labeled — cleaning and structuring this is part of the role, not a solved problem you're inheriting"). The rewritten posting is longer to write and less flattering to read at a glance, but it will filter for candidates who specifically want this kind of work, rather than attracting a large, poorly-matched pool drawn in by generic enthusiasm.

## A Misconception About Whether Specificity Scares Off Strong Candidates

Some hiring managers worry that naming a specific, unglamorous problem (like messy data) will discourage strong candidates who could go elsewhere for a cleaner slate. In practice, the opposite is more often true: strong, experienced candidates have usually worked with messy data before and find honesty about it reassuring rather than discouraging — it's the vague, over-polished posting that experienced candidates learn to distrust, precisely because they've been burned before by a "cutting-edge" role that turned out to be far less developed than advertised.

## Why This Investment Compounds Across Every Future Hire

A well-written, specific posting template, once developed for a given role type, can be reused and lightly adapted for future hires of the same kind — meaning the upfront investment in getting this right the first time pays dividends across every subsequent hiring round for that role, not just the current one.

## Who Should Actually Write the Technical Sections of a Posting

A recurring source of vague, filtering-free job postings is that they're drafted entirely by HR or a hiring coordinator without direct input from the engineer or team the role would actually join. A practical fix: have the hiring manager or a future teammate draft the specific, technical parts of the posting — the current problem, the data reality, the actual responsibilities — even if HR still handles formatting, benefits information and legal compliance language. This division of labor plays to each party's actual expertise, rather than asking a non-technical writer to invent technical specificity they don't have direct access to.

## How to Know When Your Rewritten Posting Is Actually Working

Rather than assuming a more specific posting is automatically better, track a concrete before-and-after comparison for your own hiring: the ratio of interview-worthy candidates to total applicants, before and after adopting a more specific, filtering-oriented posting style. A genuine improvement in this ratio, even a modest one, justifies the additional time a well-written posting takes, and gives you a data-backed answer the next time someone on your team suggests reverting to a faster, more generic template under time pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: hiring manager writing a posting to attract more applicants) Should I write my AI job posting to attract as many applicants as possible?
Not if your actual bottleneck is screening time rather than application volume. A posting that describes a specific current problem and separates real requirements from nice-to-haves filters for fit rather than maximising raw applicant count.

### (Scenario: recruiter unsure how specific to be about the current state of the data) Should I mention that our data is messy or poorly documented in the job posting?
Yes — naming the real state of your data attracts candidates who specifically enjoy that kind of problem and repels those who wanted a clean, green-field setup, saving both sides time before an interview.

### (Scenario: hiring manager listing every possible skill as required) Should every skill I'd like a candidate to have be listed as a requirement?
No — listing every skill as required filters out qualified candidates who don't check every box, even when most items were never truly non-negotiable. Separate "must have" from "nice to have" explicitly.

### (Scenario: recruiter using generic language to sound appealing) Does language like "fast-paced environment" or "passion for innovation" help attract better candidates?
Generally not — it's used so universally that it carries no information a candidate can act on. Space spent on it is better used describing the specific problem the role would actually work on.

### (Scenario: employer wondering if specificity narrows the pool too much) Won't a more specific job posting reduce the number of applicants I get?
Likely yes, and that's the point if your bottleneck is screening irrelevant applications rather than total volume. A smaller, better-matched pool is usually a better outcome than a larger unfiltered one.
