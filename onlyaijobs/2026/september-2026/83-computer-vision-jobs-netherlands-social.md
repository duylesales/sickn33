👁️ "Computer vision engineer" is one job title covering work with almost nothing in common. A semiconductor metrology engineer, a pathology imaging researcher and an agricultural robotics developer share a title and very little else — which is why candidates apply broadly and are surprised their experience doesn't transfer.

Six clusters in the Netherlands, and what actually separates them isn't architecture:

🏭 Industrial inspection — hard real-time, factory conditions, severe cost asymmetry on safety parts
🔬 Semiconductor metrology — very small data, physically derived labels, inverse problems
🏥 Medical imaging — expert labels that disagree with each other, regulatory validation
🌱 Agricultural vision — hyperspectral imagery, tiny seasonal datasets, outdoor light you don't control
🚗 Automotive perception — enormous data, rare-scenario tail, embedded compute
🛰️ Remote sensing — multi-scale reconciliation, ground truth that's a legal register

📊 Where the arbitrage is: industrial inspection has the most roles and thinnest competition, because it's less publicly glamorous. Medical imaging has the most applicants per role. And agricultural vision has the best ratio of problem difficulty to applicant volume — the problems are genuinely hard, but domain vocabulary hides the roles and candidates don't think of agriculture as technical.

🔧 The investment most software people skip: optics, illumination and sensor characteristics. Improving the image often eliminates the modelling difficulty entirely. Weeks to learn a working understanding, pays off indefinitely.

💡 And the single most portable skill across all six: being able to establish whether a vision system actually works. Evaluation design that reflects deployment conditions, recognising when performance comes from a dataset artefact, knowing which failure modes to look for.
