The rise of large language models gave companies a new, trendier-sounding title to reach for — "AI Engineer" — and many used it for roles that, a few years earlier, would simply have been called "Machine Learning Engineer." That history matters, because it means the two titles overlap far more than they diverge, and treating them as clearly distinct disciplines will cause you to filter out roles you'd actually want.

## Where a Real, If Inconsistent, Distinction Sometimes Shows Up

At companies that do draw a line, "AI Engineer" more often leans toward integrating and orchestrating existing models — API calls to foundation models, prompt engineering, retrieval systems, agent workflows — while "Machine Learning Engineer" more often leans toward training, fine-tuning and deploying custom models from data the company controls. This is a real pattern at some employers, but far from universal, and plenty of postings use the terms interchangeably within the same job description.

## Why the Underlying Skills Overlap More Than the Titles Suggest

Both roles typically require comfort with production systems, data pipelines, and evaluation methodology. Both increasingly touch large language models in some form. The genuinely distinguishing question is less "which title" and more "does this role build models from your own data, integrate pre-built ones, or some mix of both" — a question the job description usually answers more reliably than the title does.

## Where to Start

Don't filter out a posting because of its title alone. Read the actual day-to-day responsibilities, and ask directly in a first conversation whether the role leans toward building custom models or integrating existing ones — the answer matters more than which of the two titles is printed at the top.

## A Concrete Set of Questions to Resolve the Ambiguity

**"Does this role involve training or fine-tuning models from our own data, or primarily integrating existing foundation models?"** This single question resolves most of the practical ambiguity between the two titles.

**"What percentage of the work touches model weights directly, versus prompting, retrieval or orchestration?"** A useful follow-up that quantifies the answer to the first question.

**"Who on the team currently does the modeling work, if any, and who does the integration work?"** This reveals whether the role is meant to do both, or slot into an existing division of labor.

## Mapping the Underlying Work, Independent of Title

| Underlying work | Sometimes titled | Also sometimes titled |
|---|---|---|
| Training/fine-tuning custom models | Machine Learning Engineer | AI Engineer |
| Prompt engineering, RAG, agent orchestration | AI Engineer | Machine Learning Engineer, Applied AI Engineer |
| Evaluation and guardrail systems | AI Engineer, ML Engineer | Sometimes a distinct "AI Safety" or "Evaluation" title |
| Deployment and monitoring of either | MLOps Engineer | Sometimes folded into either title above |

## Why This Overlap Will Likely Persist for Years, Not Resolve Quickly

Unlike some past title confusions that resolved as an industry matured, this one is compounded by how fast the underlying technology itself is changing — what "AI Engineer" work looks like today is already different from what it looked like two years ago, and will likely keep shifting as foundation models absorb more of what used to require custom training. Rather than waiting for the titles to stabilize, treat every posting as requiring its own investigation, indefinitely, as a standing practice rather than a temporary workaround.

## A Walkthrough: Applying the Resolving Question to a Real Ambiguous Posting

A posting titled "AI Engineer" at a Dutch insurtech company describes responsibilities including "working with our data science team to bring models into production" and "exploring how large language models can improve claims processing." Applying the resolving question — does this touch model weights, or integrate existing models — the second bullet suggests integration work (likely using an existing foundation model for claims-related text processing), while the first bullet is ambiguous on its own but suggests deployment support for models the data science team builds, meaning this specific "AI Engineer" role sits closer to an MLOps/integration hybrid than to traditional ML engineering, despite the title. This kind of five-minute analysis, applied consistently, saves considerable wasted interview time on both sides.

## A Misconception About How Permanent This Overlap Will Remain

Some candidates assume this title overlap is a temporary confusion that will resolve within a year or two as the industry matures, the way earlier confusing titles eventually did. Given that the overlap here is driven by rapidly evolving underlying technology — what a foundation model can do out of the box keeps expanding, continuously blurring the line between "using an existing model" and "training a custom one" — there's a reasonable case that this particular ambiguity is more durable than past title confusions, since the technology itself keeps redrawing the boundary these titles are trying to describe.

## Why Building Comfort With Both Layers Is the Most Future-Proof Strategy

Rather than trying to specialize narrowly in only integration work or only custom model training, engineers who build genuine comfort with both layers position themselves well regardless of which layer any specific employer's posting turns out to actually mean — a hedge against continued title ambiguity that's more reliable than hoping the industry settles on stable, predictable definitions anytime soon.

## How This Distinction Affects Your Long-Term Skill Development

Choosing to specialize primarily in integration-layer work versus model-training work has real, divergent implications for what skills you'll build over the following years, and it's worth being intentional about this rather than letting it happen by accident based on whichever role you happened to land next. Integration-focused work builds deep expertise in systems design, API architecture, and increasingly, prompt and retrieval engineering — valuable, transferable skills, but ones that don't build the deeper statistical and modeling intuition that comes from actually training and evaluating custom models. Model-training work builds that deeper modeling intuition but can leave gaps in the systems and integration skills increasingly demanded by roles that need to actually ship AI features into real products. Neither path is a mistake, but drifting into one without a deliberate choice can leave you narrower than you intended a few years down the line.

## Why Employers Are Increasingly Valuing Comfort With Both Layers

As the AI industry matures, an increasing number of roles genuinely require comfort with both the integration layer and the model layer simultaneously — building a product feature that combines a fine-tuned custom component with an integrated foundation-model component is an increasingly common pattern, not an edge case. Candidates who've deliberately built experience across both layers, even if not equally deep in each, are positioning themselves well for this convergence, while candidates who've specialized narrowly in only one layer may find their skill set increasingly represents only half of what a growing share of postings actually require.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: candidate assuming the two titles are clearly distinct) Is there a real difference between "AI Engineer" and "Machine Learning Engineer"?
Sometimes, but not consistently. At some employers, AI Engineer leans toward integrating existing models while ML Engineer leans toward training custom ones — but many postings use the terms interchangeably.

### (Scenario: candidate who filtered out relevant postings by title) Should I only apply to postings with the exact title I'm looking for?
No — the underlying skills and responsibilities overlap far more than the titles suggest, and filtering by title alone risks missing roles that match what you actually want to do.

### (Scenario: candidate unsure what question actually matters) What should I ask instead of relying on the job title?
Whether the role primarily builds custom models from the company's own data, integrates pre-built or foundation models, or some mix of both — that distinction matters more than which title is used.

### (Scenario: candidate wondering why the "AI Engineer" title became common) Why did "AI Engineer" become a popular title recently?
Largely because of the rise of large language models and the new category of work — prompt engineering, retrieval, agent orchestration — that came with them, giving companies a new title to reach for even when the role closely resembles traditional ML engineering.

### (Scenario: candidate preparing for an interview) How should I prepare if I'm not sure which type of work a posting actually involves?
Prepare to discuss both custom model development and integration of existing models, and ask directly early in the process which the role emphasizes day to day.
