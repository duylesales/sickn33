✈️ Dutch aviation is built around one hub airport operating close to its limits for years, under noise and emissions constraints that are legally binding. That defines the technical work: almost everything interesting here is about getting more out of fixed capacity rather than expanding it.

🔗 And constraint makes the problems harder in a specific way. An airport with spare capacity absorbs disruption. One at its limit cascades it, because every resource is already committed. Runway allocation affects taxi time affects gate occupancy affects turnaround affects departure affects the next arrival that needed that gate.

So optimising any component in isolation makes overall outcomes worse. This is one of the clearer cases where system-level modelling genuinely beats local improvement — and where disruption recovery matters far more than nominal planning. A plan for a normal day is easy. Deciding what to do when a runway closes for two hours is where the value is.

🛡️ The safety culture changes how you work, and it's instructive. Evidence standards are high — aviation doesn't adopt changes on a promising correlation. And the events you most want to predict have almost no examples, precisely because the system is designed to prevent them. So work pushes toward precursor analysis: the conditions and minor deviations that precede serious events, rather than modelling the events themselves.

Human factors count too. A system that's technically correct but raises workload at a critical moment, or induces complacency, is a safety problem.

📊 The honest outlook: flight volume growth at the hub is constrained by policy, not demand, so this isn't a growth sector. But the technical function within it IS expanding — because when you can't add capacity, using existing capacity better becomes the only improvement available. That's a more durable position than growth-driven demand.

🎯 Where talent is shortest: crew rostering and network planning. Classic operations research, still hard, undersupplied.
