A role where a candidate would spend a third of their time on model evaluation and feature engineering, filed under a generic "Software Engineer" category because that's the department's default posting template, is invisible to exactly the candidates who filter specifically for AI/ML/Data work. The role isn't hidden by accident — it's hidden by a categorization decision nobody thought carefully about.

## Why This Happens

Job posting templates are often set up by whichever department historically owned the hiring process, not reconsidered role by role. A team that has always posted engineering roles under "Software Engineer" continues to do so even once a role's actual content shifts meaningfully toward machine learning, because changing the category isn't anyone's explicit responsibility.

## What Correct Categorization Actually Buys You

**Visibility to a self-selected, relevant audience.** A candidate browsing a specialist AI/ML/Data category has already filtered themselves by interest — a role correctly placed there reaches exactly the people looking for it, without competing for attention against unrelated engineering postings.

**Fewer mismatched applications.** A role correctly categorized attracts candidates who specifically want AI/ML/Data work, reducing the volume of applications from candidates whose interests don't actually match the role's real content.

**A more accurate signal to your own hiring pipeline metrics.** If you track application-to-hire ratios by category, miscategorized roles distort that data, making it harder to judge which channels and postings actually work for your AI hiring specifically.

## A Quick Check Before Posting

Ask what percentage of the role's actual day-to-day work is modeling, data science or ML-specific, versus general software engineering. If that percentage is meaningful, categorize accordingly — regardless of what a department's default template assumes.

## A Simple Audit for Your Currently Open Roles

For each open role currently categorized under a general engineering title, ask: what percentage of the actual day-to-day work is modeling, data science or ML-specific? If that number is above roughly a third, the role likely belongs in an AI/ML/Data category regardless of its historical template.

## Comparing the Consequences of Miscategorization

| Consequence | Correctly categorized | Miscategorized under generic engineering |
|---|---|---|
| Visibility to AI/ML/Data-specific candidates | High | Low to none |
| Application relevance rate | Higher | Lower, diluted by mismatched applicants |
| Accuracy of your own hiring-channel metrics | Reliable | Distorted |
| Candidate perception of role clarity | The role reads as well-defined | The role may read as an afterthought bolted onto engineering |

## Why This Small Fix Has an Outsized Effect

Categorization is a five-minute decision made once per posting, but its effect compounds across the entire lifetime of that listing — every day it's live, it's either reaching the audience most likely to be a good fit, or it isn't. Few hiring fixes offer this much leverage for this little effort, which is exactly why it's worth checking explicitly rather than assuming a template category is correct by default.

## A Walkthrough: Discovering a Miscategorized Role Through Application Data

A company posts a role under "Software Engineer" that actually involves substantial model evaluation and feature engineering work. Three months in, reviewing applications, the hiring manager notices something odd: strong candidates keep dropping out early in the process, citing that the role isn't quite what they expected, while candidates who make it further often lack the specific modeling experience the role actually needs. Only when someone finally re-reads the posting critically does the miscategorization become obvious — the posting was written and filed using an old engineering template that never got updated as the role's actual content shifted. Recategorizing it and reposting immediately changes the composition of applications, with noticeably more AI/ML-specific candidates applying within the first week.

## A Misconception About Who Should Notice This Problem

It's easy to assume that a hiring manager, being closest to the role, would naturally notice if it's miscategorized. In practice, hiring managers are often the least likely to notice, precisely because they know what the role actually is and unconsciously assume the posting reflects that reality — the miscategorization is more often caught by someone reviewing application patterns or by a candidate who mentions during an interview that they almost skipped the posting entirely.

## Why This Should Be a Standing Item in Any Regular Hiring Review

Rather than relying on someone happening to notice a miscategorization, building a simple categorization check into any regular review of open roles — quarterly, or whenever a role has been open longer than expected without good applicants — catches this systematically rather than by chance. This is a low-cost, high-leverage addition to routine hiring operations, not a one-time fix.

## How to Handle Roles That Genuinely Straddle Two Categories

Some roles genuinely and legitimately span two categories almost equally, which makes a single correct categorization less obvious than the audit framework above might suggest. In these genuinely ambiguous cases, consider whether the specialist platform you're posting to allows secondary tagging or cross-listing, and if not, default to whichever category represents the more specialized, harder-to-find half of the role — the assumption being that candidates for the more common, easier-to-find skill set will still discover the role through other means, while candidates for the rarer, more specialized skill set are more dependent on precise categorization to find it at all.

## Why This Small Discipline Reflects Broader Hiring Process Maturity

A company that categorizes its AI/ML/Data roles carefully and consistently is often, not coincidentally, a company that has thought carefully about other aspects of its hiring process too — job description quality, interview structure, response times. None of these individually is a large signal on its own, but a candidate evaluating multiple opportunities can reasonably treat careful categorization as one small data point among several suggesting an employer that takes its hiring process seriously, rather than treating a job board's category filter as pure administrative housekeeping irrelevant to their decision.
