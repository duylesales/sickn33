A large company convinces candidates with a polished employer brand video and a careers page full of testimonials. A small AI team doesn't have that budget, and often tries to imitate it anyway — with mediocre results. The convincing alternative costs no money, but it does cost time: specific, honest information that a large employer often avoids sharing.

## What Actually Convinces Candidates at a Small Team

**A concrete, current problem instead of a mission statement.** Candidates specifically looking for small-team work want to know what they would do in their first month — not an abstract ambition. That is free to write down, and more convincing than a slick video.

**Honesty about the state of the data and infrastructure.** Admitting that the pipeline is still messy, or that there is no MLOps practice yet, doesn't put people off — it attracts exactly the candidates who want to do that kind of building work, and prevents a mismatch that would otherwise only surface in the first interview.

**Direct access to the people who already work there.** A short conversation with the current (small) team, without HR in between, is often more convincing than a written testimonial — simply because it isn't filtered.

## Why "Small and Informal" Can Be a Selling Point in Itself

Not every candidate wants to work at a large, structured organisation. Some are looking specifically for the ownership and speed of decision-making that only a small team can offer. Be explicit about that instead of pretending to be a larger, more structured organisation than you are.

## Three Concrete Actions That Cost Nothing But Time

**Write one paragraph about the most interesting technical problem your team is working on right now.** No marketing language, just an honest description — this alone sets you apart from nine out of ten job ads a candidate reads that day.

**Offer a short, informal conversation with a current team member, separate from the official application process.** Fifteen minutes, no agenda, just the chance to ask questions to someone who already works there.

**Be specific about what isn't finished yet.** "We don't have an automated retraining pipeline yet" is not a sign of weakness — it is information that lets a candidate judge whether this is exactly the kind of building work they are looking for.

## Comparison Table: Polished vs. Honest

| Aspect | Polished, generic approach | Honest, specific approach |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Often a production budget | Practically zero, mainly time |
| Credibility with experienced candidates | Low — recognisable as marketing | High — feels like an honest conversation |
| Filters for fit | No | Yes, explicitly |
| Repeatable without budget | No | Yes |

## Why Small Teams Are Structurally at an Advantage Here, Not a Disadvantage

A large company can't afford radical honesty about technical debt without reassuring shareholders or the press; a small team can, and that is exactly why honesty works so well as a differentiator here. What would be a risk for a large company is a competitive advantage for a small team that no marketing budget can buy.

## A Concrete Scenario: Two Job Ads From Comparable Small Teams

Team A writes: "We are an innovative, fast-growing AI company looking for top talent to strengthen our dynamic team." Team B writes: "We currently have two people doing data work, and we are hitting the limits of what the two of us can handle. Our pipeline works, but has no automated monitoring — that would be one of your first projects." Team B's text sounds less impressive, but gives a candidate a far more concrete picture of what they would do and with whom — and therefore attracts precisely the candidates who find this kind of building work interesting, instead of candidates drawn by a promise the team can't keep.

## A Common Misconception About What Candidates Actually Look For

Small teams often assume candidates are looking for the impression of a large, successful company, and try to imitate that image. In reality, many candidates who specifically want to work at a small team are looking for the opposite: an honest picture of a small team with real, unsolved problems they can contribute to — a polished, exaggerated image tends to backfire with this particular audience rather than attract it.

## Why Honesty Is a More Durable Recruitment Advantage Than a One-Off Impression

A polished but unrealistic job ad may produce more applications in the short term, but leads to a higher chance of a mismatch once the candidate discovers the reality after being hired — with all the costs discussed elsewhere in this series. Honesty up front may cost a few applications, but produces a better-fitting, more stable hire, which is the cheaper route in the longer term.

## How to Sustain This as Your Team Grows

One of the risks of this approach is that honest, specific communication gradually gets diluted as a company grows and more people get involved in writing job ads — a larger company more often has an HR department inclined to standardise and polish language. Deliberately holding on to the honest, specific tone that worked in the early phase, even as the company grows, requires an explicit choice not to let it dilute into the generic language most larger companies eventually use.

## Why This Also Reflects Your Internal Culture, Not Just Your External Communication

A company that communicates honestly to the outside world about technical debt and unsolved problems must be able to live up to the same honesty internally — a job ad promising that you'll "help build interesting challenges" has to match what a new employee actually finds in the first weeks. When external communication and internal reality diverge, exactly the kind of mismatch arises that is discussed elsewhere in this series as one of the most expensive mistakes in recruitment.

## Channels Small AI Teams Can Use for Free or Almost Free

Employer branding without a budget is about visibility in places where AI professionals already are. An overview of channels that mainly cost time:

| Channel | What you do | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical blog on your own website** | Write one honest article per quarter about a technical problem and your approach | Credibility with engineers and findability in search engines |
| **Open source on GitHub** | Publish a small, useful tool or contribution to an existing project | Demonstrable code quality; candidates can judge your work themselves |
| **Meetups and communities** | Give a short talk at a local meetup, such as a PyData chapter in your region, or host a meetup at your office | Direct contact with practitioners nearby |
| **Graduation and internship projects** | Offer a concrete graduation project at, for example, TU/e, Fontys or another institution in your region | Talent inflow and a bond with education programmes |
| **Regional ecosystem organisations** | Join networks and events of organisations such as Brainport Development or other regional development agencies | Visibility among local candidates who don't want to move to the Randstad |
| **LinkedIn company page and personal profiles** | Let team members occasionally share what they are working on | Human, unfiltered signals |
| **Specialist job board** | Post your vacancy where the audience is already looking for AI work | Relevant candidates without competing against large advertising budgets |

Don't choose all channels at once. Two channels you keep up consistently work better than six that fall silent after a month.

## Employment Conditions That Work as a Brand

For small teams, employment conditions are often the strongest employer branding tool, because they are concrete and comparable.

**Salary transparency.** A salary range in the vacancy immediately sets you apart from companies that write "competitive salary." The **EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970** will also require employers to give applicants information about the starting salary or salary range before the interview; those who already do so are ahead.

**Training and conference budget.** AI knowledge quickly becomes outdated. A fixed budget for courses, books or one conference a year is affordable for a small team, and for candidates a concrete signal that growth is taken seriously.

**Flexibility.** Clear agreements about hybrid working, fixed office days or a 32-hour working week weigh more heavily for many candidates than a stylish office.

**Ownership and influence.** Describe concretely which decisions a new employee can make themselves — architecture, tool choice, priorities. That is something large organisations can rarely offer.

**Proximity.** For small regional employers, commuting distance itself is an argument. Candidates who would rather not travel to the Randstad every day actively look for work nearby. An exact address in your vacancy shows them you are that option — exactly the reason OnlyAIJobs shows vacancies at address level.

## Make Sure Your Vacancy Can Also Be Found

An honest job ad only works if candidates find it.

- **Publish the vacancy on your own website** with a clear title, an exact location and the salary range.
- **Add structured data** in the schema.org **JobPosting** format, so search engines can recognise title, location, employment type and salary.
- **Keep the vacancy up to date.** A vacancy that stays online for months after the position has been filled damages your brand more than having no vacancy.
- **Link to your technical blog or GitHub** from the vacancy, so candidates immediately see evidence of your work.

## Measure Whether It Works

Without a budget, time is your investment, so measure whether that time produces anything:

1. **Ask every applicant how they found you**, and record the channel.
2. **Count suitable candidates per channel**, not just the number of applications.
3. **Ask new employees after three months** what convinced them and what differed from their expectations.
4. **Stop using channels** that haven't produced a single suitable candidate after six months.

## An Example of an Honest Team Paragraph

> **Our team.** There are three of us: a data engineer, a data scientist and a product owner. Our demand forecasting models run in production, but we still do monitoring manually — automating that will be one of your first projects. We work in Python and SQL on Azure, review each other's code and meet twice a week. Feel free to talk to one of us for fifteen minutes before you apply.

This paragraph costs nothing, states concretely what is and isn't finished, and gives a candidate everything they need to decide whether this is the kind of work they are looking for.

## Key Takeaways

- Small teams win with honest, specific information instead of polished marketing.
- Technical blogs, open source, meetups, graduation projects, regional networks and specialist job boards mainly cost time.
- Salary transparency, training budget, flexibility, ownership and proximity are employment conditions that work as a brand.
- Make vacancies findable with an exact address, salary range and structured JobPosting data.
- Measure per channel how many suitable candidates it produces and stop what doesn't work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: small team that thinks it needs a marketing budget) Do I need a marketing budget to be attractive to AI candidates?
No — specific, honest information about the work and team structure is often more convincing than a polished but generic employer branding video, and costs no money.

### (Scenario: team afraid that honesty about messy data will put people off) Should I hide that our data infrastructure isn't mature yet?
No — honesty about this doesn't put people off; it attracts exactly the candidates who want to do that kind of building work, and prevents a mismatch that would otherwise only emerge in a later conversation.

### (Scenario: small team unsure whether it can compete with large employers) Can a small team really compete with a large company's employer branding?
Not on the same playing field, but it doesn't need to — candidates specifically looking for small-team work are convinced by concrete, honest information, not by a bigger marketing effort.

### (Scenario: team unsure whether direct team contact is appropriate) Is it appropriate to let candidates talk directly to the current team, without HR in between?
Often, yes, and it is more convincing than a written testimonial — it is unfiltered information candidates can judge for themselves.

### (Scenario: team that feels small and informal and sees that as a weakness) Should we pretend to be bigger or more structured than we are?
No — be explicit about what you are. Some candidates are specifically looking for the ownership and speed only a small team can offer, and appreciate that honesty.
