📦 The Netherlands is one of the most logistics-intensive economies in Europe — largest port, major cargo airport, dense inland waterways, European distribution base for many international companies. Large market for quantitative work. And the scarce skill isn't the one candidates expect.

📐 It's operations research. Data science training over the past decade concentrated heavily on statistical learning. Operations research — integer programming, combinatorial optimisation, heuristic design, queueing theory — is taught far less than a generation ago. But demand for it never fell. Logistics companies still need someone who can formulate a routing problem correctly and get an implementable answer out of a solver, and they find it much easier to hire someone who can fit a forecasting model.

So the optimisation side is a considerably less crowded specialisation. And the knowledge is stable — an integer programming formulation learned today stays correct indefinitely, which isn't true of much tooling knowledge.

✅ Something else this sector offers that product analytics often doesn't: the objectives are already measured and they ARE the value, not proxies for it. Kilometres driven. Fill rate. On-time percentage. Waste share. Your impact is verifiable rather than argued. That also raises the bar honestly — you can't claim success on a proxy while the operational number stays flat.

⚠️ But here's what actually kills logistics data projects, more than any technical issue: the human planners. Most operations already have planning software and experienced planners who override it frequently. Newcomers see irrationality to eliminate. It's usually encoded knowledge — the customer who accepts early but not late delivery, the site whose access road can't take a full trailer, the route that floods.

Arrive intending to automate them away and you fail, because the knowledge you need is held by people with no incentive to share it with someone trying to eliminate them.
