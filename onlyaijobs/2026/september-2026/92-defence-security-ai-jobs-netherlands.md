Defence and security is a growing part of the Dutch technical job market, and it is the sector where candidates most need information they cannot easily get. The work is largely invisible, the access requirements are poorly understood, and the ethical questions are real rather than rhetorical. This analysis addresses all three directly, because a candidate deciding whether to pursue this field deserves a clear picture rather than either recruitment material or dismissal.

## The Clearance Question, Properly Explained

This is the most common source of confusion, and the confusion costs candidates months.

**Screening is conducted by a national authority, not the employer.** A security screening investigates your background — residence, employment, finances, associations — over a period of years. The employer initiates it; the decision is not theirs.

**Verifiable history matters as much as nationality.** The widespread belief that only Dutch nationals can work in defence is inaccurate as a general statement. What screening requires is a history that can be verified. Someone who has lived in three countries in the past five years presents a harder verification problem than someone who has lived in one, regardless of citizenship. This is a practical constraint rather than a judgement about the person.

**Some roles do have nationality requirements.** Positions touching specific classified programmes, or material restricted under alliance agreements, may require Dutch nationality or nationality of an allied state. These are role-specific and stated.

**Export control is a separate mechanism.** Independent of clearance, export control regimes restrict who may access certain technical information based on nationality. You can hold a clearance and still be excluded from a particular project.

**Timelines are months, not weeks.** Plan accordingly. Employers who hire regularly in this space will often place you on unclassified work while screening proceeds.

**Asking is normal and costs nothing.** Recruiters in this sector answer these questions daily. Raising it in the first conversation saves everyone time and signals that you understand the environment rather than that you have something to hide.

The practical summary for an international candidate: parts of this sector are accessible to you and parts are not, the boundary is role-specific, and one direct question resolves it.

## The Ethical Question Deserves a Straight Answer

Many candidates have a genuine reservation about defence work, and treating that as naivety would be dishonest. It is worth setting out what the actual work involves so the decision can be made on accurate information.

**Most defence technical work is not weapons targeting.** The substantial majority of AI and data work in this sector concerns sensing, situational awareness, navigation, communications, logistics, maintenance, training simulation and cyber defence. A sensor fusion engineer working on maritime surveillance is building a system that tells operators what is around a ship.

**Some of it is directly military capability, and you should know which.** Systems that contribute to weapons employment exist. If that is a line for you, it is a line you can identify and ask about specifically rather than avoiding the sector entirely.

**Dual-use is genuine rather than a euphemism.** Search and rescue, disaster response, border safety, maritime traffic management and infrastructure protection use much of the same technology. The radar that detects a threat also detects a vessel in distress.

**The Netherlands has meaningful oversight.** Defence procurement and deployment are subject to parliamentary scrutiny and legal constraint, including on autonomous weapons, where Dutch policy has been comparatively engaged with international discussion.

**Refusing on principle is a coherent position.** So is participating on the view that a country's defence capability should be developed by people who take ethics seriously rather than by people who do not. This analysis does not resolve that for you. It suggests only that the decision should be made with knowledge of what the work is, and that the sector contains a wide range of work with quite different moral weight.

## What the Technical Work Actually Is

**Sensor systems and fusion.** Radar, electro-optical, infrared and electronic support measures combined into a coherent picture, as described in the Hengelo analysis. The richest technical domain in Dutch defence and closest to a research problem.

**Maritime and airspace surveillance.** Detecting, tracking and classifying objects over large areas with incomplete coverage, high false alarm costs and adversaries who may be actively avoiding detection.

**Cyber defence.** Network monitoring, intrusion detection, malware analysis and threat intelligence. The area with the most transferable skills and the lightest clearance barriers in parts of the market.

**Autonomous and unmanned systems.** Navigation, perception and control for platforms operating with limited supervision, in contested or degraded conditions where satellite positioning may be unavailable.

**Predictive maintenance on complex platforms.** Ships, aircraft and vehicles with long service lives, harsh operating conditions and very high cost of unavailability. A familiar problem shape with severe consequences.

**Logistics and readiness.** Supplying and sustaining deployed forces, which is a complex optimisation problem under uncertainty.

**Training and simulation.** Synthetic environments for training, which increasingly involves generating realistic behaviour and scenarios.

**Law enforcement and border management.** Risk assessment, document verification, forensic analysis. The area where the governance considerations described in the public sector analysis apply most acutely, because these systems affect individuals directly.

**Intelligence analysis support.** Processing large volumes of heterogeneous material to support human analysts. Assistive rather than decisive framing matters here as much as in civil administration.

## What Working Under Classification Is Actually Like

Practical expectations, because the daily experience differs from ordinary technical work in ways candidates rarely anticipate.

**You may not be able to describe your work.** Not to friends, not in a job interview two years later, not in a portfolio. This has a genuine career cost: your most substantial achievements may be undemonstrable. Engineers in this sector manage it by keeping careful track of what is releasable, by contributing to open work where possible, and by accepting that some of their best work will remain private.

**Physical and network separation is normal.** Classified work often happens on separate systems in controlled spaces. That may mean no internet access at your desk, no personal devices, and no working from home for some tasks. The convenience of modern development practice is partly unavailable.

**Collaboration is compartmented.** You may work on a component without knowing the full system it serves, because you do not need to. Engineers who need to understand the whole to be motivated find this frustrating.

**Publishing is constrained but not impossible.** Research institutes in this sector do publish, on the unclassified portion of their work. Negotiating what can be released is a routine part of the job and worth asking about if academic visibility matters to you.

**Tooling can lag.** Approved software on classified systems moves slowly. The latest framework may simply not be available, and building on top of what is approved rather than what is newest is the working condition.

None of this is intolerable, and many people prefer the clarity of a well-defined boundary. But candidates who depend on public visibility for their professional identity should weigh it seriously, because the constraint is real and permanent for the duration of the work.

## The Cyber Defence Path Is the Most Accessible and Most Portable

Worth separating out because it is genuinely different from the rest of the sector in ways that matter for a career.

Cyber security work serving defence and critical infrastructure sits partly inside cleared environments and partly in a commercial layer that serves both defence and civil clients. That commercial layer has the lightest access requirements in the sector, and the skills developed there transfer completely — every sector needs security capability, so there is no lock-in.

The technical content is substantial and distinct from other defence work.

**The adversary is adaptive and intelligent.** Unlike most machine learning settings where the data-generating process is indifferent to your model, here it actively responds. A detection method that works becomes less effective precisely because it works, since adversaries observe and adapt. This makes the field closer to game theory than to conventional prediction.

**Base rates are extreme.** Genuine intrusions are rare against enormous volumes of benign activity. A detector with a one-in-a-thousand false positive rate generates an unmanageable alert volume at realistic traffic levels, which means precision requirements are driven by analyst capacity rather than chosen freely.

**Labels are scarce and biased.** You know about attacks that were detected. Attacks that succeeded silently are, by definition, absent from your training data — which is the most consequential selection bias imaginable for this problem.

**Time matters enormously.** Detecting an intrusion three weeks after it began is much less valuable than detecting it in an hour, so evaluation must weight timeliness rather than only accuracy.

For a candidate who wants the sector's mission and stability without the portability cost of deep classification, this is the clearest route. It is also the area where demand most exceeds supply, across defence, government, finance, healthcare and industry simultaneously.

## Where the Employment Sits

**Applied research institutes.** TNO is the largest single employer of defence-related technical capability in the country, working across sensors, cyber, autonomous systems, human factors and operational analysis, usually in partnership with the armed forces and industry.

**Naval systems industry.** Hengelo principally, for radar and sensor systems, as described in that analysis.

**The armed forces themselves.** Increasingly employing civilian technical specialists alongside military personnel, in data, cyber and analysis roles.

**Ministry and policy organisations.** The Hague. Procurement analysis, capability planning, policy support.

**Cyber security companies.** A commercial layer serving both defence and civil clients, with the lightest access barriers.

**Law enforcement and border agencies.** Police, border authorities and related organisations, with their own analytical functions.

**Shipbuilding and platform integrators.** Naval vessel construction and systems integration, concentrated in the maritime cluster.

**Aerospace and space.** Satellite-related activity including earth observation and communications, with defence and civil applications.

## Why This Sector Is Expanding and What That Means

European defence spending has increased substantially, and the technical component of that spending has increased faster than the total. The reasons are straightforward: modern military capability is increasingly determined by sensing, software, networking and autonomy rather than by platform quantity.

For a candidate this has practical implications.

**Demand is growing and supply is constrained.** The pool of people who can do this work and pass screening is limited, which improves conditions for those who can.

**Skills shortages are being addressed by training rather than only by hiring.** Employers increasingly accept candidates without defence experience and develop them, because the alternative is not filling positions.

**The work is stable.** Multi-year programmes and government funding produce employment security that commercial technology work often lacks.

**Expectations about pace differ.** Programme timelines are long, which suits some engineers and frustrates others, as described in the automotive analysis.

## Key Takeaways

- Screening depends on verifiable history as much as nationality, is conducted by a national authority rather than the employer, takes months, and is worth raising in the first conversation.
- Export control is a separate restriction from clearance, and you can hold clearance and still be excluded from specific projects.
- Most defence AI work concerns sensing, awareness, navigation, logistics, maintenance and cyber defence rather than weapons employment, and the parts that differ are identifiable and can be asked about.
- Sensor fusion is the richest technical domain; cyber defence has the most transferable skills and lightest access barriers.
- Law enforcement and border applications carry the governance considerations of public sector work, because they affect individuals directly.
- The sector is expanding faster than its talent supply, which means employers increasingly train candidates without defence backgrounds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### (Scenario: international candidate) Can I work in Dutch defence without Dutch nationality?
Often yes. Screening requires verifiable history, which is harder if you have lived in several countries recently, but nationality requirements apply only to specific roles touching classified programmes or alliance-restricted material. Ask about the specific position.

### (Scenario: candidate with ethical reservations) What does defence AI work actually involve?
The substantial majority concerns sensing, situational awareness, navigation, communications, logistics, maintenance, simulation and cyber defence. Work contributing directly to weapons employment exists and is identifiable, so you can ask specifically rather than avoiding the sector entirely.

### (Scenario: candidate seeking the most accessible entry) Where are the access barriers lightest?
Cyber security, particularly the commercial layer serving both defence and civil clients. The skills are also the most transferable back out of the sector.

### (Scenario: candidate confused about clearance versus export control) How do these differ?
Clearance is a judgement about you, made by a national authority after background investigation. Export control restricts access to specific technical information based on nationality, independent of clearance. You can hold one and still be excluded by the other.

### (Scenario: candidate without defence experience) Will I be considered?
Increasingly yes. Demand is growing faster than the supply of people who can do the work and pass screening, so employers accept candidates without defence backgrounds and develop them rather than leaving positions unfilled.
