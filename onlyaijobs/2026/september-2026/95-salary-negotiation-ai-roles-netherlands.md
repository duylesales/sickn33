Negotiating compensation in the Netherlands differs from other markets in ways that matter practically. Some employers genuinely cannot move on salary regardless of your value. Others can move substantially. Components that are negotiable elsewhere are fixed here, and components that are invisible elsewhere are significant here. Candidates who negotiate using habits formed in another market frequently either leave value on the table or push on something that cannot move.

## First Establish Which Kind of Employer You Are Dealing With

This determines everything else, and candidates often do not ask.

**Collective agreement employers.** Public sector, healthcare, education, many research institutes, and a number of large companies operate under a collective labour agreement that defines pay scales. Your role maps to a scale, and the scale has steps. Within that structure, the negotiable elements are which scale you are placed in and which step within it — not the amount itself. Arguing for a number outside the scale is asking for something the person opposite you cannot grant.

**Commercially free employers.** Technology companies, scale-ups, consultancies, most financial institutions and most industrial employers set compensation themselves. Here the number is genuinely negotiable and the range can be wide.

**Hybrid cases.** Some large organisations have internal frameworks that function like collective agreements without being one. They are more flexible than a true collective agreement but less than a startup.

The practical first step in any negotiation is to ask directly: is this role within a collective agreement or a defined internal scale, and if so which one? The answer tells you what conversation you are having. Asking is entirely normal and signals that you understand the Dutch market.

## What Is Negotiable in Each Case

**Under a collective agreement**, focus on placement rather than amount. Which scale the role sits in, which step within the scale, whether prior experience justifies a higher starting step, and whether there is scope for an allowance for scarce expertise. Some agreements include such provisions and they are used less often than they could be.

Also negotiable: start date, training budget, conference attendance, working hours pattern, additional leave in some agreements, and equipment. These are not trivial — a guaranteed training budget and conference attendance have real career value.

**Under commercial freedom**, the whole package is in scope. Base salary, signing bonus, variable pay, equity where offered, pension contribution above the minimum, training budget, and the specifics of a hybrid working arrangement.

**In both cases**, the most under-negotiated item is the review timing. Agreeing that your salary will be reviewed at six months against defined expectations, rather than at the standard annual cycle, is often easier for an employer to grant than a higher starting number and can be worth more.

## The Components Candidates From Other Markets Miss

**Holiday allowance.** Dutch employment typically includes a holiday allowance of around eight per cent of annual salary, usually paid in May. This is standard rather than a bonus, but when comparing a Dutch offer against a foreign one, remember it is part of the annual total and is sometimes quoted separately.

**Pension.** Employer pension contributions are substantial in many Dutch organisations and vary considerably between employers. A difference in pension contribution can outweigh a difference in salary, and it is frequently omitted from comparisons. Ask what the employer contributes as a percentage and whether there is a mandatory employee contribution.

**Thirteenth month or year-end payment.** Some employers pay an additional month, others do not. This is a material difference in annual total that is easy to overlook when comparing monthly salaries.

**The 30% ruling.** For qualifying incoming employees, a portion of salary can be treated as tax-free expense reimbursement for a limited period. The rules, percentage and duration have been adjusted repeatedly in recent years, so verify current conditions rather than relying on what someone told you. Its effect on take-home pay can be large, and whether an employer will apply for it is worth confirming in writing.

**Travel and commuting allowance.** Standard in many organisations and meaningful if you commute, particularly given the commute-arbitrage logic described in the Gouda analysis.

**Leave days.** The statutory minimum is well below what many employers offer. A difference of five or ten days annually is a real difference in compensation.

## How to Establish a Realistic Target

Three sources, used together.

**Sector position.** As the sector analyses in this series describe, compensation differs substantially by sector. Semiconductor and quantitative finance sit at the top. Public sector, research institutes and healthcare are bounded by collective agreements. Industrial, logistics and retail sit between. A number that is aggressive in one sector is below market in another.

**Regional adjustment.** Amsterdam and Utrecht pay more and cost considerably more. Brainport pays well with moderate housing costs. Regional markets pay less with substantially lower housing costs. Compare disposable income rather than gross salary — the Apeldoorn analysis makes this point concretely and candidates consistently misjudge it.

**Scarcity of your specific profile.** This is the factor with the largest effect and the one candidates underuse. A competent generalist data scientist is not scarce. Someone who can formulate and solve a large routing problem, or who has taken a medical AI product through regulatory approval, or who understands semiconductor metrology, or who can do disclosure control properly, is scarce. The negotiation position follows from scarcity rather than from years of experience.

## The Negotiation Itself

Dutch professional culture is direct, and this works in your favour.

**State a number when asked, with reasoning.** Evasion reads as game-playing here rather than as strategy. A clear figure with a short justification — sector norms, your specific scarce capability, a competing offer if you have one — is received better than a refusal to engage.

**Do not inflate.** Dutch employers tend to check, and an implausible number damages credibility in a way that is hard to recover. Aim high within the plausible range rather than outside it.

**Ask what the range is.** Many employers will tell you. In collective agreement contexts they essentially must, since the scales are public.

**Silence is not pressure.** Dutch negotiation often includes pauses. They are thinking, not applying tactics.

**Get it in writing.** Verbal agreement about a training budget, a review at six months or an application for the 30% ruling should appear in the contract or an accompanying letter.

**Do not accept immediately.** Taking a day is normal and expected. Accepting on the spot removes the opportunity to ask for anything.

## How to Answer the Salary Expectation Question

This specific moment causes more anxiety than the rest of the process combined, so it deserves a concrete treatment.

**When it comes early, ask for the range first.** A reasonable response is: "Before I give a figure, can you tell me the range budgeted for this role?" Many Dutch employers will answer, and in collective agreement contexts the scales are public anyway. If they answer, you have the information you needed and can position within it.

**If they will not give a range, give one yourself with reasoning.** Not a single number — a range whose bottom is a figure you would genuinely accept. "Based on the sector and my experience with [specific scarce capability], I am looking in the region of X to Y." Naming the capability is what justifies the figure; a range without reasoning invites them to take the bottom.

**Do not name your current salary if you can avoid it.** It anchors the conversation to your past rather than to the role's value, and in the Netherlands you are not obliged to disclose it. A polite deflection — "I would rather focus on what this role is worth, since the responsibilities differ" — is well received.

**If you are underpaid currently, say so plainly.** "My current salary is below market because I joined as a junior and it has not been adjusted" is a legitimate and credible statement that prevents an old figure from constraining a new offer.

**When you receive an offer, thank them and ask for time.** One working day is normal. Then come back with a single consolidated response covering everything you want to discuss rather than a sequence of separate requests, which reads as negotiating in instalments and irritates people.

**Make one clear ask with a reason, not a list of demands.** "I would like to discuss the base, and I would value a guaranteed training budget" is workable. Six items with no prioritisation is not.

## What to Do When There Is Genuinely No Room

It happens, particularly under collective agreements, and it is worth having a plan rather than treating it as failure.

**Get the non-monetary items instead, in writing.** Training budget, conference attendance, a formal mentor, a defined project you want to work on, four days a week if that suits you, additional leave where the agreement permits.

**Ask what would change the scale placement.** Sometimes a role can be re-evaluated if the responsibilities are described differently, or a higher scale becomes available once you take on a specific additional duty. Establishing what that path looks like turns a closed conversation into a future one.

**Agree a review point with criteria.** If the answer is genuinely no now, ask what would make it yes in six months, and write that down. This is the single most useful fallback available.

**Decide honestly whether the rest of the offer is enough.** Sometimes it is. A stable role with genuine learning, reasonable hours and work you find meaningful at a moderate salary is a perfectly good outcome, and the sector analyses in this series describe several markets where the non-salary proposition is the actual attraction. Treating salary as the only dimension is a mistake in both directions.

## Two Mistakes That Cost the Most

**Negotiating only base salary.** Candidates fixate on the monthly figure and ignore pension, thirteenth month, leave days, training budget and review timing. The total difference across those items frequently exceeds what was available on base.

**Comparing gross to gross across regions.** An offer in Amsterdam that is ten per cent higher than one in Apeldoorn may leave you materially worse off after housing. Build the comparison on what remains after your actual fixed costs.

## Negotiating a Raise Inside a Job Is a Different Problem

Most advice covers offers. Raising your salary where you already work follows different rules and is worth treating separately, because it is the situation most people are actually in.

**Timing follows the budget cycle, not your readiness.** Organisations decide salary changes at defined points, usually annually, and the decisions are often effectively made weeks before the conversation you have. Raising the subject two months before the cycle is far more effective than raising it after.

**Your manager may not be the decision-maker.** In many Dutch organisations a manager proposes and someone above approves within a budget envelope. That means your manager is your advocate rather than your counterparty, and the useful question is what they need in order to argue your case successfully.

**Provide the argument they will use.** A short written summary of what you have delivered, with specifics and outcomes, is a genuine gift to a manager who must justify your increase to someone who does not know your work. Most people do not do this, and the ones who do get better outcomes.

**Market evidence helps but must be handled carefully.** Pointing out that comparable roles pay more elsewhere is legitimate. Implying you will leave unless matched converts a collaborative conversation into a negotiation with an ultimatum, which sometimes works and often damages the relationship permanently. Be aware of which conversation you are starting.

**Scope change is a stronger lever than performance.** Performing your existing role well is expected. Taking on responsibility beyond it — owning a system, leading a workstream, becoming the person who handles a specific problem class — changes what your role is, which is a much more compelling basis for re-evaluation than doing the same job better.

**If the answer is no twice, believe it.** Two refusals with vague reasoning usually means the constraint is structural or you are not valued as you think. Both are useful information, and the appropriate response is to test the external market rather than to ask a third time.

## Key Takeaways

- Establish first whether the employer operates under a collective agreement or sets pay freely, because that determines what is negotiable at all.
- Under a collective agreement, negotiate scale placement, step, scarcity allowances, training budget and review timing rather than the amount.
- Holiday allowance, pension contribution, thirteenth month, the 30% ruling and leave days are frequently omitted from comparisons and can outweigh base salary differences.
- Your negotiating position follows from the scarcity of your specific profile rather than from years of experience.
- Dutch culture rewards stating a clear, justified, plausible number; evasion reads as game-playing and inflation damages credibility.
- Review timing at six months against defined expectations is the most under-used ask and is often easier to grant than a higher starting figure.

## Where to Start

Before any salary conversation, establish which employer type you are dealing with and build your comparison on total package and disposable income rather than gross monthly salary. Then identify what about your profile is genuinely scarce, because that is what your position rests on.
